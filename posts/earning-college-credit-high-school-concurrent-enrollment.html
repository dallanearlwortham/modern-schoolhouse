Earning College Credit in High School: What Parents Should Know

# Earning College Credit in High School: What Parents Should Know

Your teenager could walk into their freshman year of college with a semester's worth of credits already banked — tuition-free. That's not a hypothetical. It's what more than 2.5 million American high schoolers are doing right now through concurrent enrollment programs, and the numbers keep climbing.

If you haven't looked closely at concurrent enrollment yet, the 2025–2026 school year is the right time to start.

What Is Concurrent Enrollment, and How Is It Different from AP?

Concurrent enrollment (sometimes called dual enrollment) lets high school students take actual college courses — taught by college faculty or credentialed instructors — while still completing their high school diploma. The credits earned count toward both their diploma and a future college degree.

That's the key distinction from Advanced Placement (AP) or International Baccalaureate (IB): with AP and IB, students take a rigorous course and then hope a college accepts their exam score for credit. With concurrent enrollment, the college credit is already theirs. No exam gamble. No waiting to find out if the receiving university will honor it.

Why So Many Students — and Families — Are Saying Yes

Over the past three years, the number of high school students taking college courses has increased more than 20 percent, making them a growing share of all undergraduates. A 2025 survey by Tyton Partners found that the primary motivation for students to engage in dual enrollment was to get ahead on college credits and reduce tuition costs (51 percent), while one in five students said they were looking for more advanced coursework.

Both reasons make a lot of financial sense. A dual enrollment class typically costs between $0 and $600, while the average cost per credit hour at a four-year college runs $756 — meaning a student saves $1,814 to $2,214 per class. If a student takes four classes covering science, math, English, and reading, the total savings jumps to $7,256–$8,856.

The outcomes data backs up the enthusiasm. According to the National Student Clearinghouse Research Center's Yearly Progress and Completion Report released in December 2025, 71.1% of students with prior dual enrollment completed a college credential within six years, compared to 57.2% of students without dual enrollment. More than 80 percent of dual enrollment students enrolled in college straight after high school, compared to roughly 70 percent of the general high school graduate population.

Is Concurrent Enrollment Right for Every Student?

The honest answer: not automatically. Concurrent enrollment works best when a student is genuinely ready for college-level rigor — not just academically, but in terms of time management and self-direction. High school students have yet to fully adjust to the more demanding study habits that college courses require, and they have to fit college classes into an already packed high school schedule.

That said, the flexibility of how students access concurrent enrollment has expanded dramatically. While two-thirds of surveyed students attended in-person at a local college, one-third took their concurrent enrollment courses entirely online — an option that removes geographic and scheduling barriers that once made these programs inaccessible for rural students, student-athletes, or kids in non-traditional school settings.

The right fit matters. Students who are motivated, self-aware about their workload, and supported by educators who can help them choose the right courses tend to thrive.

What Charter Schools Bring to the Concurrent Enrollment Conversation

Charter schools have become meaningful players in expanding access to concurrent enrollment — not just offering it as a checkbox, but weaving it into structured academic pathways.

During the 2024–2025 academic year, 218 public, charter, and alternative high schools participated in concurrent enrollment in Utah alone, with students from 41 school districts and 41 charter schools earning college credit. Utah high school students were awarded 495,117 college credit hours — saving them a combined $125.2 million in tuition costs in a single school year.

The online charter model, in particular, is well-suited to integrating concurrent enrollment in a way that traditional schools often struggle to match. Scheduling flexibility is the key reason. A student at a traditional brick-and-mortar school has to navigate fixed class periods, transportation to a college campus, and a schedule built around dozens of other students' needs. An online charter student — especially one on a flexible or self-paced track — can often arrange their coursework so concurrent enrollment fits naturally rather than feeling bolted on.

Lumen, Utah's tuition-free K-12 online charter school, lists Concurrent Enrollment directly within its high school academics — alongside live virtual classes and a Flex Program — giving students the structural room to pursue college credit without overloading their schedule or sacrificing the core high school experience.

The Equity Gap That Still Needs Attention

Concurrent enrollment isn't a solved problem. A 2024 report from the Community College Research Center at Teachers College, Columbia University found that dual enrollment students' outcomes vary greatly across and within states, with important gaps in access and success for low-income, Black, and Hispanic students.

Access is the first barrier. In rural communities especially, proximity to a college campus has historically limited who could participate. Colleges like Yuba College in California have begun working directly with small districts and charter schools to offer core general education and career-technical courses online — specifically to address the transportation and socioeconomic challenges that keep rural students out of these programs.

The push to expand online concurrent enrollment is, at its core, an equity argument: the benefits of earning college credit in high school shouldn't be reserved for students who live near a campus, attend a large suburban school, or have parents who already know how to navigate the system.

What Parents Should Ask Before Enrolling

If your high schooler is interested in concurrent enrollment, these are the right questions to start with:

  • Which college is the credit from, and will it transfer? Credits from an accredited institution are far more portable than those from a proprietary program. Ask specifically about the receiving college's transfer policies.
  • What does it cost, and is there financial support? Many states subsidize or fully cover the cost for high school students. Know exactly what your family would owe before signing up.
  • How will it fit into the existing school day? Scheduling conflicts are the most common reason students drop concurrent courses mid-semester.
  • Does the school offer guidance through the process? Concurrent enrollment works best when a counselor or teacher helps the student choose courses strategically — not just grabbing credits for their own sake.
  • Is the student genuinely ready? An honest conversation with a trusted teacher or school counselor is worth more than any enrollment deadline.

The Bottom Line on Concurrent Enrollment

What is concurrent enrollment, and should my high schooler do it? Concurrent enrollment lets high school students earn real, transferable college credit — often at little or no cost — while still in high school. Research consistently shows that students who participate are more likely to enroll in college, more likely to finish a degree, and more likely to do so faster than peers who didn't. It isn't the right fit for every student at every moment, but for motivated, ready high schoolers, it may be one of the highest-return academic decisions a family can make before graduation day.

The conversation is worth starting earlier than most families do — junior year at the latest, sophomore year even better. College credit earned at 16 spends just as well as credit earned at 19. The main difference is the price tag.

How PBIS Builds School Culture at Charter High Schools

# How PBIS Builds School Culture — and Keeps Students in the Building

When a high schooler walks through a door every morning, the culture they meet determines whether they lean in or check out. Curriculum matters. So do teachers, credits, and college pathways. But what holds all of it together — what actually keeps a teenager coming back day after day — is whether the school feels like a place that's on their side. That's where Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports, better known as PBIS, quietly does some of its most important work.

What Is PBIS, and Why Does It Matter at the High School Level?

PBIS is an evidence-based, tiered framework for supporting students' behavioral, academic, social, emotional, and mental health needs. Rather than waiting for students to break rules and then suspending them, schools that implement PBIS define what respectful, responsible behavior looks like — and then teach it, reinforce it consistently, and use data to identify students who need extra support before small problems become big ones.

When implemented with fidelity, PBIS improves social-emotional competence, academic success, and school climate — and it also improves teacher health and well-being. That's a significant return for a framework that costs more in professional development and intentional design than it does in dollars.

For most of its history, PBIS was most commonly associated with elementary schools. The push toward rigorous secondary implementation is newer — and, research suggests, overdue. A growing body of evidence suggests that PBIS decreases overall suspension and misbehavior rates and improves social-emotional functioning at the high school level. Studies tracking urban high schools placed under corrective action plans for disproportionate suspensions have shown meaningful reductions in discipline referrals across multi-year PBIS implementation periods.

The Three Tiers, and Why the Structure Matters

Understanding PBIS means understanding its architecture:

  • Tier 1 (Universal): Schoolwide behavioral expectations are defined, explicitly taught, and consistently reinforced for every student. Positive behavior is acknowledged. Behavioral data is reviewed regularly to spot trends before they become crises.
  • Tier 2 (Targeted): Students who need more than the universal foundation provides receive structured small-group interventions — check-in/check-out systems, mentoring, or social-skills groups — without being removed from the general school community.
  • Tier 3 (Intensive): A smaller subset of students with more complex behavioral needs receive individualized support plans, often coordinated with school counselors, social workers, or community mental health partners.

There is a strong relationship between academic skills and students' social-emotional-behavioral functioning, and a greater opportunity for meaningful outcomes occurs when those supports are integrated with academics through a multi-tiered framework. That integration is exactly what the three-tier structure is designed to make possible at scale — not as a separate "behavior program" bolted onto the school day, but as the underlying operating system for how the school responds to students as human beings.

Does Positive School Culture Actually Reduce Dropout?

The short answer is yes — and the mechanism is belonging.

Researchers in psychology emphasize that dropout often results from a prolonged erosion of bonds between individuals, schools, and society, especially in socioeconomically disadvantaged contexts. School trust — the sense that the institution is fair, caring, and consistent — is what keeps those bonds intact. PBIS is a systematic way to build exactly that kind of environment.

A systematic literature review of factors influencing students' sense of belonging found a small positive association between belonging and academic achievement, and a negative association with absence and dropout rates — confirming that students who do not feel they belong are at measurably higher risk of leaving school early. That finding holds across school types, demographics, and grade levels. Belonging isn't a soft concept; it's a retention lever with a measurable effect size.

The suspension link is direct and damaging. The PBIS framework is currently implemented in more than 25,000 schools across the globe, and a growing evidence base has found that it has been particularly effective at reducing both in- and out-of-school suspensions. Every suspension is a student removed from instruction, distanced from the school community, and nudged one step closer to disengagement. Reducing suspensions through PBIS doesn't just improve a discipline spreadsheet — it keeps students in the building long enough for the relationships that produce graduation to form.

A 2024 brief from the Center on PBIS at the University of Oregon also documents the effectiveness of integrating school mental health within a PBIS framework to improve student and adult well-being — a finding that matters more than ever as post-pandemic anxiety, chronic absenteeism, and student disengagement continue to challenge high schools nationwide.

How Charter High Schools Are Putting PBIS Into Practice

For charter schools in particular — which operate without the attendance boundaries that automatically fill seats — school culture isn't just a niceness. It's a retention strategy. A school where students feel seen and supported generates word-of-mouth, re-enrollment, and community trust. A school where discipline feels arbitrary or punitive loses students quietly, one family at a time.

Among Albuquerque's tuition-free charter options, Sena HS illustrates how seriously high-performing schools treat culture as infrastructure. Gilbert L. Sena Charter High School — serving grades 9–12 — maintains a dedicated, named PBIS program as a core part of its student support structure, sitting alongside its individualized accelerated learning model and a reported 95% graduation rate. The fact that PBIS appears as a resourced, active program rather than a policy footnote signals something important: the school has made an institutional commitment to the conditions that make academic success possible, not just the academic programming itself. A 95% graduation rate doesn't happen through curriculum design alone — it happens when students feel enough of a connection to keep showing up, and when adults have systematic tools to notice when someone starts slipping away.

What Families Should Ask When Evaluating School Culture

School culture is harder to evaluate than test scores or graduation rates — but for many students, it's more predictive of whether their specific child will thrive. Here are practical questions worth raising on any school tour or information night:

  • What are your schoolwide behavioral expectations, and how are they taught? A school with genuine PBIS implementation can answer this with specifics, not slogans.
  • How do you handle a first-time behavioral issue? Listen for proportionality and for responses that keep the student in school.
  • What happens when a student starts withdrawing or falling behind? Look for systematic early-warning processes, not just reactive interventions after the fact.
  • How do staff build relationships with individual students? Ask about advisory periods, mentor programs, or structured check-in systems.
  • What does your suspension rate look like, and has it changed over time? Public charter schools are required to report this data — it's worth asking for it directly.

A school that can answer these questions with evidence has actually thought through how culture gets built — and rebuilt when students arrive already disconnected from school.

The Bigger Picture for School Choice

The school choice movement has long emphasized measurable academic outcomes: test scores, graduation rates, college placement numbers. Those metrics matter. But PBIS is an evidence-based framework with documented impact on reducing problem behaviors, reducing suspension rates, enhancing school climate, and improving academic performance. Culture and academics aren't competing priorities. Culture is the prerequisite.

Charter high schools have something traditional district schools often don't: the autonomy to implement PBIS with real fidelity — to build it into their daily structure rather than bolt it on as a compliance exercise. The schools that use that autonomy wisely, that treat belonging and behavioral support as central to the mission rather than ancillary to it, tend to be the ones worth choosing.

Graduation rates are a lagging indicator. The question worth asking earlier is simpler: does this school have a plan for making sure students want to stay?

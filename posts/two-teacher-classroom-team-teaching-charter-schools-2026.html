Two Teachers, Better Results: The Case for Team Teaching

# Two Teachers, Better Results: The Case for Team Teaching in Charter Schools

The traditional American classroom has looked the same for a century: one teacher, one room, one roster. It's a model that isolates educators, amplifies burnout, and limits how much any single professional can truly differentiate instruction for 25 or 30 students at once. In 2026, a growing body of research — and a rising number of schools willing to bet on a different structure — is making a compelling case that putting two educators in the room may be one of the most underrated investments a school can make.

What Is the Two-Teacher Classroom?

"Two teachers" is an umbrella term covering a spectrum of arrangements, from full co-teaching (where two licensed teachers share equal ownership of a class) to team-based staffing (where a small group of educators shares a larger roster of students and divides roles by strength). In a team-based staffing model, licensed teachers, paraprofessionals, teacher candidates, or other staff work together and share a single, larger roster of students instead of each teacher working in isolation. The idea is that teams can divide up roles according to each educator's strengths, plan together, and support one another — easing the load on any one teacher while creating more personalized, responsive learning environments for students.

The model is not new, but its current resurgence is backed by sharper evidence and a more urgent set of problems — teacher shortages, burnout, persistent achievement gaps — than previous generations of reformers faced.

What Does the 2026 Research Actually Show?

The clearest causal evidence comes from a rigorous study published in August 2026 by researchers at the Center on Reinventing Public Education (CRPE), who examined Arizona State University's Next Education Workforce model as it rolled out across a large, diverse Arizona district over five school years. Because the district phased the model in gradually across different schools, grades, and subjects in different years, the researchers could compare grades that adopted teaming to similar, traditionally staffed grades that hadn't yet — allowing them to estimate a causal effect rather than a simple before-and-after comparison.

The headline findings were striking on the teacher side: educators working on teams were about seven percentage points less likely to leave their school the following year — a sizable improvement in a district with around a 27% annual turnover rate — and teamed teachers earned significantly higher classroom evaluation ratings than their non-teamed peers. These teacher benefits showed up fairly evenly across grade levels, including in elementary grades.

On student achievement, the picture is more nuanced. Across the district as a whole, teaming did not meaningfully change test scores in a way that could be cleanly measured. However, there was one clear, consistent exception: middle school math, where teaming improved student achievement by about 0.08 standard deviations — translating to roughly 2–3 months of additional learning. That result was consistent across multiple statistical models, and there is suggestive evidence that the gains grow over time, with the largest improvements appearing four and five years after initial implementation.

The researchers' honest message to school leaders: don't expect uniform test-score gains right away, and plan to sustain the model long enough to actually learn whether it is working. Treating team teaching as a one-year pilot and declaring failure is a category error.

Does It Help Teachers and Students at the Same Time?

Yes — but the pathways are different, and that matters for how schools frame the investment.

The teacher benefits are the most consistent finding across all grade levels and contexts. For school system leaders wrestling with teacher shortages and burnout, those retention and evaluation-rating findings may point to a viable structural solution — one that doesn't rely solely on salary increases or external incentives that schools, especially charters, often can't match.

Real-world charter school results echo the research. At Detroit Academy of Arts and Sciences, a K-8 charter school in Michigan, early returns on team teaching showed improved math scores for second graders in the pilot's first fall. The school's chief academic officer cited two concurrent objectives: attracting and retaining talented teachers, and making the profession more sustainable and connected by giving teachers more ownership over student learning. Teachers themselves designed and continually tweaked the model — switching morning small-group meetings to daily themes, shifting how phonics rotations ran — because they had genuine stake in whether it worked.

Teachers describe something that quantitative studies can't fully capture: the relief of shared professional responsibility. "I could stand up there and teach, and my co-teacher could go around and support scholars. Even if there was a behavior issue, she could quietly put that fire out without me ever having to stop teaching," one second-grade team teacher said. And critically, with five grade-level teachers sharing a roster of students, the team could more easily cover for each other when there were absences — resulting in fewer disruptions to student learning, a chronic problem in under-resourced schools.

What Makes It Work — and What Gets in the Way?

Where Team Teaching Tends to Thrive

Team-based staffing appears most promising when it increases collaboration between teachers without disrupting established teacher-student relationships. Middle schools are a natural fit: they're often already departmentalized, so adding a team structure likely doesn't change teacher-student relationships in the same way it might in elementary settings. If anything, teaming adds what traditional departmentalized settings often lack — structured collaboration and shared accountability among educators who teach the same students.

In early elementary grades, the picture is more complex. When one teacher has traditionally built a deep, all-day relationship with the same group of young children, introducing a second educator requires careful design to ensure the relational bonds are strengthened rather than fragmented. Prior research on departmentalization at the elementary level suggests that the downsides of specialization can sometimes cancel out the benefits, which is why elementary implementation deserves a more deliberate approach.

Common Implementation Pitfalls

  • Parity gaps. Co-teaching research consistently finds problems when one teacher dominates and the second becomes a floating aide. Both educators need clearly defined, meaningful roles — not one lead and one shadow.
  • Insufficient planning time. Team teaching multiplies the need for coordination. Schools that cut joint planning time to fit the master schedule undercut the entire model's premise.
  • Uniform rollout without teacher co-design. Arizona State University's Next Education Workforce program explicitly operates with a "no one model" philosophy — whatever the staffing model ultimately becomes is co-constructed by the educators in the communities implementing it. Top-down mandates tend to produce compliance, not collaboration.
  • Unrealistic timelines. Because the strongest student outcome gains in Arizona appeared at years four and five, leaders need to build institutional patience into the plan from the beginning.

Charter Schools and the Two-Teacher Opportunity

Charter schools occupy an unusual position in this conversation. They operate with more structural flexibility than most district schools — freedom to redesign schedules, staffing models, and hiring pipelines without many of the contractual constraints that slow staffing innovation elsewhere. That flexibility is a genuine asset that charters rarely fully exploit when it comes to classroom structure.

It also means that decisions about co-teaching at the charter level are genuinely discretionary from the start: schools can build it into their foundational model from day one, rather than retrofitting it onto an existing structure. That matters enormously for implementation quality. Schools that design a two-teacher model into their physical space, schedule, and hiring criteria from the outset face far fewer implementation barriers than those trying to bolt it on later.

BRICK Rochester, a tuition-free public charter school in Rochester, NY currently serving grades K–1, has built two teachers per classroom directly into its foundational model — alongside free afterschool care and family wraparound services — recognizing that the earliest grades are the highest-leverage window for literacy and social-emotional development, and that young children benefit from distributed, responsive adult attention in ways a single educator simply cannot provide alone.

That design choice reflects the same insight the research keeps surfacing: staffing models that reduce educator isolation and increase attentive adult presence for students aren't a soft amenity. They are a structural lever that shapes outcomes from the first day of kindergarten.

A Direct Answer: Is the Two-Teacher Model Worth the Investment?

For most charter schools in 2026, the evidence supports a qualified yes. The research base points to team teaching as a reliable path to improved teacher retention — roughly a seven-percentage-point reduction in turnover in the Arizona study — stronger instructional practice as measured by evaluation ratings, and meaningful student achievement gains in contexts where the model fits the grade-level structure, particularly in middle school math.

The case for elementary implementation is less definitive on test scores, but far from weak. Even where measurable achievement gains haven't been cleanly detected, keeping more teachers in their schools and supporting stronger instructional practice are outcomes worth valuing in their own right — especially given the documented costs of turnover, which include not just recruitment and onboarding expenses but the relationship disruption that matters most to young learners.

The model works best when it is co-designed with teachers rather than imposed on them, sustained long enough to mature through implementation dips, and differentiated thoughtfully by grade level rather than applied as a blanket policy across every classroom in a building.

The Structural Bet Worth Making

"It creates this distributed expertise, but also this shared responsibility," as one superintendent implementing team teaching put it. That phrase names precisely why the two-teacher classroom is gaining traction in 2026: it identifies the core problem — the artificial isolation baked into the one-teacher, one-room model — and offers a structural fix that benefits both sides of the classroom door simultaneously.

Schools that take it seriously aren't simply adding a body to the room. They're redesigning what professional practice, instructional support, and student belonging all look like — at the same time, in the same space. The evidence increasingly says that investment, done well and sustained, pays off in ways that show up both in retention data and in the daily experience of students who now have two invested adults in their corner.

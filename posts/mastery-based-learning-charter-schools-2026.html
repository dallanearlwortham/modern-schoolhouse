Mastery Over Seat Time: Charter Schools Lead on CBE

# Mastery Over Seat Time: Why Charter Schools Are Leading the Competency-Based Learning Revolution in 2026

Imagine two students sitting side by side in the same algebra class. One mastered the material three weeks ago and is quietly bored. The other hasn't yet grasped a foundational concept and is quietly lost. Both receive the same grade at the end of the semester — because both sat in the same seat for the same number of hours. That is how the American K–12 system has measured learning since 1906, when the Carnegie Foundation created the Carnegie unit: one academic credit for every 120 hours of classroom instruction, regardless of what a student actually learned.

In 2026, a growing number of states, schools, and families have had enough of that bargain. And the schools moving fastest to replace it are charter schools.

What Competency-Based Education Actually Means

Competency-based education (CBE) — also called mastery-based learning, proficiency-based learning, or personalized learning, depending on the state — is built on a single principle: students advance when they demonstrate mastery of a concept, not when the calendar says it's time to move on.

Under a well-designed CBE model, students:

  • Progress through content at a pace calibrated to their actual learning, not an arbitrary semester schedule
  • Receive timely, differentiated support based on their individual needs rather than whole-class pacing
  • Demonstrate mastery through varied assessments — projects, demonstrations, performances, or tests — rather than exclusively through standardized exams
  • Engage with explicit, measurable, and transferable learning objectives they can understand and articulate

What competency-based education is not is a lowering of standards. As Lisa McIntyre-Hite, executive vice president of the Competency-Based Education Network, put it plainly at a March 2026 convening of state leaders: "We're not talking about different expectations or different standards for different learners. We're saying everyone's going to get to this level of competency, and we're going to personalize the pathway to get there."

The model flips the traditional formula. Instead of holding time constant and letting learning vary, CBE holds the standard constant and lets the time vary. Every state now allows schools to embrace this model — but allowing it and actually doing it are two very different things.

The Problem with the Status Quo

The Carnegie unit has served as a convenient administrative shorthand for 120 years. It's easy to count. It fills calendars and generates transcripts. But it describes how long a student sat somewhere, not what they learned while they were sitting there.

The consequences of this are visible in every classroom. Within a typical classroom, there are students functioning at three to five different grade levels simultaneously. A system designed around the average student is, by definition, failing most of them — moving too fast for those who need more time to solidify understanding, and too slowly for those who have already moved on.

Brenda Berg, president and CEO of BEST NC, an education advocacy organization that convened state leaders in March 2026 to address exactly this problem, put it bluntly: "We're doing everything based on averages. We have age-based grade levels. We have standard pacing guides. We have uniform semester timelines. We have credits earned by time, not by mastery. And the list goes on and on. If there's one thing we know, there are no students who are average."

Susan Patrick, president of the Aurora Institute — the leading national nonprofit tracking and supporting CBE adoption — makes the equity case directly: "If you are ensuring that every student gets to mastery, you're addressing these gaps that are taking place and the holes in learning that are deeply impacting students, especially our most underserved and our most vulnerable students."

Why Charter Schools Have the Structural Edge

Here is where school structure matters enormously. Transitioning to a genuine competency-based model requires changes that most traditional districts are architecturally slow to make: redesigning grading systems, rethinking credit requirements, rebuilding assessment frameworks, and — critically — abandoning the Carnegie unit as the currency of academic progress.

Charter schools often don't have to dismantle that infrastructure. In many states, they were never required to build it in the first place.

Angela Whitford-Narine, CEO of Second Mile Education Schools — a charter school network in North Carolina that operates on a competency-based model — made this distinction explicit at the March 2026 state leaders' convening. The flexibility charter schools receive under state law, she said, has been "critical to us being able to do what we do." A concrete example: her schools are not required to use Carnegie units. That single structural exemption removes one of the most stubborn obstacles to CBE adoption that district schools face. In her network, teachers' jobs are defined not by delivering content on a schedule, but by "coaching and intervention" — meeting each student where they are.

District schools attempting the same shift must navigate collective bargaining agreements, state credit requirements tied to seat time, reporting systems built around Carnegie units, and public accountability structures calibrated to grade-level cohorts. Charter schools operate with far greater latitude to redesign all of these from scratch.

Where States Stand in 2026 — and Why Utah Stands Out

The Aurora Institute tracks state-level CBE policy on a spectrum from "emerging" (basic policy permission) to "developing" (active pilots) to "advanced" (comprehensive policy plus active capacity-building). Most states remain in the emerging or developing categories. A handful have broken through to advanced status — and Utah is among them.

Utah is classified as "advanced" in CBE policy: it has a clear statewide policy supporting competency-based education and is actively building educator capacity across the system. The state's Personalized, Competency-Based Learning program has awarded grants to help school districts begin the transition, and the state's own Portrait of a Graduate framework describes an ideal graduate not just in terms of academic content mastery, but in terms of self-advocacy, critical thinking, communication, and integrity.

Utah State Superintendent Sydnee Dickson has framed the shift in terms that get at its fundamental logic: "For many, many years we have been driven by what I call the three B's — bells, buses, and butt time, or seat time — to really drive how we think about education. Flipping into thinking about time as a variable and learning as the constant is really key."

For charter schools operating in Utah, that advanced policy environment creates a uniquely supportive backdrop. Freedom Prep, a tuition-free public charter school network in Utah serving students from PreK through 12th grade — with physical campuses in Provo, Vineyard, and St. George, and a statewide online option — operates in precisely this environment: a state that is already among the national leaders in building the policy infrastructure for student-centered, mastery-oriented education.

What Parents Should Ask When Evaluating Schools

For families drawn to the idea of mastery-based learning, a few concrete questions cut through the marketing language:

  • Does the school use Carnegie units or a mastery-based credit system? If a school awards credits purely based on seat time, its commitment to CBE is likely surface-level.
  • How does the school handle a student who hasn't yet mastered a concept before the class moves on? A CBE school has a clear, individualized answer. A seat-time school often doesn't.
  • How does the school define and measure competency? Look for explicit, transparent standards — not vague assurances about "personalization."
  • What role do teachers play? In genuine CBE models, teachers shift from content deliverers on a fixed schedule to coaches who monitor and respond to individual student progress in real time.
  • Does the school use flexible pacing across subjects? A student might move quickly through content they grasp intuitively while spending more time on areas where they need support. This is a sign the model is actually individualized, not just rebranded.

The Bigger Shift Underway

The competency-based movement is not niche anymore. Every state permits it in some form. National organizations are investing in it. State governors are pointing to workforce readiness — which CBE directly serves — as a top policy priority. And the families most likely to seek out CBE are often the same families who are already drawn to charter schools: parents who have weighed their options and concluded that the default assignment isn't the right fit for their child.

Charter schools didn't create the problems with seat-time education. But their structural flexibility — freedom from Carnegie unit mandates, smaller governance footprints, mission-driven cultures, and authorizer accountability tied to outcomes rather than inputs — positions them to solve those problems faster and more completely than the traditional district model typically allows.

The 120-year reign of the Carnegie unit may not be over. But it's facing its most serious challenge yet — and charter schools are leading the charge.

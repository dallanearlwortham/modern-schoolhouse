The Attendance Crisis Schools Can't Ignore in 2025

# The Attendance Crisis Schools Can't Ignore—And How School Choice Is Part of the Answer

Kids can't learn if they're not in school. That sentence sounds obvious, but the numbers behind it are staggering—and three years after classrooms fully reopened, the attendance emergency is far from over.

Chronic absenteeism—defined as missing 10 percent or more of the school year—rose from about 15 percent of U.S. public school students before the pandemic to more than 28 percent by 2022. The good news is that the rate has been edging down. National chronic absenteeism fell to 25.4 percent in 2023 and to 23.5 percent in 2024, according to the American Enterprise Institute's Return to Learn Tracker, which pulls district-level data from 44 states. The troubling news: improvement is slowing significantly, and without additional resolve, researchers warn that about half of the pandemic-related increase in absenteeism could become the permanent new baseline.

For parents, policymakers, and educators trying to engineer an academic recovery, that slowdown should set off alarm bells.

---

Why the Numbers Matter More Than People Realize

Chronic absenteeism isn't just a logistics headache—it's an academic catastrophe hiding in plain sight. Last year, roughly 26 percent of students missed a month of class or more, contributing to dramatic declines in academic performance. Research has linked chronic absenteeism to 27 percent of the drop in math scores and 45 percent of the decline in reading scores between 2019 and 2022.

In roughly half of urban school districts, more than 30 percent of students were chronically absent in 2024–2025—meaning they missed at least 18 days of school. Meanwhile, grade-level data show that students in kindergarten through third grade—children who entered school after the pandemic—have similarly elevated absenteeism to older cohorts that lived through classroom closures. That pattern suggests a broad cultural shift rather than a lingering emergency response.

In other words: for a growing number of families, skipping school has quietly become normalized.

---

What's Actually Working to Bring Students Back

The research on effective interventions points in a consistent direction: relationships and belonging matter more than compliance and punishment. One charter school network cut chronic absenteeism by more than 13 percentage points—from 40 percent down to 26.8 percent—in a single year, then averaged over 95 percent daily attendance in the opening month of the following school year. Their approach centered on positive, proactive outreach rather than reactive consequences.

A 2025 report out of Indianapolis spotlighted six charter and innovation schools, each with a distinct but effective strategy for reducing chronic absenteeism. One of those schools built its entire approach around student agency and belonging—deliberately engineering a culture where students felt genuine ownership over their education and authentic connection to the people around them. The lesson: when students feel like a school was built for them, they show up.

That's not a coincidence. It's the logic of school choice made visible.

---

School Choice as an Absenteeism Intervention

Here's a question worth sitting with: Do students who actively choose their school attend more reliably than students simply assigned to one?

The research is still developing, but the underlying mechanism is well-supported. When a family selects a school based on mission alignment, academic specialty, or values fit, that act of choosing creates investment. Students at mission-driven schools aren't filling a seat mandated by a zip code—they enrolled because the school reflects something they care about. That intrinsic motivation is one of the strongest predictors of consistent attendance.

This is exactly why the surge in school-choice demand is directly relevant to the absenteeism conversation. A June 2025 survey of 3,214 parents by the National School Choice Awareness Foundation found that parent demand for K–12 school choice has reached a new high-water mark, with 74 percent of parents reporting they weighed school choices in the past year. Looking ahead, 56 percent said they are likely to search for a new school for at least one of their children within the next year. Families aren't browsing casually—they are actively hunting for schools that will hold their children's interest and loyalty from September through June.

Charter schools, with their specialized missions and tighter community cultures, are a natural destination for families making that search. Charter school enrollment rose approximately 14.69 percent from 2020 to 2025, a sustained multi-year shift in family priorities that shows no signs of reversing.

---

What Charter Schools Can Do Right Now

For charter school leaders, the absenteeism research offers a practical checklist, not just a warning:

  • Make belonging explicit. Absenteeism drops when students feel genuinely connected to their school community. Morning advisory periods, mentorship programs, and intentional culture-building aren't soft extras—they are attendance strategy.
  • Use data early and often. The most effective schools flag students who miss three or more consecutive days and reach out before chronic absence sets in. Early intervention is exponentially more effective than late-stage outreach.
  • Engage families directly. Targeted text outreach and home visits have shown measurable impact across multiple districts. Charter schools, with fewer bureaucratic layers than large district systems, can move faster on this.
  • Audit your schedule for barriers. Flexible scheduling, credit-recovery pathways, and accessible calendars can quietly remove structural obstacles that push vulnerable students toward chronic absence.

A school like Career Path High, a tuition-free charter high school serving grades 9–12 in Kaysville, Utah, represents the kind of focused, mission-driven environment where these attendance-driving design choices can be built in from the start—rather than bolted onto a massive, legacy district infrastructure after the fact.

---

The Foundation Everything Else Depends On

What is the single best thing a school can do to improve student achievement in 2025?

Get students through the door—consistently, willingly, and regularly. Every tutoring program, literacy initiative, and instructional innovation runs at a fraction of its potential when a quarter of the students aren't in the building to benefit from it. Academic recovery is structurally impossible without attendance recovery first.

School choice isn't a silver bullet. Students who choose their school still face real-world barriers: transportation gaps, family instability, work obligations, and mental health challenges. But choice creates the conditions for motivation—and motivation is the engine that keeps students showing up when showing up is hard.

As school leaders head deeper into the 2025–26 school year with absenteeism rates still stubbornly elevated, the schools that treat attendance as a culture problem—not just a compliance problem—will be the ones that come out ahead.

The data is unambiguous about what's at stake. The question is whether schools will respond with the urgency the moment demands.

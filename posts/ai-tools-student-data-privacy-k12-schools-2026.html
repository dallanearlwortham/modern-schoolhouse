AI in School? Here's What Happens to Your Child's Data

Your Child's School Is Using AI — But Does Anyone Know What It's Doing With Their Data?

Walk into almost any K-12 school in America in 2026 and you'll find artificial intelligence already embedded in the daily routine: writing assistants, adaptive tutoring platforms, automated grading tools, behavior monitoring software, and attendance trackers. The technology is moving faster than the policies governing it — and students' personal data is sitting in the gap.

In 2026 alone, lawmakers have filed more than 134 bills related to AI in education across 31 states, focusing on data privacy, classroom use restrictions, and curriculum integration. That legislative scramble is itself a signal: policymakers, educators, and parents are collectively realizing that the rollout of AI tools in schools far outpaced the guardrails meant to protect children.

This isn't a fringe concern. New York City Public Schools and Los Angeles Unified School District — the nation's two largest districts — both imposed bans on students using artificial intelligence tools for the 2026-27 school year. The pause came amid public concerns over the technology's ability to safeguard student data and well-being, as well as mounting questions about whether AI actually improves learning outcomes.

What Data Are AI Tools Actually Collecting?

The direct answer: When a school deploys AI-powered tools, those platforms may collect far more than grades and attendance. Depending on the platform, schools may collect information such as student names and identification numbers, attendance records, assignment submissions, assessment scores, classroom participation patterns, and learning progress over time. When AI tools are layered on top of that, they may also process student prompts, written responses, research questions, and other interactions used to generate personalized feedback.

The concern isn't just what's collected today — it's where that data goes tomorrow. In California, proposed legislation would prohibit student data from being used to train AI models unless doing so directly benefits the school, reflecting growing concern that student information should not be used to develop commercial products unrelated to educational purposes. That a state the size of California felt compelled to write that into law tells you something about current industry norms.

Why Parents Feel Left Behind

Here's the uncomfortable reality: most parents don't actually know which AI tools their child's school is using, let alone how those tools handle student information. Research in 2026 found that most parents said either their child's school lacks AI policies (35%) or they are simply unsure whether such policies exist (37%). Only about 1 in 5 districts that allow students to use generative AI have a formal policy governing its use.

That uncertainty is feeding real anxiety. More than 70% of parents say they worry about biased evaluations of student work, threats to student data privacy, and unequal access to AI tools that could give some students an unfair advantage. And 64% of parents think AI in schools will do more harm than good.

Students and teachers are uneasy too. In a Center for Democracy and Technology survey, half of students said using AI in class made them feel less connected to their teachers, and 70% of teachers said they were concerned that students' use of AI was preventing them from learning important skills.

The silence from schools makes all of this worse. Families are asking basic questions — "What are the rules? How is student data being protected? What counts as appropriate use?" — and too often getting no clear answers.

What the Law Actually Requires Right Now

Federal law — chiefly FERPA and COPPA — provides a baseline of protection, but the specific rules around AI-generated student data remain unsettled at the federal level. States are rushing to fill the void, with very different approaches:

  • Idaho enacted legislation requiring a statewide framework for AI in K-12 schools, mandating local policies for its use, establishing AI literacy standards and educator training, setting data privacy requirements for AI tools, and prohibiting AI from replacing human teachers.
  • Oregon enacted legislation requiring certain design features intended to protect minors, including measures that reduce excessive or compulsive use when a platform user is known or reasonably presumed to be a child.
  • Oklahoma enacted a law requiring AI tools to be age-appropriate and requiring teachers to review anything AI produces before using it in the classroom, while also allowing parents to opt their children out of AI tools entirely.
  • Florida's State Board of Education voted to require public school districts and charter schools to establish rules for AI use and include those rules in their internet safety policies.

The patchwork nature of these laws means a student's level of protection depends heavily on where their family lives — and which school they choose.

What Schools with Strong AI Policies Actually Do Differently

Schools that handle AI responsibly share a few common traits. The biggest privacy safeguard in K-12 comes from requiring AI vendors to sign strict data privacy agreements before any tool reaches a classroom. But beyond vendor contracts, transparent schools proactively publish lists of approved tools, train teachers on responsible use, and maintain clear channels for parents to ask questions or exercise opt-out rights.

This is where school model matters. Online charter schools, by design, often document their technology infrastructure more explicitly than traditional district schools — families who enroll must understand and consent to the digital learning environment from the start. Oregon Charter Academy (SYS Education), a free, fully online K-12 public charter school in Oregon, illustrates this well: its entire instructional model runs through Canvas, a widely adopted and institutionally governed learning management system, with the school's own curriculum materials built directly into the platform. That kind of centralized, school-controlled setup limits the data exposure that emerges when students independently bounce between dozens of unvetted third-party AI apps across multiple classes with no unified oversight.

8 Questions Every Parent Should Ask Their School Right Now

Regardless of where your child is enrolled, these questions are worth sending in writing to your principal or district technology coordinator this fall:

  1. Which AI tools are officially approved for classroom use?
  2. What student data does each platform collect, and why is it needed?
  3. Is student data used to train commercial AI models?
  4. Who owns the data once it's collected — the school, the district, or the vendor?
  5. How long is student data retained, and can I request deletion?
  6. What cybersecurity protections — encryption, access controls — are in place?
  7. How are teachers trained to use AI tools responsibly?
  8. Can I opt my child out of specific AI tools without academic penalty?

Districts that can answer these questions clearly and promptly typically demonstrate stronger oversight and a genuine commitment to protecting student information. Districts that can't — or won't — are telling you something equally important.

The Bottom Line

AI in K-12 education isn't going away, and it shouldn't. Adaptive tutoring, personalized feedback, and AI literacy are genuinely valuable preparation for the world students are entering. But schools need to weigh the benefits of adopting AI tools against their effect on student privacy, learning depth, and social development — and they need to do it transparently, with families in the loop.

The wave of state legislation in 2026 is a starting point, not a finish line. For now, the most powerful student data privacy tool any parent has is still a direct, specific question asked of the right person at their child's school — and the expectation of a straight answer.

High-Dosage Tutoring in 2026: What Actually Works

# High-Dosage Tutoring in 2026: What the Evidence Actually Says—and Why It Still Matters

Nearly every student in America has now had six full school years to recover from pandemic-era learning disruptions. The honest verdict? Recovery is incomplete. The 2024 National Assessment of Educational Progress found that approximately 40% of 4th graders scored below the NAEP Basic level in reading—the largest such share since 2002—while nearly a third of 8th graders scored below Basic as well. Against that backdrop, one intervention has attracted more bipartisan consensus than almost any other: high-dosage tutoring. But 2026 has also brought a more honest accounting of where it works, where it hasn't, and what families and school leaders should understand heading into the 2026–27 school year.

---

What Is High-Dosage Tutoring, Exactly?

The term gets thrown around loosely, which is part of the problem. Researchers have identified specific structural features that separate genuine high-impact tutoring from ordinary homework help:

  • Frequency: Sessions at least three times per week
  • Duration: Programs running ten or more weeks continuously
  • Tutor consistency: The same trained tutor paired with the same student throughout
  • Setting: Delivered during the school day, not before or after
  • Group size: No more than four students per tutor, with one-to-one preferred
  • Alignment: Instructional materials matched directly to the student's classroom curriculum

When those conditions are met, the results are striking. Research consistently shows that high-impact tutoring is among the most effective evidence-based interventions available, commonly delivering the equivalent of three to fifteen months of additional learning in a single school year. The word commonly does a lot of work in that sentence—it depends entirely on fidelity to the design.

---

The ESSER Boom, the Fidelity Problem, and an Honest Reckoning

Between 2021 and 2024, federal ESSER relief funds sent billions into schools for learning recovery, and high-dosage tutoring became a top destination. Complications surfaced quickly. Third-party companies rushed to fill demand, branding themselves as "high-dosage" and "high-impact" while frequently delivering only remote homework help—nothing close to the vision of a consistent tutor working alongside a student throughout the school year. Many of those vendors struggled to engage students reliably.

Even well-intentioned in-house programs ran into trouble. In Nashville, Tennessee, district officials struggled to recruit and train volunteers, retired teachers, and college students; the district ended up relying largely on its own teachers, who had little free time during the school day, so tutoring sessions were pushed to before or after school—a departure from one of the model's most critical design requirements.

The result was a wave of studies showing underwhelming gains, not because tutoring doesn't work, but because what was delivered often wasn't truly high-dosage. University of Chicago researcher Monica Bhatt has been direct about the explanation: "You spend less, you get less dosage, you get less learning gains." Brown University professor Matthew Kraft, who has both studied and championed the intervention, put the broader lesson plainly: an effort to increase personalized and individualized instruction is "following the right North Star"—but the pitch should have come with more conservative expectations and a much louder warning about implementation challenges.

---

What High-Dosage Tutoring Looks Like When It Works

The discouraging scale-up stories are not a verdict against tutoring. They are a roadmap for doing it right. At schools that ran high-quality sessions at least three times a week with a consistent tutor working with one student or a very small group, students recovered on average as much as four months in literacy and nearly ten months in math over a single school year.

State-level results bear this out. Arkansas named high-impact tutoring as a core strategy under its LEARNS Act. Three years in, more than 25,000 students have completed in-school tutoring, and the state is already seeing gains on K-3 literacy and numeracy screeners and on state assessments. Washington, D.C., which built out its City Tutor program with consistent fidelity, led the nation in academic growth, ranking first in both math and reading recovery.

The common thread: dosage fidelity is non-negotiable. Schools that cut corners on frequency, group size, or scheduling time produced corners-cut results.

---

What Does High-Dosage Tutoring Cost—and How Do Schools Fund It Without ESSER?

This is the practical question that dominated school planning conversations in 2026. Federal relief funding expired in 2024, and many tutoring programs quietly disappeared with it. But the need didn't. States that treated tutoring as a permanent strategy rather than a one-time ESSER expenditure are sustaining programs through Title I Direct Student Services set-asides, state-funded competitive grant programs, and new tutor workforce pathways enabled by National Guideline Standards for Tutor Apprenticeship Programs, issued by the U.S. Department of Labor in 2026. The District of Columbia and 17 of the 18 states that previously ran competitive tutoring grant programs continued those programs through the 2025–26 school year with stated plans to sustain them going forward.

One of the more consequential policy moves: starting in the 2026–27 school year, Louisiana is requiring all tutoring providers receiving state-allocated high-dosage tutoring dollars to implement outcomes-based contracting—paying for demonstrated results rather than for hours logged. Several other states are watching that model closely.

---

Why Charter Schools Are Particularly Well-Positioned

High-dosage tutoring's biggest logistical hurdle is scheduling. Sessions must happen during the school day, which means restructuring the master schedule around a new instructional layer. Large district bureaucracies often struggle to move that quickly—approvals, union agreements, and cross-campus coordination slow the process considerably. Charter schools, by design, operate with far more scheduling autonomy and can redesign their school day within a single planning cycle.

That structural flexibility is a meaningful advantage when fidelity requirements are this specific. A charter school that commits to embedded tutoring can build small-group pull-out blocks into the daily schedule, assign consistent tutors by grade level and subject, and align tutoring materials to the exact classroom curriculum in use that week—without waiting for district-wide approval.

This philosophy of meeting every child exactly where they are is already embedded in the culture of schools purpose-built for underserved communities. BRICK Buffalo, a charter school serving East Buffalo, NY neighborhoods, explicitly describes individualized learning as one of its founding pillars—customizing curriculum to each student's individual interests and ensuring students have the support they need beyond the classroom to reach their full potential. That disposition, baked into a school's identity from the start, is precisely what makes high-dosage tutoring succeed rather than stall.

---

Six Questions Families Should Ask Before the 2026–27 School Year

If your child's school offers any form of tutoring, these questions cut through the marketing language:

  1. When does tutoring happen? (During the school day = strong indicator; only before/after = weaker model)
  2. How often per week? (Three or more times is the research-supported minimum)
  3. How many students per tutor? (Four or fewer; one-to-one is the gold standard)
  4. Is my child's tutor consistent throughout the program? (Rotating staff undermines the relationship that makes it work)
  5. How is tutoring aligned to classroom instruction? (Misaligned content wastes sessions and confuses students)
  6. How is progress measured and reported? (Outcomes-based accountability, not just attendance logs)

A school that answers all six with specifics is taking this seriously. One that pivots to vague language about "enrichment opportunities" probably isn't running a genuine high-dosage program.

---

The Bottom Line

High-dosage tutoring is not a failed experiment—it is a proven intervention that was frequently executed poorly during the ESSER years. The 2026 evidence offers a clearer map: meet the structural design requirements and produce real learning gains; water them down and produce watered-down results. Schools and states that understood that distinction early are now showing measurable academic recovery. The students still waiting for that recovery deserve schools willing to do the work the right way—and the flexibility to actually do it.

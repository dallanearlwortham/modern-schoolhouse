What Parents Really Want in a School (Beyond Test Scores)

# What Parents Really Want in a School (Beyond Test Scores)

Three-quarters of American parents actively weighed their school options last year. That number isn't a blip—it's a fundamental shift in how families think about education. And the schools winning those families over aren't always the ones with the highest test scores.

School Shopping Is Now the Default Parenting Behavior

A June 2025 survey of 3,214 parents conducted by the National School Choice Awareness Foundation found that 74% of U.S. parents of school-aged children—nearly 47 million moms and dads—considered sending at least one child to a new school over the past year, marking a new high-water mark for parent demand in K–12 school choice.

Among those who considered their options, 37% actually enrolled a child in a new school. Another 40% searched seriously but ultimately did not switch—which means even families who stay put are doing genuine homework on alternatives. Looking ahead, 56% of parents say they are likely to search for a new school for at least one child in the coming year.

This isn't panic or dissatisfaction driving the trend. More than half of parents who considered a new school did so for routine life reasons: a household move, a child entering school for the first time, or a grade-level transition. School searching has become a standard part of raising children—as normal as researching a pediatrician or a neighborhood. As one National School Choice Awareness Foundation leader put it, "school choice is the new normal for parents."

So What Are Active School Shoppers Actually Looking For?

Test scores still matter—but they are no longer sufficient on their own. According to EdChoice research, parents who choose private schools most commonly cite a safe environment (50%) and academic quality (47%) as their top priorities. But even for families staying within the public system, a growing body of survey data shows that school culture, climate, and values are shaping decisions alongside—and sometimes ahead of—raw academic metrics.

Niche's 2025 parent survey data points to academic rigor, teacher qualifications, and a sense of belonging as paramount factors in the school selection process. Notably, nearly two out of three parents say reviews from other parents influenced their school decisions, and in 2024, nearly three-quarters of parents said negative feedback from other families would cause them to remove a school from their shortlist entirely.

Parents aren't comparing data sheets—they're trusting stories. What other families say about a school's culture, its people, and what it feels like to be a student there day to day carries enormous weight in the decision.

Why School Culture and Character Programs Have Become Enrollment Differentiators

What do parents look for in a school beyond academics?

The clearest answer from recent research: they want a school where their child will feel safe, known, and challenged to grow as a whole person—not just as a test-taker. School climate, student leadership opportunities, and visible frameworks for character development increasingly tip the scale for families who have already narrowed their list academically.

This is why evidence-based whole-child programs are gaining real traction as enrollment drivers, not just feel-good additions to a curriculum brochure. Eighty-three percent of school principals reported using an SEL curriculum or program in 2024, up from 73% in the 2021–22 school year, according to a nationally representative RAND and CASEL survey—a signal that school leaders across the country are responding to what families want to see.

Programs with documented outcomes carry extra weight. CASEL's program guide awards its highest "SELect" designation—reserved for programs demonstrating evidence of effectiveness at improving student outcomes—to Leader in Me, a schoolwide framework from FranklinCovey Education that serves grades K–12. A quasi-experimental evaluation found school-level reductions in conduct behaviors among participating schools compared to control schools, with significant outcomes spanning improved academic performance, reduced emotional distress, improved identity development and agency, reduced problem behaviors, improved school climate, and improved social behaviors.

That is a wide portfolio of wins—and it maps almost exactly onto the portfolio of qualities today's school-shopping parents describe when they explain what they are looking for.

The Schools Families Are Choosing

Survey data shows that after going through a deliberate school search, enrollment in traditional neighborhood public schools dropped by 6.6 percentage points, while every alternative school type—charter, magnet, private, homeschool, and full-time online—saw post-search enrollment gains.

Public charter schools are the only type of public school growing in enrollment in 2025, with approximately 7,800 charters across the U.S. now serving about 3.7 million children. The charters gaining ground tend to be the ones offering something parents can point to and name: a clear mission, a coherent school culture, a specific approach to developing the whole child.

Navigator Academy Valrico - Compass Schools is a concrete example of this model. The tuition-free K–8 public charter school in Valrico, Florida is built on the idea that developing independent learners and leaders is the mission—not an extracurricular add-on. The school weaves together the Leader in Me program, S.M.A.R.T. academics, and a cross-curricular integration of science, math, art, reading, and technology into a single coherent identity. For a parent doing exactly the kind of deliberate school research the surveys describe, a school with that kind of clear, values-driven answer to "why here?" stands out quickly.

What Stops Parents From Switching—and What Schools Can Do About It

Among parents who searched for new schools but ultimately did not enroll elsewhere, the most common barriers were concerns about disrupting their child's academic or social life (29%), transportation difficulties (27%), a lack of accessible local options (26%), and a fear of making the wrong choice (26%).

Notice what is absent from that list: dissatisfaction with the new school's curriculum. Parents who stay put often do so because of logistics and anxiety—not because the school they found failed to impress them. That means schools with a strong culture and clear mission are already winning the consideration battle. The enrollment gap is being lost to friction, not to academics.

The fix, according to Niche's 2025 research, is both human and operational. Eighty-six percent of parents say personalized emails from a school would positively impact their interest in enrolling their child, compared to 67% for generic newsletters. Families want to feel seen and addressed as individuals—not processed as leads.

The Bigger Picture

The families shopping for schools right now aren't simply looking for a building their child will attend. They are looking for a community to join and a framework that will shape who their child becomes. Test scores earn a spot on the consideration list. School culture, character, and a coherent mission earn enrollment.

Schools that understand the difference aren't just better at marketing—they're building something that genuinely deserves the trust of a generation of parents who have decided, in record numbers, that choosing a school is one of the most important things they can do for their kids.

Dual Enrollment in High School: Start College Early

# Dual Enrollment in High School: How to Earn Real College Credit Before You Graduate

Your high schooler could walk into their freshman year of college with a semester's worth of credits already banked — no AP exam score required, no tuition bill in many states. That's the promise of dual enrollment, and in 2026 it's one of the smartest academic moves a family can make. The question isn't really whether your student should consider it. It's whether their school is structured to actually help them do it.

What Is Dual Enrollment, and Why Does It Matter Right Now?

Dual enrollment — sometimes called concurrent enrollment or dual credit — allows high school students to take college-level courses that count simultaneously toward their high school diploma and a college transcript. Unlike AP classes, where a student studies all year and bets everything on one exam score, dual enrollment produces an actual college transcript entry the moment the course is passed.

About 2 million U.S. high school students are taking college courses for credit while still in high school in 2026 — roughly one in three high school students at some point in their high school career. That's no longer a niche enrichment activity. It's a mainstream pathway — and the financial case for it is hard to ignore. Dual enrollment lets high school students earn college credits often for free or at drastically reduced tuition, shaving semesters or even full years off a four-year degree, with the average student saving $10,000–$30,000 in total college costs.

The Outcome Numbers Families Should Know

Beyond the savings, the research on what dual enrollment actually does for students is consistent and striking. According to data compiled by Complete College America:

  • Dual enrollment participants complete bachelor's degrees an average of 1.4 years faster, saving approximately $14,500 in the process.
  • Students who participate are 2.5 times more likely to enroll in college immediately after high school.
  • Participation is associated with a 12% higher degree completion likelihood overall — and an 18% higher completion rate among first-generation college students.

That last figure matters enormously. Dual enrollment isn't just a tool for students who were already bound for a four-year degree. It's one of the most effective on-ramps to higher education for students who might not otherwise make the leap at all.

What Does Dual Enrollment Actually Cost?

The honest answer: it depends almost entirely on your state.

In several states — including Florida, Indiana, Ohio, Texas, Minnesota, and Colorado — dual enrollment is fully state-funded and free to high school students. Other states offer reduced tuition ranging from around $25 to $75 per credit hour, which still represents a steep discount against the national average of roughly $394 per credit hour at public four-year universities. Tuition may be free or reduced, and families may be able to offset remaining expenses using state grants, scholarships, or qualified 529 plan funds, which now cover dual enrollment fees directly under recent federal changes.

Before assuming cost is a barrier, check your state's specific policy — and ask the school's counseling team, which should have current answers.

Why Flexible Online Charter Schools Have a Structural Advantage Here

Here's a logistical reality that doesn't get enough attention: a student's daily schedule is the single biggest barrier to dual enrollment participation in a traditional school setting.

In a conventional high school, a student who wants to take a 9 a.m. college course has an immediate problem — that's first period. Rearranging a locked bell schedule around an external college calendar is genuinely difficult for most schools to accommodate. Students either miss courses, commute at odd hours, or quietly drop the idea.

Online dual enrollment courses offer flexibility and accessibility, especially for students in rural areas or those whose schedules don't align neatly with a traditional campus setting. Online charter schools — where students already manage their own time across digital platforms as a core part of how school works — can integrate a college course far more naturally. A student who finishes school coursework by noon has an obvious window for an afternoon college class. A student in a rural county doesn't have to commute to a community college campus at all.

This is increasingly recognized at the institutional level as well. Pennsylvania College of Technology recently entered into its first cyber charter dual enrollment partnership — offering 58 technical and general education courses to students at an online charter school, tuition-free. As colleges recognize the growing online charter population, more of these arrangements are forming across the country, expanding access to students who previously had far fewer pathways.

That same convergence of flexibility and college-prep mission is exactly what makes programs like Lisa Academy (Arkansas Online School) worth noting. The school serves K–12 students statewide through live and flex online learning options, and its academic model is explicitly built around an advanced, college-prep curriculum — including dedicated advanced academics programming and a Future Readiness focus. For students already operating in a structured, self-directed online environment, adding a dual enrollment course is an extension of skills they're practicing daily, not a scheduling impossibility.

Dual Enrollment vs. AP: Which Is Right for Your Student?

Many families treat AP classes and dual enrollment as interchangeable. They're not. Here's a clear side-by-side:

| | Dual Enrollment | AP Classes | |---|---|---| | Credit earned by | Passing the course (typically a C or better) | Exam score of 3, 4, or 5 | | Appears on college transcript | Yes — a real college record | No | | Credit transfer acceptance | High, especially in-state | Varies significantly by college | | Cost | Often free or low-cost | $98 exam fee | | Risk profile | Lower (course grade, not one exam) | Higher (one exam determines everything) |

For students who test well under pressure, AP remains a strong option. But for students who want a verifiable college record, prefer a course-grade model over high-stakes testing, or are pursuing fields where community college credit transfers cleanly — health sciences, CTE pathways, general education requirements — dual enrollment often delivers more reliable results and more transferable credit.

What Students Need to Be Ready

Dual enrollment isn't for every ninth grader — but the bar is more accessible than families often assume. Most programs require:

  • A minimum GPA (typically 2.5–3.0, depending on the program)
  • A qualifying score on a college placement assessment or SAT/ACT
  • Counselor or instructor recommendation
  • Parental consent for students under 18

Most dual enrollment students take one to two college courses per semester while maintaining their regular high school schedule — enough exposure to build college-readiness without overwhelming a student who still has diploma requirements to meet.

The skills that matter most aren't necessarily academic brilliance. Time management, independent work habits, and comfort navigating digital platforms matter at least as much as raw GPA. Students who've already developed those habits in an online or flexible school environment tend to find the transition to college-course expectations less jarring than their peers who are encountering self-directed work for the first time.

Questions to Ask Before Your Student Enrolls

  1. Will these credits transfer to the colleges my student is actually considering? Always verify credit acceptance at specific target institutions before registering.
  2. What happens to my student's academic record if they struggle? Dual enrollment grades appear on a real college transcript — which follows a student permanently.
  3. Does our school have a counselor who actively builds dual enrollment into academic planning? A school that lists dual enrollment as an option is very different from one that proactively integrates it into advising.
  4. Are there subject-area restrictions? Some programs limit dual enrollment to specific disciplines — often math, English, or CTE fields.
  5. What does our state actually fund? In many states, families pay nothing out of pocket. Don't assume cost is an obstacle without checking.

Start the Conversation Earlier Than You Think

The families who get the most out of dual enrollment are the ones who start asking about it in 8th or 9th grade — not as a senior-year scramble. A student who begins dual enrollment in 10th grade and takes one or two courses per year can realistically enter college with 15–24 transferable credits, the equivalent of an entire semester or more of tuition already paid.

That's not a small advantage. In an era when the cost of a bachelor's degree continues to climb and completion rates remain stubbornly uneven, starting college with momentum — a real transcript, proven study habits, and a meaningful head start — changes the odds in ways that compound over time.

The opportunity exists. The question is whether your student's school makes it genuinely easy to pursue.

Serving Multilingual Learners in 2026: What Schools Must Do Now

# Serving Multilingual Learners in 2026: What Schools Must Do Now

More than 5 million children in U.S. public schools are English learners — and this year, the federal infrastructure built to support them took a serious hit. The U.S. Department of Education dissolved its Office of English Language Acquisition (OELA) in spring 2026, eliminating a dedicated team that had spent years developing guidance, overseeing Title III funding, and enforcing the civil rights protections that multilingual learners depend on. At the same time, the FY2027 budget proposed eliminating Title III's $890 million in English language acquisition funding outright.

The legal obligations schools carry haven't changed. The students certainly haven't. But the moment is forcing every school leader — and especially charter school leaders — to decide what they actually believe about the children whose first language isn't English.

Why "English Learner" Is Already the Wrong Frame

The terminology matters. More and more educators and researchers are using the term multilingual learner (MLL) instead of "English learner," and the shift isn't just semantic. The EL label centers what a child can't yet do in one language. The MLL frame centers what a child already does — navigate multiple linguistic worlds, code-switch with sophistication, and bring cultural knowledge that most monolingual peers simply don't have.

A 2024 University of Chicago Consortium study tracking Chicago Public Schools students into adulthood found that students who had exited EL status by ninth grade went on to outperform the district average on GPAs and SAT scores, and showed higher two-year college persistence rates. The finding, highlighted in the 2026 Ensemble Learning State of Education for Multilingual Learners report, underscores a critical point: when schools track only current ELs, they miss the full arc of multilingual student success — and they miss the proof that strong early support actually works.

The Dual Language Evidence Is Decisive — And Access Is Still Inequitable

What does research say about dual language immersion programs? The short answer: they work, and they work especially well for the students they were originally designed to serve.

A widely cited RAND study followed 1,625 Portland-area students whose dual language immersion (DLI) program admission was determined by lottery — a research design that eliminates the selection bias that plagues most education comparisons. Students who won a seat outperformed their peers on state reading tests by the equivalent of roughly seven additional months of learning by fifth grade and nine months by eighth grade. English learners assigned to immersion were up to 14 percentage points more likely to reach English proficiency by sixth grade when their home language matched the partner language in the classroom. Math and science outcomes showed no significant differences — meaning DLI students gained in reading without losing ground elsewhere.

Newer 2026 research reinforces the picture. A February 2026 Penn State study found that two-way dual language programs can modestly reduce school segregation by attracting students from outside traditional attendance boundaries — an equity benefit that goes beyond language development alone.

Yet access remains deeply unequal. Despite the evidence, fewer than 10% of California schools — one of the most linguistically diverse states in the country — offer any bilingual or dual language immersion program. And in cities where DLI programs do exist, a troubling pattern has emerged: as dual language becomes fashionable among English-dominant, well-resourced families, the English learner students the programs were built for get squeezed out. Access, as the Ensemble Learning report notes, is part of implementation — not a downstream consideration.

The Federal Vacuum Requires a School-Level Response

With OELA dissolved and Title III funding uncertain beyond December 2026, school leaders cannot wait for Washington to restore a stable policy environment. The practical questions are urgent:

  • Who owns MLL strategy in your building? Without a federal office providing technical assistance and enforcement signals, the responsibility falls to district and school leaders to maintain legal compliance and program quality simultaneously.
  • Where does your bilingual staffing pipeline actually stand? The bilingual teacher shortage is structural, not seasonal. Even experienced bilingual paraprofessionals can face unpaid student-teaching requirements and English-only licensure exams that make credentialing financially out of reach. Schools need a two-year workforce plan — not another emergency hire in August.
  • Is your data telling the full story? Tracking outcomes only for currently-identified ELs systematically undersells multilingual students' trajectories. Former EL outcomes, long-term English learner rates, and dual-language proficiency in both languages all need to be in the picture.
  • Have you written a one-semester contingency plan for Title III delays? Given that summer 2025 saw the federal government withhold nearly $6.8 billion in school funding — including Title III — before releasing it weeks later, schools that had hired staff around that money paid an operational price. The pattern could repeat.

What Asset-Based MLL Instruction Actually Looks Like

The cultural dimension of multilingual education has moved from the margins to the center of serious school design. Research and practitioner experience increasingly confirm that language development is inseparable from identity and belonging. When students sense that a core part of who they are is not valued in the classroom, engagement suffers — and so does language acquisition.

Asset-based multilingual instruction treats a child's home language as a cognitive and academic resource, not a barrier to be overcome. Practically, it looks like:

  • Translanguaging practices that allow students to draw on their full linguistic repertoire during learning, rather than compartmentalizing languages artificially
  • Bilingual resources — dual-language books, bilingual glossaries, heritage texts — present in the classroom environment, not locked in a resource room
  • Cultural literacy integration — multicultural literature, heritage storytelling, and history that reflects students' backgrounds woven into the core curriculum, not siloed into a single unit
  • Community-facing enrollment practices — registration offered in families' home languages, in their neighborhoods, through trusted community partners

This approach also matters for attendance. A Los Angeles Unified study found that English learner students enrolled in dual language immersion attended school at substantially higher rates than comparable peers — about 12 additional days per year on average.

Charter Schools at a Crossroads

Charter schools have both a responsibility and an opportunity here. Research from The Century Foundation found that English learners in charter schools showed stronger academic gains in reading and math than ELs in traditional public schools — with even higher gains for Hispanic EL students. But that aggregate finding masks enormous variation. Some charter schools have designed exemplary multilingual programs; others have underserved multilingual families or made enrollment harder, not easier, for them.

The 2026 moment — federal retreat, bilingual teacher shortage, rising MLL enrollment in many cities — is exactly the kind of inflection point where school mission either proves itself or doesn't.

BRICK Rochester, a tuition-free public charter school in Rochester, NY currently serving grades K–1, offers one illustration of what genuine cultural commitment can look like from the ground up. The school grounds its curriculum in Ubuntu principles of collective care, explicitly celebrates the cultural identities and lived experiences of its students and families, and pairs academic instruction with wraparound family support — a design that takes seriously the whole context a child brings to school, not just the English-language portion of it.

Five Things School Leaders Can Do Right Now

  1. Run your MLL data with both lenses — current ELs and former ELs — before the end of the first semester.
  2. Audit dual language access. Who applies? Who enrolls? Who stays through upper grades? Assign ownership and a timeline to every gap you find.
  3. Map bilingual staffing two years out. Identify candidates already in your buildings and bring a written proposal to your state agency or preparation partner this fall.
  4. Build a funding contingency plan. Know which staff and services depend on Title III, and identify allowable local or state resources that could bridge a gap.
  5. Reframe your school's public narrative. Multilingualism is an asset your community should be proud of — in enrollment materials, at family events, and in how teachers talk about language in the classroom every day.

---

The students in your hallways who are learning English while simultaneously mastering math, science, history, and the unwritten rules of American schooling are doing something genuinely difficult and genuinely impressive. The federal government's retreat from that work in 2026 makes it more important, not less, that schools treat these students' languages and cultures as the strengths they actually are.

Teacher Shortage 2025: What It Means for Your Child

# The Teacher Shortage Is Still a Crisis—and Your Child May Already Be Feeling It

Most education headlines focus on test scores and curriculum debates. But there is a staffing reality quietly shaping your child's daily classroom experience, and it deserves a direct conversation: the United States still does not have enough qualified teachers, and the gap is large enough to touch millions of students every single school year.

An estimated 410,000-plus teaching positions—representing 1 in 8 of all teaching positions nationally—are either unfilled or filled by teachers not fully certified for their assignments, according to the Learning Policy Institute's 2025 update. Given the nation's average student–teacher ratio, that figure suggests more than 6 million students are currently impacted by teacher shortages. That is not a rounding error. It is a structural problem that school-choice families need to factor into their decisions as deliberately as they factor in test scores or extracurricular offerings.

What Teacher Shortages Actually Do to Students

The downstream effects of chronic understaffing are predictable and well-documented. When districts cannot fill classrooms with fully prepared teachers, they often resort to larger class sizes, long-term substitutes, or course cancellations. As teachers leave the profession and aren't replaced, class sizes grow—and that means classroom behavior becomes more difficult to manage and each student receives less individual attention.

For families, "less individual attention" is not an abstraction. It is the difference between a child who gets pulled aside when a concept isn't clicking and one who quietly falls behind until the gap becomes too wide to close in a single school year. It is the difference between a student who gets honest, specific feedback on a paper and one who gets a checkmark and a grade.

When schools face shortages, they also hire underprepared teachers or those not fully certified for their assignments, staff classrooms with substitute teachers for weeks at a time, cancel courses, and stack additional responsibilities onto teachers who are already stretched. The courses most vulnerable to cancellation are often the ones students most need: upper-level math, advanced electives, and specialized programs that set one school apart from another. Schools continue to face specific and persistent shortages in subjects such as upper-level math and special education—and in historically under-resourced and rural schools, the burden falls heaviest on the students who have the fewest alternatives.

Why So Many Teachers Are Still Walking Out

Teachers were more likely in 2025 than similar working adults to report experiencing poor well-being on every measured indicator—a pattern that has held consistently since 2021, according to RAND's State of the American Teacher Survey. The share of teachers who intended to leave their jobs fell to 16 percent in 2025, down from 22 percent in 2024, which is genuine progress. But 16 percent still represents significant, ongoing churn—and it does not account for the many teachers who stay in their positions while quietly withdrawing from the kind of engaged, energetic instruction that students actually need.

The data on why teachers stay or leave points clearly to one underappreciated factor: autonomy. Only 33% of U.S. teachers are extremely satisfied with their jobs, according to a recent Pew Research Center survey, and a significant share of that dissatisfaction stems from feeling micromanaged. The OECD's TALIS 2024 findings confirm the relationship: teachers with higher instructional autonomy are more likely to report greater adaptability, higher job satisfaction, and stronger overall well-being. Increasing teacher autonomy will not solve every problem in K-12 staffing, but it meaningfully improves retention—and better-retained teachers mean more consistent, higher-quality instruction for students.

What School-Choice Families Should Ask About Teacher Quality

Here is a direct answer to a question many parents search: How do I evaluate teacher quality when choosing a K-12 school?

Star ratings and composite rankings rarely surface what matters most. When you tour a school or speak with an administrator, push past the brochure and ask these questions specifically:

  • What is your year-over-year teacher retention rate? High turnover means students constantly build relationships with new adults—the opposite of the stability that supports learning.
  • How long have your longest-tenured teachers been here? A faculty where many educators have stayed a decade or more signals something genuine and positive about school culture.
  • How much curricular autonomy do classroom teachers have? Schools where teachers own their instruction tend to keep sharper, more motivated educators.
  • What does professional development look like, and who drives it? Top-down compliance training differs enormously from collaborative, teacher-led professional learning.
  • What is the student-to-teacher ratio by grade level and subject? A school that answers this question honestly is being honest about its tradeoffs.

No single answer disqualifies a school, but evasive responses to straightforward staffing questions are worth noting carefully.

Why Mission-Driven Schools Have a Structural Retention Advantage

As more public school educators exit the system, charter schools and mission-driven schools have a genuine opportunity to recruit and retain teachers who are specifically aligned with their approach. This is not wishful thinking—it reflects a real dynamic in how motivated educators make career choices. A teacher who actively sought out a classical school, a project-based school, or an arts-integrated school is far less likely to feel the institutional friction that fuels burnout. Self-selection into a coherent mission is itself a retention mechanism. By keeping educators on staff long-term, schools maintain instructional continuity, manage workloads more effectively, and build the kind of stable, purposeful culture that students and families can feel from the first tour.

The Vanguard School, a tuition-free K-12 charter in Colorado Springs serving students across the Pikes Peak region, illustrates this dynamic. Built around a classical, liberal arts curriculum based on the Hillsdale Academy Model and now marking 30 years of continuous operation, it has the kind of deep, established pedagogical identity that draws teachers who want to work within a coherent, content-rich framework—not simply survive inside a large and diffuse district bureaucracy. That clarity of mission, sustained over three decades, is precisely the environment in which dedicated educators tend to put down roots.

The Numbers Heading Into 2026

Despite evidence-based solutions existing for teacher recruitment and retention, shortages remain a persistent challenge across K-12 education. The 2025 national scan showed a small increase of approximately 4,600 positions that were unfilled or filled by teachers not fully certified compared to 2024—meaning the trend is still moving in the wrong direction, if only slightly. There is cautious reason for optimism: a 2025 EdChoice/Morning Consult poll found that 67% of teachers feel a sense of purpose and are hopeful about the future of the profession, and 54% say they would recommend teaching as a career. Purpose matters. But it coexists with structural underfunding, thin pipeline programs, and working conditions that remain genuinely hard in a significant number of schools.

What Families Should Take Away

The teacher shortage is not a background story—it is an active, present-tense reality shaping what happens inside classrooms across the country right now. For families with the ability to choose, teacher retention and school culture deserve to sit at the top of the evaluation list alongside academics and location.

A school that keeps its teachers year after year is a school where your child gets to build real, sustained relationships with the adults guiding their education. Those relationships—stable, consistent, and grounded in a shared sense of purpose—may be the single most undervalued variable in all of K-12 schooling. When you're comparing schools this enrollment season, it is worth asking not only what a school offers students, but how it treats and supports the people doing the teaching.

How Charter Schools Are Winning the Recovery Race

# How Charter Schools Are Winning the Post-Pandemic Recovery Race

Six years after COVID-19 upended classrooms, the biggest story in K–12 education isn't just how much learning was lost — it's who is getting it back fastest. A growing body of 2026 research points to a consistent answer: students in charter schools are recovering at a faster pace than their peers in traditional public schools, and the students benefiting most are those who needed help the most to begin with.

That's not a talking point. It's what the data is showing — and it has real implications for how we think about school design, learning recovery, and educational equity going into the 2026-27 school year.

What the Research Actually Shows

A statewide analysis from Indiana — produced by eight researchers and posted through Brown University's Annenberg Institute in May 2026 — found that charter school students have experienced greater academic growth in the years following the pandemic than their peers in traditional public schools. The gains were particularly pronounced for Black and Hispanic students, and for those who were among the lowest-performing and economically disadvantaged.

The trajectory matters as much as the snapshot. During the 2020-21 school year, charter students performed comparably to traditional public school peers in math and modestly outperformed them in English. After that baseline year, however, charter students outperformed traditional public school students in all years in English, and in nearly all years in math — and the gap grew in 2022-23 and again in 2023-24.

The study's own conclusion is striking: charter school students are experiencing "more accelerated test score gains than their traditional public school peers," and those impacts "appear to be growing over time in the post-pandemic period."

Florida tells a parallel story. According to the Florida Department of Education's latest annual report, released in May 2026, charter school students are outperforming peers in traditional public schools across the majority of academic measures statewide. Charter students outperformed traditional public-school students in 55 of 77 achievement comparisons in 2024-25 — up from 53 of 77 in 2023-24 — with positive outcomes observed specifically among economically disadvantaged students and students with disabilities.

Why Are Charter Schools Gaining Ground?

This is the question researchers most want to answer — and the honest response is: we don't have the full picture yet. Indiana's researchers are now surveying school leaders in both sectors to understand the variation. Candidates include additional instructional time, greater use of small-group pull-out sessions, targeted staffing additions, and tutoring partnerships. What they've already ruled out is telling: chronic absenteeism rates and the mode of instructional delivery — in-person versus virtual — did not account for the achievement differences. The gaps persisted after controlling for those variables, which points toward something in the school design itself.

The broader research base on recovery strategies offers a shortlist of what tends to work:

  • High-dosage tutoring — consistent, small-group or one-on-one sessions tied directly to grade-level content
  • Extended instructional time — more hours in the school day or additional weeks focused on foundational skills
  • Culturally responsive curriculum — instruction connected to students' identities and community contexts, which research consistently links to stronger engagement and retention
  • Integrated wraparound support — addressing the non-academic barriers (housing instability, food insecurity, family stress) that compound learning setbacks and drive absenteeism

Charter schools with flexible governance structures are often better positioned to implement all four without waiting for district-wide policy cycles to catch up.

What This Means for Equity

Do charter schools actually close the gap for the most disadvantaged students? Based on the most current evidence, yes — and more consistently than many observers expected.

In post-pandemic years, the largest achievement gains in the Indiana study were concentrated among Black, Hispanic, economically disadvantaged, and lower-performing students — exactly the populations where the pandemic caused the deepest damage. This challenges the persistent narrative that charter schools primarily serve already-advantaged families shopping for alternatives. Increasingly, rigorous longitudinal research shows charter schools functioning as genuine recovery engines for students who had the furthest ground to make up.

That said, "charter school" is not a monolith. The sector is intentionally heterogeneous, and an underperforming charter school offers no more equity than an underperforming district school. Authorizer accountability and transparent disaggregated outcome data remain essential for families trying to make sound enrollment decisions. The gains described above reflect averages — and averages can obscure wide variation.

One Model Worth Paying Attention To

Among the networks trying to build recovery-ready infrastructure into their permanent school design — not as a temporary pandemic patch — BRICK Rochester reflects an approach worth understanding. BRICK's model is built around what the organization describes as a "pre-cradle to career" framework: excellent K–12 schools, comprehensive holistic support across education, health, housing, and social-emotional wellness, and structured college and career pathways — all woven into a single, unified network serving Black and Brown families. Rather than treating academic recovery as an intervention layered onto an otherwise unchanged school, BRICK's design premise is that lasting academic outcomes are inseparable from the conditions of students' lives outside the classroom. That kind of structural integration is precisely what researchers are trying to isolate when they ask why some charter schools bounced back faster.

Questions Every Family Should Be Asking Right Now

As enrollment decisions get made for 2026-27, these are worth pressing any school on — charter or traditional public:

  1. What does your learning recovery data show for your lowest-performing students specifically?
  2. How are you measuring and reporting outcomes for Black, Hispanic, and economically disadvantaged subgroups?
  3. What does your instructional model look like for students performing significantly below grade level?
  4. How do you address the non-academic barriers — attendance, housing, food, family stability — that affect whether recovery is even possible?

Schools that can answer these questions with specifics, data, and honest candor are worth a closer look. Schools that respond with generalities are telling you something important too.

The Bottom Line

The post-pandemic period has become an unplanned natural experiment in school resilience. Charter schools are, on average, emerging from it with stronger academic momentum — and the students driving those averages are the ones with the most at stake. The more useful policy and practice question now isn't whether charter schools outperformed traditional public schools during the recovery window, but what specifically they did differently — and how those practices can reach every student who is still climbing back.

The research is still catching up to the reality on the ground. But the direction of the evidence is clear enough that families, educators, and policymakers don't have to wait to start asking the right questions.

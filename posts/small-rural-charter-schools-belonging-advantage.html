Why Small Rural Charter Schools Win on Belonging

# Why Small Rural Charter Schools Win on Belonging — and Why That Matters More Than You Think

Every parent who has ever pulled a child from a large, anonymous high school for a smaller alternative has said some version of the same thing: "My kid finally feels like they belong somewhere." It turns out that instinct is backed by a growing body of research — and it's quietly reshaping what "quality" means when families in rural America evaluate their school choices.

The Hidden Driver of Academic Success: Belonging

School belonging isn't a soft feel-good bonus. It's one of the most powerful levers schools have for lifting student outcomes.

A 2024 quasi-experimental study published in Frontiers in Psychology, drawing on more than 8,000 U.S. student observations, found strong evidence that the dominant causal effect runs from sense of school belonging to academic achievement — not just the reverse — with effect sizes substantially larger than those previously reported in correlational research. Belonging has also been linked to higher engagement, higher life satisfaction, stronger self-esteem, and fewer symptoms of depression and anxiety.

For students in rural communities — who may already face geographic isolation, limited extracurricular options, and a smaller peer group — the presence or absence of that belonging can make an outsized difference in whether they stay enrolled, stay motivated, and ultimately graduate.

Why Small Rural Charter Schools Have a Structural Advantage

What gives a small rural charter school its edge on belonging? The answer is largely structural. When a school's entire enrollment fits in a single hallway, anonymity is nearly impossible. Teachers know not just students' names, but their families, their struggles, and their goals.

Research from the Rural Schools Collaborative confirms this: student connectedness and belonging are now recognized as a critical component of student success — and small and rural schools, especially those serving historically disadvantaged students, are particularly well-positioned to build that connection. As Taylor McCabe-Juhnke, Executive Director of the Rural Schools Collaborative, puts it: "Rural and small schools are incredibly innovative and nimble, and they especially shine when it comes to student relationships. Talk to any rural educator, and they'll know every single student, and likely the student's family, on both a personal and academic level. This means small schools have a leg up in building Student Success Systems."

That advantage isn't incidental. In many cases, it's the reason rural charter schools were founded in the first place.

Three Things That Make Rural Charter Schools Work

A Bellwether Education study featured in Education Next examined high-performing rural charter schools that outperformed both their local district and statewide averages in reading and math. Across very different school models and geographic contexts, three common factors emerged:

  1. Deep community ties. The founders, leaders, and board members of successful rural charter schools tend to have long, established roots in the community — in several cases, schools were launched by local parents, lifelong residents, or community members already invested in local youth. Community members consistently cited this trust as essential: these schools would not have opened had outsiders tried to start them.
  1. A clear gap-filling mission. High-performing rural charters tend to exist as an explicit answer to something missing in the community's existing education landscape — a closed district school, families migrating out of an underperforming system, or the absence of a curriculum that reflects students' actual environment and identity.
  1. Consistent, committed leadership. While the average national tenure of a school principal is just four years, the leaders of the most successful rural charter schools studied had been at the helm for a decade or more. That kind of continuity builds the relational depth and institutional trust that can't be manufactured quickly — and that shows up in student outcomes.

These aren't abstract program features. They are the structural conditions that generate belonging. And belonging, as the research makes clear, generates achievement.

A Real-World Example in the Mountains of New Mexico

Moreno Valley High School, a charter high school serving students in grades 9–12 in Angel Fire, New Mexico, illustrates what this model looks like in practice. The school has earned consistent rankings on major national surveys — Newsweek, U.S. News & World Report, and the Washington Post Challenge Index — and has appeared on that Challenge Index list eight consecutive years in a row. New Mexico historically ranks near the bottom of all states for high school graduation rates, yet MVHS has built a culture that a graduating senior once described this way: "Our test scores are good, but that does not define us. Our graduation rate is high, but that does not define us. The strength of our community defines us." That's the belonging advantage made concrete: a small, rurally situated charter school outperforming statewide norms because it treats community as its core curriculum.

What This Means for Parents Choosing a School

When rural families evaluate school choice options, the conversation is often framed around AP offerings, test scores, or college placement rates. Those metrics matter. But the research on belonging suggests there's an even more fundamental question worth asking first: Will my child feel known here?

According to January 2024 national polling by Morning Consult and EdChoice, 73% of rural school parents support education scholarship account programs — a signal that rural families are increasingly open to exploring options beyond their default district assignment. The belonging research gives them a concrete framework for what to look for when they do:

  • A school where every teacher knows every student by name — and knows their family
  • A school where leadership has stayed consistent for years, not months
  • A school with a mission specifically shaped around what this community needs, not a generic model imported from somewhere else

These aren't luxuries or nice-to-haves. They are measurable predictors of student success.

The Bigger Picture

More than 320,000 students are currently enrolled in rural charter schools across the United States, a number that has grown substantially over the past decade. That growth reflects something real: families in small towns and mountain communities are seeking schools that see their children as whole people, not enrollment units.

The belonging research gives that instinct both a name and a mechanism. Small rural charter schools are not a niche alternative for a narrow slice of families. In many communities, they represent the highest-leverage educational investment available — because they do structurally what larger institutions have to work hard and spend heavily to replicate: they make students feel like they genuinely matter.

It turns out that's worth quite a lot more than we used to measure.

Why Students Skip School—And Why Families Leave

# Why Students Are Skipping School—And Why More Families Are Leaving for Good

One in five American students is now chronically absent from school. That number has stubbornly refused to return to pre-pandemic levels, and it is quietly reshaping what families expect from education—and where they choose to send their children.

Chronic absenteeism isn't just a behavior problem. It's a signal. And in 2025, more and more parents are responding to that signal not by doubling down on the school where their child has disengaged, but by switching to something different entirely.

What "Chronically Absent" Actually Means

Chronic absenteeism is defined as missing 10 percent of school days or more in a single academic year—roughly 18 days, or less than a month of class. But the consequences compound fast. Chronic absenteeism accounted for 27 percent of the drop in national math scores and 45 percent of the decline in reading scores between 2019 and 2022. Students who are chronically absent are seven times more likely to drop out before graduating.

Before COVID-19, approximately 13 percent of U.S. students were chronically absent. Since the pandemic, that rate doubled to 26 percent. The most recent federal data shows it fell back to 20 percent during the 2023–2024 school year—movement in the right direction, but still well above pre-pandemic norms. In urban school districts, the situation is more severe: in roughly half of urban districts, more than 30 percent of students were chronically absent during the 2024–2025 school year, according to a RAND Corporation report published in August 2025.

The Root Causes Go Deeper Than "Kids Not Wanting to Go"

Schools and researchers have long treated chronic absenteeism as a logistical problem: unreliable transportation, illness, poverty. Those are real barriers. But the post-pandemic surge has revealed something more complicated at work.

The shift is significantly cultural. During the pandemic, as parents worked from home and lectures were recorded and posted online, attending class began to feel optional to many families. When school doors reopened, many students simply didn't fully come back. Nearly 75 percent of student absences today are "unexplained"—no authorized parent contact, no documented reason. That opacity makes it nearly impossible for schools to offer personalized solutions.

Compounding the problem: one-quarter of K–12 students do not believe being chronically absent is a problem. That perception gap makes school-level interventions extraordinarily difficult to sustain.

Research is also converging on something schools can directly influence—belonging. Studies consistently show that students who feel known, safe, and academically connected attend more reliably. Mental health challenges, bullying, academic disengagement, and a climate where students don't feel they matter all erode that sense of belonging. Those factors show up repeatedly in the data as root drivers of absence—not just excuses for it.

When Absenteeism Becomes a School-Choice Decision

Here is the piece of this story that rarely gets told: for a growing number of families, chronic absenteeism isn't a problem they're trying to solve within their current school. It's the reason they leave.

Reporting from Philadelphia—now one of the most studied cities in the country for this dynamic—found that nearly 15,000 students are enrolled in cyber charter schools, a 55 percent increase since the 2020–2021 school year. Families interviewed said they were choosing virtual schools primarily for scheduling flexibility, smaller class sizes, and safety concerns—including fears about gun violence and bullying—at their children's traditional schools.

The pattern is playing out nationally. Families who once would have kept a struggling student enrolled, hoping attendance would improve or the right teacher would come along, are now exercising school-choice options that didn't exist or weren't well-known a decade ago.

What is the connection between chronic absenteeism and school choice? When a student is persistently absent, it often signals that the school is not meeting a core need—whether that's physical safety, academic fit, scheduling flexibility, or simply a feeling of being known. School choice gives families a practical way to pursue a better match rather than waiting for their current school to change. For many, virtual and online charter schools have become the most accessible alternative, removing the very barriers—commute, campus safety fears, rigid daily schedules—that were fueling the absences in the first place.

What Good Virtual Schools Do Differently

Not every virtual school solves the problem. Research is honest about this: some online programs have struggled with completion rates and weak academic outcomes. The ones that work tend to share identifiable traits—structured daily routines that create real accountability, consistent teacher-student relationships that replicate the belonging students were missing, and curricula organized around something meaningful enough to make a student want to log on each morning.

Dove Virtual Academy, a tuition-free PK–12 online school in Oklahoma, illustrates what that kind of intentionality looks like in practice. Rather than marketing itself purely on flexibility, the school organizes its academic program around three explicit pillars: STEM education, character development, and college and career readiness—even framing its approach as a PreK–16 educational model aimed at producing students who are "college ready, career ready, life ready." That clarity of mission matters. A student who has been chronically disengaged at a traditional school doesn't just need a screen swap; they need a school that has thought carefully about why a student should want to participate every single day.

What Families Should Ask Before Making a Switch

If your child's attendance has become a persistent struggle, here are the questions worth asking before any school-choice decision:

  • Is the absence about logistics or disengagement? Transportation and illness are solvable problems. Deep disengagement—from a school's culture, from peers, from the curriculum—may require a genuinely different environment.
  • Does the alternative school have real attendance expectations? A virtual school with no meaningful engagement requirement often replicates the same absenteeism problem in digital form. Ask specifically about participation and attendance policies before enrolling.
  • What is the school's academic identity? "Flexible learning" is not a mission. Schools organized around a clear purpose—STEM, college readiness, arts, character—give students something to connect to that transcends convenience.
  • Will teachers know your child by name? Research on belonging and attendance points clearly in one direction: students show up for people who notice when they're gone. Ask how teacher-to-student ratios work and how teachers track individual progress.
  • Where does the school expect your child to be in three years? A good school-choice move should point toward something concrete—graduation, a college pathway, a career credential—not simply away from a situation that wasn't working.

The Bigger Picture

Chronic absenteeism is a serious, documented crisis—and it will not be solved by attendance letters and automated robocalls alone. But an underreported part of the story is that the same families pulling students from schools where they've disengaged are, in many cases, finding environments that work far better. School choice, including virtual charter programs, is functioning as an attendance intervention for some students, not merely a consumer preference.

The students who drifted away from classrooms after the pandemic did not stop wanting an education. Many of them are still looking for a school that gives them a reason to show up.

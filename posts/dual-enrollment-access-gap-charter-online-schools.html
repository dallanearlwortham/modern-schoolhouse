Dual Enrollment: Who It's Missing and How to Fix It

# Dual Enrollment Is Booming—So Why Are Millions of Students Still Left Out?

More high schoolers than ever are earning college credits before graduation. The number of dual enrollment participants skyrocketed from roughly 300,000 in the early 2000s to 2.5 million in 2022–23, and dual enrollment grew another 7 percent in the fall of 2024—even as the number of traditional college freshmen fell. That's a remarkable trajectory, and the outcomes data behind it is equally compelling.

But buried inside those headline numbers is an uncomfortable truth: the students who would benefit most from dual enrollment are the ones least likely to be in it.

What the Research Actually Shows About Dual Enrollment Outcomes

The case for dual enrollment is strong. Nationally, about 81% of dual enrollment students go directly to college after high school, compared to roughly 70% of all high school graduates. According to research from the Community College Research Center at Teachers College, Columbia University, 50% of dual enrollment participants completed a college credential within four years, compared to 44% of their peers who didn't participate.

The benefits extend well beyond individual students. Research has found that dual enrollment increases the likelihood of high school graduation, college access and enrollment, credit accumulation, and degree attainment—and these positive outcomes are greatest for students underrepresented in higher education: first-generation college students, students experiencing poverty, and Black, Latine, and Indigenous students.

Read that again: the students who gain the most from dual enrollment are the ones who are most systematically left out of it. That's not a footnote—it's the central problem with how dual enrollment currently works in America.

Who Dual Enrollment Is Leaving Behind

Which students are most likely to be shut out of dual enrollment? Despite broad availability on paper, participation is far from equitable. White students are overrepresented in dual enrollment, while Black and Hispanic students, students with disabilities, and English learners are consistently underrepresented. Dual enrollment options are less prevalent in schools primarily serving low-income students or students of color, and researchers point to insufficient funding as a primary culprit. When states don't adequately fund dual enrollment, high schools and colleges may not offer it at all—or pass the costs of tuition and materials on to students, putting participation out of reach.

Geography compounds the problem. Access gaps are especially persistent for rural students, who may have no nearby college campus and attend high schools without the administrative bandwidth to negotiate partnership agreements. A student in a well-resourced suburban district has routine access to dual enrollment through established pipelines. A student in a rural or under-resourced school may never hear the option mentioned in a meaningful way.

A lack of information is its own barrier. Research consistently shows that many families simply don't know dual enrollment is available, or assume it's reserved for students who are already on a traditional four-year college track.

Why School Model Matters More Than Most People Realize

This is where school choice enters the conversation in a way that's frequently overlooked. The access gaps in dual enrollment aren't only about funding formulas—they're partly structural. Traditional high schools face real constraints: rigid scheduling, limited counselor capacity, narrow college partnerships, and a campus-bound model that doesn't adapt easily to individual student needs.

Charter schools and online schools, operating with more structural flexibility, have been building dual enrollment into their core offerings rather than treating it as an add-on for high achievers.

Oregon Charter Academy (SYS Education), a fully online, tuition-free public charter school serving K–12 students in Oregon, illustrates what this can look like in practice. The school's dual credit program has put college credits within reach of over 1,700 high school students—a meaningful number for an online school whose stated mission is preparing Oregon students for college and career success in a technologically evolving world. That mission isn't aspirational language; it shows up in the program structure.

An online school has a particular advantage here. Flexible scheduling means a student can take a college course without it colliding with family obligations or part-time work. Statewide reach means the school can serve students in rural communities with no nearby college campus. And because an online charter enrolls students from across a state rather than a single zip code, it can aggregate enough volume to sustain college partnerships that would be economically unworkable for a small rural district acting alone.

The Equity Argument for Expanding Dual Enrollment Through School Choice

Is dual enrollment actually an equity tool? It can be—but only if access is deliberately expanded beyond students who are already assumed to be "college material." Research is clear that active outreach, culturally responsive advising, and removing cost barriers are what separate a dual enrollment program that genuinely democratizes college access from one that reinforces existing advantages.

Researchers have emphasized that educators must do more than simply launch a dual enrollment program. Ensuring that Black students, rural students, first-generation students, and students with disabilities actually participate requires intentional recruitment—treating dual enrollment as a standard offering available to every student, not a selective perk for the top of the class.

Charter schools, and especially online and flexible-model charters, have structural incentives to do this well. Their enrollment depends on delivering concrete, visible value to families who have actively chosen them. A robust, accessible dual credit pathway is exactly the kind of outcome families are looking for when they make that choice.

What Families Should Ask Before Enrolling

If you're evaluating school options and college readiness is a priority, here are the right questions to ask about any school's dual enrollment program:

  • Is dual enrollment open to all students, or only those who clear a GPA or test-score threshold?
  • Are there costs to the student or family, or is participation fully covered?
  • How many credits can a student realistically earn before graduation?
  • Do credits transfer to in-state public universities and community colleges?
  • Is there counseling support to help students choose courses aligned with their intended major or career path?
  • How does the school's schedule accommodate college coursework alongside standard high school requirements?

The answers reveal whether a school treats dual enrollment as a prestige signal or a genuine, equitable pathway.

---

Dual enrollment's growth is real and worth celebrating. But "more students participating" and "the right students participating" are two different goals—and right now, the gap between them remains wide. The schools doing the most meaningful work are the ones treating early college access not as a reward for students who were already going to be fine, but as a baseline offering available to every student from the moment they enroll.

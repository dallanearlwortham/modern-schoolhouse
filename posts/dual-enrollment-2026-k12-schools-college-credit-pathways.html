Dual Enrollment in 2026: What K-12 Schools Must Know

# Dual Enrollment in 2026: What K-12 Schools Need to Know

More than 3 million high school students took at least one college course in the 2024–25 school year — a new national record. That figure, drawn from federal data analyzed by the Community College Research Center (CCRC) at Teachers College, Columbia University, represents a 24% increase in just two years, with gains recorded in every single state and the District of Columbia. Community colleges served about 7 in 10 of those students, more than all other types of colleges combined.

For K-12 school leaders, this isn't a trend to watch from the sidelines. It's a strategic decision point. Done well, dual enrollment accelerates college completion, reduces cost, and opens doors for students who might not otherwise walk through them. Done poorly — or not at all — it's a missed opportunity your community will notice.

What Is Dual Enrollment and Why Does It Matter Right Now?

Dual enrollment allows high school students to take college courses and earn credit on a real college transcript, simultaneously fulfilling high school graduation requirements. Unlike AP classes, which only award credit if a student scores high enough on a single end-of-year exam and the receiving college accepts that score, dual enrollment awards credit for completing a course with a passing grade.

What does the research actually show about outcomes?

The evidence base is robust and growing. Research shows strong evidence that dual enrollment — even in relatively small doses — improves academic outcomes, including completing high school, enrolling in college, and completing college degrees. Students who participate are approximately 12% more likely to enter college within seven months of high school graduation than non-participating peers.

The payoff extends beyond the classroom. A 2025 Texas study found that CTE-focused students who also took dual enrollment were more likely to enroll in college and complete a degree — and by age 24, students who combined CTE coursework with dual enrollment earned 16% more than those who did not.

The Gap Nobody Talks About: CTE Students Left Behind

Here's a number that should stop every career and technical education coordinator in their tracks: more than 1 million high school graduates each year complete substantial CTE coursework without taking a single dual-enrollment course. That's a significant missed opportunity — for students, for community colleges, and for K–12 schools that have already invested in building career pathways.

A new October 2026 report from the CCRC, "Scaling Career-Connected Dual Enrollment," draws on interviews with 135 college and K–12 practitioners at 12 sites across the country. Researchers examined how colleges and school districts are weaving dual enrollment into existing career-focused programs rather than creating entirely new schools or structures.

The researchers identified three practical approaches:

  • Embed college courses directly into existing high school CTE pathways — no new program architecture required
  • Add dual enrollment to theme-based or career-field academies that already group students around a shared interest area
  • Allow high school students to begin college CTE certificate or degree programs before they graduate, earning credentials with real labor market value

At Oakland Unified School District in California, dual enrollment is presented as an option for all students, not just top academic performers. Over 10 years, the proportion of district graduates who had taken a dual-enrollment course increased from 25% to 49%.

Who Is Being Left Out — and What Schools Can Do About It

Participation is not evenly distributed. Boys, Black and Hispanic students, English learners, and students with disabilities all take dual enrollment courses at lower rates than their peers. Rigid eligibility rules — particularly test-score cutoffs — are a primary driver of this inequity. Schools that have replaced score-based entry with grades-based criteria, or opened CTE dual enrollment to every student in a given pathway, have found that students still succeed when adequate supports are in place.

The CCRC's research also highlights the importance of thinking in "packages" rather than individual disconnected courses. Strong programs work with college partners so that students finish high school with roughly 12 transferable credits aligned to a specific credential or degree — not a random assortment of courses that may not count toward anything coherent.

This kind of systemic design thinking is exactly what statewide support organizations exist to accelerate. The Public Charter School Alliance of South Carolina — dedicated to ensuring every student in South Carolina has access to a high-quality public school — works with charter schools across the state to build this kind of capacity, connecting member schools to best practices, college partners, and policy guidance so individual schools don't have to build complex college-access pipelines entirely from scratch.

Four Things That Make Dual Enrollment Programs Work

Across strong programs nationally, the CCRC's research points to four consistent design elements that separate programs that move the needle from ones that look good on paper:

  1. Broad, intentional outreach — not waiting for motivated students to self-select, but actively recruiting across all demographics and ability levels, including students who don't yet see themselves as "college material"
  2. Meaningful academic support — holding college standards high while providing tutoring, coaching, and regular check-ins so students aren't set up to fail their first college experience
  3. Pathway alignment — ensuring every course connects to a recognized credential, transfer agreement, or specific degree program, not just floating credit hours that may not transfer anywhere
  4. Dedicated, embedded advising — not just school counselors stretched thin with existing responsibilities, but college advisers spending regular time on high school campuses, helping students understand exactly how their credits will count and what comes next

On the instructor side, finding enough qualified teachers remains a persistent challenge. Schools and college partners are addressing this in creative ways: paying teachers adjunct wages on top of their base salary, funding graduate coursework for interested instructors, and working with industry professionals to help them earn the credentials needed to teach college courses.

What School Leaders Should Do Right Now

If your school doesn't yet have a dual enrollment program — or has one that only reaches your most academically advanced students — here's a practical starting framework for 2026:

  • Audit your current CTE pathways and identify where college courses could be embedded without overhauling what's already working
  • Request a frank conversation with your nearest community college partner about shared goals, advising capacity, and credit transferability
  • Survey students, especially those in underrepresented groups, about what barriers they face and what would make participation feel possible
  • Track outcomes, not just participation — not simply how many students enrolled in a dual-enrollment course, but whether they completed it, whether those credits transferred, and whether they went on to enroll and persist in college

The growth of dual enrollment is one of the most consequential structural shifts in K–12 education happening right now. The schools that treat it as a core pillar of their college-readiness strategy — rather than an enrichment add-on reserved for high achievers — are the ones that will best serve students in an economy where the gap between a diploma and genuine opportunity keeps widening.

Three million students are already in. The question every school leader should be sitting with this fall is: Who at our school is still being left out, and what are we going to do about it?

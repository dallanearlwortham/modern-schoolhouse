The EFTC: A New Funding Lifeline for Charter Schools

# The Education Freedom Tax Credit Is Coming in 2027 — Here's What Charter School Leaders and Families Need to Know

A federal program that didn't exist eighteen months ago is about to reshape how public charter schools access private funding. The Education Freedom Tax Credit — enacted as part of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act on July 4, 2025, and codified at IRC §25F — creates a dollar-for-dollar federal income tax credit of up to $1,700 per individual (up to $3,400 for married couples filing jointly) for donations made to approved nonprofit Scholarship Granting Organizations beginning January 1, 2027. For charter school communities that have long operated on structurally constrained budgets, this is not a minor policy tweak. It's the first federal tax-credit scholarship program in U.S. history, and it opens a genuinely new private funding channel that charter schools are well-positioned to tap.

What the EFTC Actually Does — and How Charter Schools Fit

The mechanics are straightforward: individual taxpayers donate to an approved Scholarship Granting Organization (SGO) in a participating state. The SGO pools those contributions and awards scholarships to eligible K–12 students — including students at public charter schools. Donors receive the credit against their federal tax liability; families receive scholarship funds they can apply to a broad range of qualifying education expenses.

What many charter school leaders don't yet realize is that "qualifying expenses" under the federal law includes far more than private school tuition. The statute explicitly covers:

  • Tutoring — academic support in any subject, including online
  • Enrichment and after-school programs
  • Educational materials, supplies, and curriculum
  • Dual enrollment and Advanced Placement course costs
  • Transportation
  • Support services for students with disabilities

This matters enormously for public charter schools. Because charter schools are tuition-free, families there don't face a tuition bill — but that doesn't mean they're excluded from EFTC scholarships. Far from it. Charter families can receive SGO scholarship funds for tutoring, instructional materials, enrichment programming, AP exam fees, and transportation costs. The EFTC isn't a voucher program steering students away from public schools; it's a private donation incentive that the federal law explicitly extends to public school students, including those at public charters.

Who Qualifies for an EFTC Scholarship?

To receive a scholarship funded by EFTC contributions, a student must be eligible to enroll in a public elementary or secondary school and live in a household with income at or below 300% of their area's median gross income. That income ceiling is wide enough to encompass many working- and middle-class families — not just those at the lowest income levels. Students at public charter schools qualify just as students at private schools do, provided their state has opted into the program and an approved SGO is operating there.

The key gating factor is state opt-in. The program is voluntary for states, and not every governor has agreed to participate — some have actively vetoed opt-in legislation. As of 2026, roughly half of states have opted in. Charter school leaders in non-participating states should monitor their state legislatures closely; this policy landscape is still moving rapidly.

The Scale of the Opportunity — and Why Colorado Is a Case Study

For states that have opted in, the potential funding scale is significant. A March 2026 analysis by the Common Sense Institute modeled Colorado's participation and found that even a 1% participation rate among eligible taxpayers would generate approximately $33 million in scholarship funding in the first year alone. A 10% participation rate would yield roughly $329 million — annually, through private contributions rather than state appropriations, without touching existing per-pupil public school funding.

Colorado is a particularly instructive example of how the EFTC can land in a broadly favorable way. Governor Jared Polis opted the state in on January 29, 2026 — making Colorado one of the first states with a Democratic governor to participate. That signals something important: the EFTC is not cleanly a partisan issue. When structured to benefit public school families alongside private ones, it has found supporters across political lines.

It's in that context that the Colorado League of Charter Schools — the Denver-based advocacy and membership organization that supports Colorado's charter sector — announced it is actively helping member schools participate in the EFTC through a partnership with ACE Scholarships, so schools can join an SGO and access League-facilitated technical assistance, fundraising resources, and expert guidance without having to build costly compliance infrastructure on their own. That kind of sector-wide coordination matters: the operational burden of running an SGO is real, and few individual schools have the bandwidth to manage it in-house.

What Charter School Leaders Should Be Doing Right Now

The window between now and January 1, 2027 is preparation time — and it's shorter than it looks. Here's what forward-thinking charter school leaders in opt-in states should be prioritizing today:

1. Partner with an established SGO rather than building your own. Running an SGO requires legal structure, state compliance filings, donor management systems, eligibility verification, and ongoing oversight. National partners with existing SGO infrastructure — and state-level organizations like charter leagues that have vetted those partners — can absorb that operational weight so school leaders can focus on activating donors rather than managing compliance paperwork.

2. Start educating your donor community now. The EFTC creates a compelling pitch for donors who have never given to education before: a dollar-for-dollar federal tax credit means a qualifying donation can cost a taxpayer in a 22% federal bracket as little as zero in additional net tax liability. This is a fundamentally different donor conversation than a standard charitable ask — and it requires education that takes time to deliver well. Waiting until January to start those conversations will cost real money.

3. Tell your families what's eligible. Charter families who don't pay tuition may assume the EFTC has nothing to offer them. That's a missed opportunity. SGO scholarships could cover costs families are already paying out of pocket — tutoring, AP exam fees, educational materials, enrichment programs. Applying to an SGO is a family action, not a school action, but families won't act without clear guidance from school leaders they trust.

4. Understand the income eligibility rules for your community. Students must live in households at or below 300% of their area's median gross income. For most charter school communities — which tend to serve higher proportions of low- and middle-income families than the district average — this threshold will encompass the majority of enrolled students. Schools serving higher-income populations may find a narrower eligible pool and should model accordingly before making promises to families or donors.

5. Stay current on IRS guidance. Treasury published temporary and proposed regulations in October 2026, and additional guidance is expected before the January 2027 launch. Rules around state tax credit interactions, SGO certification, and income verification safe harbors are still being refined. School leaders who track these developments will be better positioned to give families and donors accurate information when it counts.

The Bigger Picture: A New Private Funding Layer, Not a Replacement

The most common misconception about the EFTC is that it redirects existing public education dollars. It doesn't. It creates an incentive for private donations to flow into an entirely new channel — one that sits alongside, not in competition with, state per-pupil funding. For charter schools already navigating a structural per-pupil funding gap, no automatic facilities funding, and fierce competition for philanthropic attention, a scalable private funding mechanism backed by a federal tax incentive is a genuine addition to the toolkit.

The families most likely to benefit first are those in states with active opt-in programs, strong SGO infrastructure, and charter sector organizations willing to do the coordination and donor education work up front. The schools that move early — building donor relationships, locking in SGO partnerships, and communicating proactively with families about eligible expenses — will enter 2027 with a real advantage over those that treat this as next year's problem.

The EFTC is not a cure for every financial challenge charter schools face. But in a funding environment that has rarely offered charter leaders anything genuinely new, this one is worth treating with urgency.

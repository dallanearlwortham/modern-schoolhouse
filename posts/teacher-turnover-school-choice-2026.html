Teacher Turnover Is a School Choice Issue in 2026

# The School Choice Question Almost Nobody Is Asking

Most families walk into a school tour with a mental checklist: test scores, class sizes, safety record, extracurricular offerings. Almost no one asks about teacher turnover. In 2026, that omission may be the most consequential one a family can make.

The national teacher shortage has shifted in character. It is no longer primarily a recruitment failure — it is a retention failure. According to the Learning Policy Institute's 2026 national scan, at least 425,412 teaching positions are either unfilled or filled by teachers who are not fully certified for their assignment — roughly 1 in 8 U.S. teaching positions nationwide. What makes that figure structurally dangerous is the churn underneath it: teachers are being hired, then leaving 18 months later, cycling students through a revolving door of instructors who never stay long enough to truly know them.

For families actively choosing between schools, this is not background noise. It is a direct signal about learning quality — one that rarely appears on a school's marketing brochure but shows up unmistakably in student outcomes data.

Why Teacher Stability Is an Academic Issue, Not Just a Staffing One

The research connecting teacher turnover to student achievement is consistent and unambiguous. A landmark NBER study — still one of the most rigorous on the subject — found that students in grade levels with higher teacher turnover scored measurably worse on achievement tests compared to years with lower turnover in the same school. Students in grades experiencing 100% turnover performed significantly below those in stable grade-level teams. The effect is particularly pronounced in math.

The mechanism is not mysterious. Instructional continuity allows for cumulative skill development and refined pedagogy. Sustained teacher–student relationships facilitate differentiated instruction and strengthen academic trust — benefits that compound over multiple years and are especially important in STEM disciplines. When a teacher leaves mid-year or fails to return in the fall, they take with them not just their subject expertise but their knowledge of individual students: who needs extra time, who shuts down under pressure, who is quietly struggling at home. A replacement teacher inherits a roster of names, not people.

This is why teacher retention strengthens student achievement, school culture, and community trust in ways that raw test score comparisons between schools simply cannot capture. Families optimizing purely on published rankings may be missing the variable that does the most to determine whether those rankings hold up year to year.

The 2026 Landscape: Structural Pressure, Not a Passing Crisis

Teacher stress declined modestly in 2026, but teachers remain far more likely than similar working adults to report higher stress, worse well-being, and greater financial strain — extending a pattern that has persisted since 2021, according to RAND's 2026 State of the American Teacher Survey. Specifically, 55% of K–12 public school teachers reported frequent job-related stress, down from 62% in 2025, but still far above the 34% reported by comparable working adults outside education.

There is a cautious note of stabilization in exit intentions: the share of educators planning to leave their current teaching jobs dropped from 23% to 18% between the 2022-23 and 2025-26 school years. But stabilization is not recovery. The underlying structural pressures — wage gaps, administrative overload, insufficient planning time, and behavioral challenges in classrooms — have not resolved. One survey found that teachers with unsupportive school administration are nearly twice as likely to be planning to leave (53%) as those who feel their leadership is supportive (28%).

That last data point connects directly to the school-choice decision. Administrative culture is not merely a workplace variable. It is a leading predictor of whether your child's current teacher will still be in that classroom come April — and whether the colleague hired to replace a departing teacher in October has any institutional knowledge of your child at all.

Roughly 44% of public schools reported having at least one teaching vacancy in recent national surveys. Hard-to-staff subjects — math, science, special education, and bilingual education — face the deepest shortages. If your child is in one of those subject areas and their school is already running thin, teacher stability is not an abstract concern. It is the daily instructional reality.

Where Mission-Driven Small Charter Schools Have a Structural Advantage

Large comprehensive high schools face a specific retention problem: anonymity. A teacher managing 150 students across six sections per semester, navigating a sprawling district bureaucracy, and rarely interacting meaningfully with school leadership is a teacher at elevated risk of burnout and exit. Nothing in that environment signals to the teacher that they matter as an individual, that their specific skills are seen and valued, or that the school's mission is something they personally shaped.

Small, mission-aligned charter schools disrupt that dynamic by design. When a school enrolls 80 to 120 students total, teachers know every kid by name — and every administrator knows every teacher by name. Mission alignment is frequently the primary reason an educator chose the school in the first place, and that intrinsic motivation is one of the most durable buffers against attrition that any school can cultivate. Research on charter school culture consistently finds that when hiring genuinely reflects mission fit, and when teachers feel ownership over the school's direction, voluntary departure rates fall significantly.

This is part of what makes a school like Moreno Valley High School worth examining as a concrete example. Located in Angel Fire, New Mexico, MVHS is a small charter high school serving students in grades 9 through 12 that has earned consistent placement on major national rankings — Newsweek, US News & World Report, and the Washington Post Challenge Index — year after year. A graduating senior once captured what drives that consistency in plain terms: "The strength of our community defines us." That kind of community identity, built deliberately at a small scale, is precisely the condition under which teacher retention and student success reinforce each other over time. It is not a marketing claim. It is a structural outcome of being small, mission-focused, and deeply rooted in a specific place and community.

The Questions Families Should Ask on Every School Tour

What does teacher turnover actually reveal about a school? It is simultaneously a leading indicator of instructional quality, school culture, and leadership health. A school with chronic teacher turnover will struggle to maintain curriculum coherence, student-teacher trust, and academic momentum regardless of how its programs are described in an enrollment brochure or highlighted on a ranking list. Here are the questions that surface what a polished tour script will not:

  • What is your year-over-year same-school teacher retention rate? Rates above 85% suggest genuine stability. Rates below 70% warrant serious follow-up questions about leadership, compensation, and culture.
  • What is the median number of years your current faculty has been at this school? Median tenure tells you far more than average tenure, which can be skewed by a handful of long-serving veterans surrounded by constant churn among newer staff.
  • How many teachers left mid-year in the past two school years? Mid-year departures are significantly more disruptive than summer attrition and frequently signal a culture or leadership problem rather than personal life circumstances.
  • What does your school do specifically to reduce teacher burnout? Vague answers — "we have a great team," "our teachers really love it here" — are a red flag. Concrete answers involving protected planning time, reduced non-instructional duties, and accessible leadership are meaningfully encouraging.
  • How is instruction covered when a teacher leaves or takes extended leave? Schools with a genuine contingency plan — cross-trained staff, structured coverage protocols — handle inevitable turnover far better than those who rely entirely on short-notice substitute agencies.

No school will answer every one of these questions perfectly. Schools with genuinely stable faculties will answer them confidently and specifically. Schools with chronic retention problems will not.

Stability Is a Differentiator Families Can Actually Evaluate

In a January 2026 parent survey by the National School Choice Awareness Foundation, 75% of U.S. parents of school-aged children actively engaged with school choice — comparing and selecting schools rather than defaulting to a neighborhood assignment. That is a remarkable proportion of families doing serious homework. And yet teacher stability almost never appears on the evaluation checklist, even though the research ties it directly to the academic outcomes those same families are working so hard to optimize.

The 2026 teacher shortage is not a crisis that will resolve itself before your child needs a school. It is the operating environment. Within that environment, the schools most likely to protect your child's learning are those that have built cultures, compensation structures, leadership practices, and community identities that keep great teachers in the building not just through August, but through May — and then bring them back the following September.

That is worth a direct conversation before you sign an enrollment form. Not after you notice your child has had three different math teachers since September.

---

Teacher turnover is one of the most underexamined variables in K–12 school quality. In 2026, with roughly 1 in 8 teaching positions nationwide either vacant or underqualified, it may also be one of the most consequential — and unlike published rankings, it is something every family can ask about directly.

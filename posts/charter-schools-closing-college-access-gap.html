How Charter Schools Are Closing the College Access Gap

# How Charter Schools Are Closing the College Access Gap — And Why the Pipeline Has to Start in Kindergarten

A student who graduates high school from a low-income community faces a statistical headwind that begins long before senior year. For the class of 2024, only 52.3% of graduates from low-income high schools enrolled immediately in postsecondary education, compared to 64.2% of graduates from higher-income high schools — a gap that the National Student Clearinghouse has tracked for years without meaningful narrowing. And that's just enrollment. When researchers look at actual degree completion six years out, the divide becomes more stark: among the class of 2018, only 24.9% of students from high-poverty high schools had completed a college degree, compared to 58.7% of their peers from low-poverty schools.

Those aren't abstract percentages. They're a portrait of foreclosed economic mobility — and they explain why a growing number of charter school networks are rethinking what it means to prepare students for life after graduation.

The Gap Is Racial and Economic — and It Compounds

The postsecondary enrollment gap tracks closely with both income and race. According to the College Board's Education Pays report, in 2022, 81% of Asian and 64% of white recent high school graduates enrolled in college within a year of graduation, compared with 59% of Black and 58% of Hispanic graduates. Low-income high schools post an immediate enrollment rate that runs about 12 percentage points below higher-income high schools — and the gap widens further once students arrive on campus and face the reality of tuition, food insecurity, and competing family obligations.

What makes this particularly urgent right now is that the labor market is simultaneously demanding more credentials, not fewer. Certificate enrollment grew nearly 10% in fall 2024 versus 5% for undergraduate enrollment overall — meaning there are more viable pathways to economic stability than ever before, including two-year degrees, industry certifications, and registered apprenticeships. But accessing any of them requires a K-12 experience that actively prepares students for those options, not one that simply delivers content and hopes for the best.

Why a Strong K-12 Education Alone Isn't Enough

Is a good K-12 education sufficient to close the college access gap? Research increasingly says no — not by itself. Students from low-income backgrounds and communities of color face a specific set of structural barriers that academic preparation alone doesn't overcome: unfamiliarity with the college application process, a lack of adults in their network who have navigated higher education, financial aid confusion, and a curriculum that may never have connected their cultural identity to a sense of academic belonging. The most effective charter schools are recognizing this and building systems — not just classrooms — that address the full arc of a student's journey from early childhood through career entry.

This is the difference between a school that teaches well and a network that engineers pathways. The school gets students to graduation. The network gets them to a credential, a career, and genuine economic mobility.

What Integrated Pipeline Models Look Like in Practice

The charter schools making real headway on postsecondary outcomes tend to share a few structural features:

  • Culturally responsive curriculum from the start. When students see their cultural heritage and personal identity reflected in what and how they learn, academic engagement and persistence improve. This isn't a soft accommodation — it's a documented driver of both enrollment and completion rates for Black and Latino students.
  • College advising embedded in the school day, not offered as an afterthought. Students who have access to dedicated counselors, campus visits, and hands-on help navigating financial aid are far more likely to both apply and enroll.
  • Career pathway awareness that begins before high school. Knowing that a specific career exists, and understanding the educational steps to reach it, gives students a concrete reason to persist academically through the middle grades and beyond.
  • On-ramps to postsecondary education that reduce friction. Dual enrollment, partnerships with community colleges, and on-site technical training programs shrink the psychological and logistical distance between K-12 and what comes next.
  • Wraparound support that addresses barriers to persistence. Housing instability, food insecurity, and family health crises derail college-going students at every income level — but disproportionately affect those from low-income households. Networks that address those barriers aren't doing charity work; they're protecting their postsecondary investment.

California charter schools offer a useful data point here. A 2024 California Charter Schools Association study found that charter public schools send substantially more socioeconomically disadvantaged, Latino, and Black students to University of California and California State University campuses compared to the public school average. Site-based charters in particular were sending nearly triple the percentage of English learners and students with disabilities to those universities. These outcomes don't happen by accident — they're the product of intentional pipeline design built into the organizational model from the ground up.

Networks That Go Beyond the School Building

One example of this integrated approach is BRICK Rochester, which structures its entire model around the premise that excellent K-12 schooling must be paired with college and career programming to deliver real outcomes. Alongside its K-12 schools, BRICK operates Gateway U — a local, affordable higher education program explicitly designed so that students can continue working while leveling up their credentials — and Career Pathways programming that includes one-on-one career advising, exploration of growing industries, and coaching structured around participants' own schedules and goals. The network's K-12 curriculum is explicitly culturally responsive and individualized, built on the understanding that a student's cultural heritage and personal identity aren't obstacles to academic achievement — they're the engine of it.

The School Choice Dimension

Charter school enrollment has grown by 11.7% from fall 2019 to fall 2024, even as traditional public school enrollment fell by 3.9% over the same period. Part of what's driving that demand is precisely this question of postsecondary outcomes. Families — particularly Black and Latino families in urban communities — are increasingly choosing charter schools not just because of test scores or school culture, but because they want an institution that will take genuine responsibility for where their child lands at age 22, not just at age 18.

That's a higher bar than most schools are willing to accept. But it's the right bar. The college access gap doesn't close with better instruction alone — it closes when schools build the entire pipeline, from kindergarten readiness through career entry, and then resource it seriously enough to actually work.

The schools willing to hold themselves to that standard are the ones worth choosing.

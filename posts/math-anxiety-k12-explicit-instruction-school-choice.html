Math Anxiety in K-12: What Schools Can Do

More Than One in Three Students Dreads Math—and the Research Explains Why

A child who freezes when called on to solve a problem at the board isn't simply "bad at math." They may be experiencing math anxiety—a measurable emotional and physiological response to mathematical tasks that interferes with learning in real time. The OECD reports that over 33% of young learners experience mathematics anxiety, characterized by fear, tension, avoidance, and feelings of inferiority toward mathematics. That's roughly one in three students, starting as early as elementary school, carrying a psychological barrier into every math lesson.

What the research increasingly shows is that math anxiety isn't random. It has identifiable causes, it compounds over time, and—critically for families choosing a school—it can be significantly reduced by how math is taught from the very beginning.

What the Latest Research Says About Causes

A comprehensive 2026 scoping review published in Frontiers in Psychology analyzed 132 empirical studies on K–12 mathematics anxiety published between 2019 and 2025. The review found that antecedent factors influencing math anxiety fall into two broad categories: individual influences (such as gender and grade level) and environmental influences (such as parents, teachers, peers, and classroom culture).

Of those environmental factors, a teacher's instructional approach stands out as one of the most consistent and actionable variables. The research identifies what it calls "process factors"—the proximal mechanisms through which background conditions actually produce anxiety in students—as encompassing behavioral, cognitive, and emotional dimensions, with cognitive aspects dominating the literature. Working memory, metacognitive awareness, and a student's sense of control over math tasks are the cognitive levers most reliably linked to whether anxiety rises or falls during a lesson.

This has a direct implication for how classrooms should be designed: when students don't know what to expect, don't have clear procedures to follow, or haven't built fluency in foundational skills, their cognitive load spikes—and anxiety follows. Research shows that arithmetic fluency and working memory further modulate anxiety experiences, with behavioral, cognitive, and emotional processes interacting dynamically throughout learning.

The Anxiety–Achievement Loop: Why the Elementary Years Are Critical

What happens to math-anxious students over time? The research is clear: the effects don't stay contained to math class. Mathematics anxiety consistently predicts lower self-efficacy, reduced engagement, and poorer academic performance. The impact extends well beyond elementary school. Students with higher math anxiety are significantly more likely to avoid STEAM-related majors and careers, narrowing their professional options before they ever reach high school graduation.

The 2024 NAEP results make the urgency concrete. Eighth grade math performance was nine points below pre-pandemic levels—roughly a full grade level of lost ground. While that gap has multiple causes, anxiety-driven avoidance beginning in early elementary is part of the picture. Longitudinal research demonstrates that young students' feelings about mathematics and their arithmetic performance reciprocally predict one another over time—meaning anxiety and low performance reinforce each other in a self-perpetuating cycle that is far easier to interrupt early than to reverse later.

This is why the elementary years matter so much. A student who develops genuine math confidence and procedural fluency in grades K–3 is building a psychological foundation, not just a skill set.

How Explicit, Sequential Instruction Interrupts the Cycle

The most actionable finding from recent research concerns what actually reduces math anxiety—and it points directly toward pedagogy, not just emotional support.

Research findings indicate that instructional strategies focused on enhancing students' mathematical understanding were more effective than emotional regulation interventions alone in reducing math anxiety. In other words, teaching math more clearly and sequentially does more to lower student anxiety than reassurance or mindfulness exercises in isolation—because genuine competence is the most powerful antidote to fear.

Effective anxiety-reducing instruction tends to share several key features:

  • Explicit teacher-directed lessons with clear modeling before students attempt independent practice
  • Sequential skill-building where each concept rests on a solidly mastered prior concept
  • Consistent procedures that reduce cognitive load and give students reliable tools to reach correct answers
  • Frequent low-stakes practice that builds fluency and automaticity without timed-test pressure
  • Immediate, specific corrective feedback so errors are addressed before they harden into misconceptions

The 2026 Frontiers in Psychology scoping review concluded that effective interventions must pair anxiety-reducing classroom environments with skill enhancement and self-regulation training—and that neither approach alone is sufficient. This rules out both the "just make math feel fun" approach and rote memorization in isolation. What the evidence supports is structured, explicit teaching embedded in a classroom climate that treats mistakes as information rather than failure.

What This Means for School Choice

Parents evaluating K–6 schools should ask a pointed question: How does this school's math instruction prevent anxiety from forming in the first place?

The answer won't be found in flashy manipulatives or an engaging app. It will be found in the school's instructional philosophy—whether teachers model procedures explicitly, whether the curriculum is logically sequenced so students are never left to flounder, and whether the school treats mathematical fluency as a serious goal alongside conceptual understanding.

Jefferson Academy, a tuition-free public charter school in Kaysville, Utah serving grades K–6, offers one concrete model of this philosophy in practice. The school describes its instruction as "explicit, sequential, and teacher-directed with a multi-sensory approach" and grounds its curriculum in the Core Knowledge Sequence—a systematic, cumulative framework that builds content knowledge year over year. When students know what to expect, have the foundational knowledge to engage with new material, and receive clear direct instruction, the structural conditions for math anxiety are substantially undermined from the start.

The Bottom Line for Parents and Educators

Math anxiety is not a fixed trait children are born with. Teachers' instructional styles are consistently identified in the research as an environmental antecedent—meaning the school a child attends actively shapes their emotional relationship with mathematics, not just their test scores.

For families weighing school options, and for educators examining their own practice, the research in 2026 converges on a clear principle: the most reliable path to math confidence runs through math competence. Schools that invest in explicit, sequential instruction from kindergarten onward aren't merely teaching procedures—they're building the psychological scaffolding that keeps students engaged, resilient, and on track all the way through high school and beyond. In an era when NAEP scores remain below pre-pandemic levels and over a third of students report dreading math, that kind of intentional instructional design isn't a luxury. It's the job.

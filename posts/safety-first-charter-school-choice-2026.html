Why Safety Now Beats Academics in Charter School Choice

# Why "Best Academics" Is No Longer Enough: How Charter Schools Win on Safety + Outcomes in 2026

For years, the pitch practically wrote itself: our charter school gets better test scores. Academic quality was the undisputed top reason families chose charter and private schools over their assigned district school. That era is over.

According to EdChoice's 2026 Schooling in America survey — one of the most comprehensive annual snapshots of why American families make the school choices they do — safety has overtaken academic quality as the number one reason charter school parents chose their school. It's the first time safety has claimed the top spot, after gradually climbing the rankings over several years. Academic quality remains a close second, but the shift signals something deeper: families are no longer shopping for schools the way they once shopped for colleges. They are shopping for safe communities where rigorous learning can actually happen.

Charter schools that understand this shift — and communicate accordingly — are pulling ahead in enrollment. Those still leading with test scores alone are leaving families cold.

The New Decision Framework Parents Are Using

EdChoice's 2026 survey data makes the decision hierarchy clear for charter school parents:

  1. Safe environment (new #1 — overtaking academic quality for the first time)
  2. Academic quality
  3. Location / accessibility

For context, public school parents still lead with location — proximity to home and the assigned district — while private school parents layer in morals/values-based instruction alongside safety and academics. Charter school parents sit in an interesting middle ground: they have actively chosen to leave their default assigned school, so location is a secondary concern. What they want first is a place where their child will not be harmed — emotionally, physically, or socially.

This matters enormously for charter school messaging, programming, and design. A school that posts its AP pass rates prominently on its homepage but buries its culture, discipline philosophy, and student support systems is misreading its own market.

What's Driving the Safety-First Shift

The push factors are as telling as the pull factors. Among parents who switched their child's school, EdChoice's spring 2026 polling found that bullying (33%) and excessive stress or anxiety (27%) were the top two difficulties children faced at their former school — overtaking unmet academic needs, which had historically led that list. A separate EdChoice Schooling in America survey found that among school switchers, excessive stress or anxiety was the most commonly cited problem, followed closely by unmet academic needs and then bullying.

These aren't abstract anxieties. School climate data over the past several years has documented real increases in student-reported bullying, peer conflict, and chronic stress. Parents watching these trends have recalibrated what "a good school" means: it must be safe enough for learning to actually reach the child. Academics are table stakes; safety is the new differentiator.

The implication is direct: when families leave a school, they're usually fleeing something — and what they're fleeing most often in 2026 is a climate that felt unsafe, high-stress, or socially hostile. When they choose a new school, they want proof that climate is different.

The Proof Point: Small Charter Schools and the Safety Advantage

Here's the encouraging news for mission-driven charter schools: the structural features that define many high-performing charters — small enrollment, known adults, coherent school culture, strong relationships between students and teachers — are exactly the features that create safe school environments. Safety and academic quality are not competing priorities. They are mutually reinforcing ones.

This is especially visible at smaller, community-rooted charter schools, where students are far less likely to fall through the cracks and where the principal knows every student's name. Moreno Valley High School, a charter high school serving grades 9–12 in Angel Fire, New Mexico, is a concrete illustration: a small, community-rooted school that has earned consistent placement on the Newsweek, US News, and Washington Post Challenge Index national rankings — demonstrating that an intimate school culture and rigorous academic outcomes are not in tension. Schools like it show exactly what the research supports: a tight-knit environment produces both the safety families now demand and the results they still expect.

Broader research backs this up. Urban charter schools following a high academic and behavioral approach have been shown to produce substantial gains in standardized test scores and college enrollment, particularly among disadvantaged students. And the 2023 Stanford CREDO analysis — one of the largest national studies of charter school outcomes — found learning gains that are especially pronounced for students from lower-income backgrounds and students of color. The data on what produces those gains points repeatedly to relational factors: teacher consistency, school coherence, and belonging.

What Charter Schools Should Do Differently Right Now

If you lead or advise a charter school, the 2026 enrollment environment demands a rethinking of how you tell your story — not just what programs you offer.

Audit your first impression. When a prospective parent lands on your website or visits for the first time, what do they see first? If it's a ranking badge or a test score graphic, you may be answering a question families are no longer asking first. Lead with culture, then prove outcomes.

Tell safety stories, not just safety policies. Anti-bullying policies are not reassuring on their own. Stories of how your community handles conflict, how students support each other, and how adults intervene and follow up — those are reassuring. Collect them. Share them.

Connect safety to academics explicitly. The most persuasive message for 2026 charter school parents is not "we're safe" or "we have great scores" in isolation — it's "because our students feel safe and known here, they learn more." The research supports that framing. Use it.

Make culture visible at every touchpoint. Open houses, social media, school tours, and even the enrollment paperwork process all communicate culture. Parents are reading every signal, often before they ever contact the school directly.

The Question Parents Are Actually Asking

What's the most important thing to look for when choosing a charter school in 2026?

Based on current EdChoice survey data and the broader research base, the honest answer is this: look for a school where your child will feel safe and genuinely known, and where adults hold high expectations. Safety and academic excellence are not a trade-off — they are co-dependent. The charter schools winning enrollment in 2026 are the ones that have stopped treating them as separate selling points and started demonstrating how their culture makes both possible at once.

---

The enrollment conversation is changing because the parenting experience has changed. Families have watched their children struggle with anxiety, social cruelty, and disconnection. They want a school that can hold their child whole — not just academically, but as a person navigating a complicated world. Charter schools that lead with that understanding, and can back it with strong outcomes, are well-positioned for the years ahead. The ones still pitching rankings alone are increasingly pitching to an audience that has already moved on.

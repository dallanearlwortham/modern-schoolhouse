Restorative Discipline in Charter Schools: 2026 Guide

# Restorative Discipline in Charter Schools: What the Evidence Says and What Families Should Ask in 2026

A student disrupts class. In one school, she's sent to the office and suspended for three days. In another, she's guided through a structured conversation about who was affected and what repair looks like — and she's back in her seat by the next period. These aren't just different responses to the same event. They reflect fundamentally different theories about what school discipline is actually for.

That philosophical divide has significant, measurable consequences for students — and it's one of the least-discussed factors families weigh when choosing a school.

What Is Restorative Discipline, and How Does It Differ from Zero Tolerance?

Restorative discipline — sometimes called restorative justice in schools — replaces the automatic punishment model with a relationship-centered one. Instead of asking "What rule was broken and what's the consequence?", a restorative approach asks "Who was harmed, what do they need, and how can the harm be repaired?"

In practice, this can look like:

  • Restorative conversations — brief, structured dialogues between a student and a teacher or peer, often using guided open questions
  • Peer mediation circles — facilitated group processes that bring affected parties together
  • Harm-repair conferences — more formal structured meetings for serious incidents, involving families and support staff
  • Community-building circles — proactive relationship-building used schoolwide, not just in response to conflict

Zero-tolerance policies, by contrast, apply predetermined exclusionary consequences — typically suspension or expulsion — to specific behaviors, with little room for context or judgment. Despite their widespread adoption in the 1990s, research from the American Psychological Association has found that zero-tolerance approaches do not meaningfully improve school safety and can remove students from learning without a measurable deterrent effect.

What Does the Research Actually Show?

The evidence base for restorative practices is substantial and continues to strengthen, though researchers are careful to note that implementation quality matters enormously.

A 2021 systematic review of 34 studies found that restorative practices improve school climate, reduce suspensions, and build social-emotional skills. That is not a single promising pilot — it represents two decades of accumulated evidence across widely varying school contexts.

The most rigorous large-scale study to date, published by the Learning Policy Institute (2023), examined data from 485 middle schools and approximately 2 million students. Its findings were striking: student exposure to restorative practices improved standardized test performance in both English language arts and mathematics, reduced the probability of suspension, and decreased the number of days suspended for students who did receive one. At the school level, increased use of restorative practices was associated with declines in misbehavior, gang membership, victimization, depressive symptoms, and substance abuse — a breadth of outcomes that few single school interventions can claim.

Critically, the effects on academic outcomes were stronger for Black and Latino students than for White students — meaning well-implemented restorative practices don't just improve outcomes overall, they actively narrow the discipline and achievement gaps that have persisted for decades.

Those gaps are wide. Black students are nearly four times more likely than White students to receive an out-of-school suspension, according to Learning Policy Institute data — a disparity that holds across all student subpopulations, grade levels, and school types. Black boys represent roughly 8% of U.S. students but account for approximately 18% of students suspended, according to data compiled by EdTrust. Black students also lose, on average, five times as many instructional days as White students due to disproportionate discipline. Restorative discipline offers one of the most evidence-backed tools available for addressing this disparity directly.

Real-world adoption is accelerating. In New York City — the country's largest school district — suspensions dropped 8% in the first half of the 2025-26 school year compared to the same period a year earlier, falling below pre-pandemic levels. Advocates and school officials credited restorative justice programs and peer mediation as meaningful contributors to the trend, though proposed budget cuts now threaten some of those programs heading into 2026-27.

Why Charter Schools Have a Structural Advantage Here

Can charter schools implement restorative discipline more effectively than traditional public schools? For many, the answer is yes — not because charter school educators are inherently more committed, but because of how charter schools are structured.

Traditional public schools operate within district-wide discipline codes that are often slow to change, shaped by collective bargaining agreements, and standardized across schools with widely varying student populations. A discipline policy written for 80,000 students across 150 campuses is a blunt instrument. Restorative discipline, as researchers consistently note, works best when it is embedded in a whole-school culture — when every adult in the building uses the same relational language, when restorative conversations happen in the hallway and not just the principal's office, and when the approach is tailored to the specific community being served.

Charter schools hold their own charters and set their own school cultures. A charter school can decide, from the moment it opens, that restorative practice is central to its identity — hiring for it, training staff deeply, building it into the school schedule, and measuring it year over year. That kind of intentional, schoolwide coherence is harder to achieve inside a large district system with centralized HR, rotating staff assignments, and competing policy mandates from above.

This is exactly why discipline philosophy — not just test scores — has become a sharper school-selection variable for families who understand what the research shows. In Texas, where charter school operators design their own programs and cultures from the ground up, statewide bodies like the Texas Public Charter School Association — which advocates for diverse, high-quality public charter schools and operates a school-finder tool to help Texas families connect with schools that match their values and needs — have become important resources for parents navigating these decisions.

What Families Should Ask When Evaluating a School's Discipline Culture

Discipline philosophy is rarely featured in school marketing materials, but it shapes daily life for students more than most families realize. Here are the questions to bring to any school visit or enrollment conversation:

  1. What does your code of conduct say about suspensions? Ask specifically whether the school has zero-tolerance triggers — behaviors that result in automatic suspension regardless of context. If so, ask how that interacts with any restorative approaches the school uses.
  1. How are behavior expectations taught, not just enforced? Strong schools teach behavioral norms explicitly and early, rather than assuming students arrive already knowing them.
  1. What happens after a conflict? The answer reveals a school's actual culture. Listen for language about structured conversations, repair processes, and relationship restoration — not just "consequences."
  1. Who is involved in discipline decisions? Schools where counselors, families, and the affected students themselves participate in discipline responses are practicing what the research supports.
  1. What do your suspension and expulsion data look like, broken down by student group? Any school should be able to share this. Significant racial or disability-status disparities in suspension rates are a serious warning sign.
  1. How are staff trained — and how often? Restorative practices succeed when all staff use them consistently, not just administrators. Ask whether professional development in restorative approaches is ongoing or a one-time onboarding item.

A Note on the Nuance: What Restorative Discipline Is Not

Research is clear that restorative practices are not a substitute for school safety or clear behavioral expectations. They work best alongside — not instead of — a structured environment with firm, consistently applied norms. Serious incidents involving imminent safety risks still require formal responses. The research-backed argument is not that consequences should disappear, but that reflexive exclusion is a poor first response to the vast majority of student behavior challenges, and that relationship repair is more durable than punishment alone as a behavior-change mechanism.

Schools that implement restorative approaches with fidelity — with genuine staff training, visible leadership commitment, and multi-year investment — show real results across the research base. Schools that adopt the vocabulary of restorative justice without the underlying cultural shift tend to see little change. A 2023 Learning Policy Institute analysis found that even within schools nominally implementing restorative programs, students in higher proportions of Black or economically disadvantaged populations were actually receiving less access to restorative practices than their peers — a finding that underscores how much implementation quality, and equity of access within schools, matters.

For families, that distinction is the whole ballgame. A school can claim to be restorative. Visiting, observing how adults interact with students in hallways and common spaces, reviewing discipline data, and asking direct questions will tell you far more than any brochure or website.

---

School discipline is one of those topics where the gap between what parents assume is happening and what is actually happening can be surprisingly large. Taking time to investigate a school's real discipline culture — not just its stated values — is one of the most underrated moves in the school-choice process. The evidence now strongly suggests that how a school handles conflict shapes not just its climate, but its students' academic trajectories, mental health, and long-term outcomes. That is worth a few hard questions before enrollment day.

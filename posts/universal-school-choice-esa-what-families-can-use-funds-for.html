Universal School Choice Is Here: What ESAs Actually Cover

# Universal School Choice Is Here — What Families Can Actually Do With an ESA

Three years ago, not a single U.S. state offered a truly universal school choice program. Today, families in more than a dozen states can access public education dollars regardless of their income, their child's disability status, or whether they've ever seriously considered an alternative to their neighborhood public school. The speed of this shift is difficult to overstate — and most families still don't know what they can actually do with the money.

Here's what the landscape looks like in 2025, and what it means in practical terms for your family.

The Surge in Universal School Choice Is Real — and Recent

Just three years ago, no state had a universal school choice program. Today, 13 states have universal or near-universal programs in place. The legislative momentum hasn't slowed: heading into the 2025 session, 33 states had some form of private school choice on the books, with 12 offering universal eligibility regardless of family income — and more states are actively moving to join that list.

At the beginning of 2020, ESA programs operated in only five states, while approximately a dozen states offered traditional private school vouchers. As of the start of 2025, more than 30 states offer ESAs, education vouchers, or tax-credit scholarship programs. States like Indiana, following the lead of Arizona, Ohio, and West Virginia, have moved to make all K-12 students eligible for private education subsidies regardless of household income.

That's a remarkable five-year run — and it shows no signs of slowing.

What Is an ESA, and How Does It Actually Work?

An Education Savings Account (ESA) is not a voucher. The distinction matters more than it might seem.

With a traditional voucher, a state pays a private school directly on a student's behalf — and the transaction ends there. With an ESA, eligible families receive a portion of their state's per-pupil funding in a dedicated account they control, which can be used for a wide menu of approved learning expenses. The money follows the student, not the school building, and families are not required to spend it all in one place.

In practice, most education savings programs provide families with a debit card, an online purchasing portal, or a reimbursement system. Every ESA program is state-run with its own name, eligibility rules, and approved expense list — so the specifics vary, but the core mechanism is the same: public education dollars, directed by parents.

What Can Families Actually Spend ESA Funds On?

This is where most families are genuinely surprised. ESAs are not just a private school tuition subsidy. In many states, the approved expense list is broad enough to let families build something that no single school building could offer on its own.

Approved expenses typically include:

  • Private school tuition and fees — the most commonly cited use, but far from the only one
  • Tutoring and supplemental instruction — one-on-one academic support, whether after school or as a primary learning model
  • Educational therapies — speech therapy, occupational therapy, and behavioral therapy in many states
  • Curriculum and instructional materials — textbooks, workbooks, and structured home or microschool learning programs
  • Online learning programs — virtual courses, classes, and accredited educational platforms
  • Assistive technology — devices and tools that support students with learning differences or physical disabilities
  • Standardized testing fees — including college entrance exams in some states
  • Transportation — to and from approved educational providers in select states

For families of children with disabilities, the flexibility goes even further. Florida's FES-UA (Family Empowerment Scholarship for Students with Unique Abilities) allows families to spend funds on private school tuition, therapies, curriculum materials, tutoring, and other approved educational expenses. During the 2025–26 school year, the program funded more than 140,000 students and provided average annual awards of approximately $10,000 through quarterly deposits. Fifteen states now have active ESA programs with dedicated priority funding for special needs students, with some annual awards reaching $17,000 or more.

For families of children with autism, this is significant: in states where Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) therapy qualifies as an approved expense, ESA funds can directly offset the cost of intensive behavioral support — often the single largest out-of-pocket cost these families face each year.

The "Blended Approach" That Smart Families Are Building

One of the most underreported shifts in school choice is how families are stacking ESA benefits to construct something that no single school or clinic could offer alone.

Many families use their ESA to create a blended approach — part-time enrollment at a specialized school or learning center, combined with individualized tutoring or therapy services their primary school doesn't provide. Rather than being forced to choose between a classroom environment that builds social and academic skills and a clinical setting that delivers intensive behavioral support, families who understand their ESA benefits increasingly don't have to choose at all.

That "both/and" philosophy is exactly what Prism is built around. The Miami-based early intervention center serves children under 6 with autism, integrating 1:1 ABA therapy from a Registered Behavior Therapist with group school readiness instruction under the same roof — because families shouldn't have to sacrifice preschool socialization to access behavioral therapy, or forfeit therapy hours to sit in a classroom. Florida's ESA programs are among the funding tools making this kind of integrated, multi-service model financially accessible for more families than ever before.

Practical Questions to Ask Before You Apply

Universal eligibility doesn't mean automatic approval, and a broad approved-expense list doesn't mean every purchase will be covered without scrutiny. Before applying for any ESA program, get clear answers to these questions:

  1. What are the actual eligibility requirements? Some "universal" programs still prioritize lower-income families in early application windows, or operate waitlists when funding is oversubscribed.
  2. Which specific expenses are approved in my state? Therapy coverage in particular varies widely — request the approved vendor or expense category list in writing before committing to any provider or service contract.
  3. How are funds disbursed? Debit card, reimbursement, or direct vendor payment — the mechanism affects how you plan purchases and manage cash flow across the school year.
  4. Can ESA funds be coordinated with insurance? For families already using private insurance or Medicaid for therapies, understanding how the two funding streams interact is essential before assuming costs can be doubled up.
  5. What happens to unused funds at year-end? Some states allow rollover into the following school year; others require that funds be spent within the year or forfeited entirely.

Always verify your state's current guidelines with your ESA administrator before making purchases — approved expense categories can change between school years, and a disqualified purchase may need to be repaid out of pocket.

Why This Matters Beyond the Policy Headlines

The school choice surge has moved faster than public awareness of it. Millions of parents are currently eligible for programs they don't know exist, or assume are reserved for families in financial hardship. In 2023, Florida expanded eligibility to make its flagship scholarship program universal — meaning all Florida families with children eligible for public K-12 education can now apply, regardless of household income. Florida was an early mover, but it's no longer the exception.

By the start of 2025, 12 states had enacted universal school choice programs, giving nearly all K-12 students in those states access to public funds for private education options. That's a structural change to how American K-12 education is financed — not a pilot, not a niche program for edge cases.

Whether a family wants a private school that reflects their values, a tutoring arrangement that closes a specific learning gap, a therapy provider that gives their young child a stronger shot at kindergarten readiness, or some combination of all three, there is now a real, state-funded pathway worth understanding.

The most important first step is simply knowing it's there.

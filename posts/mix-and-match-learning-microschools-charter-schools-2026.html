The Unbundled School: What Mix-and-Match Learning Means for Charters in 2026

# The Unbundled School: What Mix-and-Match Learning Means for Charters in 2026

For three decades, if a family wanted a publicly funded alternative to their assigned district school, charter schools were essentially the only option on the menu. That menu just got a lot longer—and charter schools are feeling it.

The rise of microschools, learning pods, homeschool co-ops, and ESA-funded hybrid arrangements is fundamentally reshaping how American families think about K-12 education. The shift isn't a post-pandemic blip. It's a structural change, and 2026 is proving to be the year it moves from trend to mainstream pressure on every school type—including charters.

What "Mix-and-Match Learning" Actually Means

Mix-and-match learning isn't a single model. It's a philosophy: rather than enrolling a child in one school that handles everything, families are assembling custom educational experiences across multiple providers.

A family practicing mix-and-match learning in 2026 might look like this:

  • One child attends a charter school full time for its specialized STEM or early college program
  • Another child takes core academics through an online public school, attends in-person co-op classes twice a week, and uses ESA funds for private tutoring
  • A third child is enrolled in a microschool for most instruction but participates in a district athletics program

This isn't hypothetical. A 2026 EdChoice study found that among households that homeschool at least one child, 53% simultaneously send another child to a conventional school. Families aren't loyal to sectors—they're loyal to fit.

ESAs Are the Engine Behind the Shift

Education Savings Accounts are the policy mechanism making mix-and-match learning financially viable at scale. As of 2026, twelve states operate ESA programs with universal eligibility, and the private school choice sector saw the largest single-year growth of the modern school choice era, with eight states expanding existing programs or establishing new ones.

ESA dollars aren't limited to tuition. Families can spend them on tutoring, curriculum, online courses, educational therapies, and technology—essentially building a bespoke education from a menu of providers. The National Microschools Center reports that 38% of microschools now receive public funds through state school choice programs.

The result: charter school leaders are paying attention, and not entirely comfortably. An August 2026 survey by the American Enterprise Institute of charter school leaders across Arizona, Florida, and Utah found that nearly half had seen stagnation or decline in student enrollment they attributed to ESA programs drawing families toward microschools and hybrid home-based arrangements.

Are Charters at a Disadvantage?

The honest answer is: some are. Charter schools carry regulatory obligations—state testing mandates, special education requirements, non-sectarian status rules—that private and microschool providers don't. When ESAs didn't exist, these constraints were an acceptable trade-off for being publicly funded. Now that families can access public money without attending a charter school, some charter leaders are questioning whether the trade-off still makes sense.

A 2026 Reason Foundation analysis of that same AEI survey found that about two-thirds of charter school leaders believe the charter sector needs to respond to ESAs by becoming less bureaucratic and granting schools greater operational autonomy. In Arizona—which has the longest-running universal ESA program—42% of charter leaders said they might have opened a private or microschool instead of a charter school if universal ESAs had existed when they launched.

That's a remarkable signal. It suggests the charter school model, while still dominant in publicly funded choice, is facing its most serious marketplace competition yet.

What "Winning" Looks Like for Charter Schools in This Landscape

The charter schools that are holding enrollment and building waiting lists in 2026 share one trait: they offer something families genuinely cannot assemble on their own through mix-and-match arrangements. That usually means an integrated, coherent program model—not just a collection of courses, but a structured experience with real outcomes attached.

Early college high schools are a strong example of this dynamic. Earning college credit requires institutional partnerships with accredited colleges, official transcript agreements, and coordinated scheduling across high school and college coursework. A family cannot replicate that by mixing a microschool with an online provider and some ESA tutoring funds. The outcome—actual college credits on an official transcript, at no cost to the family—requires the school to deliver the whole package.

ITINERIS, a 9th–12th grade charter school in West Jordan, Utah, illustrates exactly why integrated models hold up under marketplace pressure. Students there can earn up to two years of free college credit while completing their high school diploma—a concrete, documented outcome that a mix-and-match arrangement simply cannot replicate without the institutional infrastructure the school provides. That's not a marketing claim; it's a structural advantage that only exists because the program is designed as a coherent whole.

What Should Charter Leaders Take From This Moment?

What does the rise of mix-and-match learning mean for a charter school's enrollment strategy? The clearest answer is this: charter schools that define themselves by their model—rather than just by being "a better school"—will be the most resilient. If a school's unique value can be reassembled piece by piece through an ESA-funded patchwork, families increasingly will try to do exactly that. If it can't be replicated outside the school's walls, families will keep coming.

Practically, that means charter leaders in 2026 should be asking:

  • What does our school offer that cannot be purchased or assembled elsewhere? Dual enrollment credits, coherent project-based learning progressions, specialized therapeutic environments, arts conservatory training—these are hard to replicate through à la carte spending.
  • Are we competing on model or on perception? Perception ("we're safer," "our teachers are better") is fragile in a marketplace with dozens of options. A documented, specific outcome is stickier.
  • Are we making our value legible to families? Families navigating ESA portals and microschool directories are comparison-shopping actively. A charter school's enrollment pitch needs to articulate specific outcomes as clearly as any other consumer product.
  • Are we being honest about what we're not? A charter that tries to compete with microschools on flexibility will usually lose. Leaning into structure, community, and credential outcomes is a stronger play.

The Bigger Picture: Pluralism Is Here to Stay

The expansion of ESAs and microschools doesn't represent a threat to the idea of charter schools. It represents a maturation of the broader school choice movement that charters helped create. The families who would be best served by a specialized, mission-driven charter aren't going to be satisfied by a generic microschool or a patchwork of online providers. But they need to be able to find the charter—and understand clearly why it's worth choosing over every other option now available to them.

In 2026, the education landscape looks less like a set of competing school types and more like a pluralist ecosystem where families are empowered, informed, and genuinely choosy. The charter schools that thrive in that ecosystem will be the ones with a clear, honest answer to the question every family is now asking: Why this school, and not something else?

That's always been the right question. Now, more than ever, families actually have the power to act on the answer.

The Belonging Gap: Why School Connectedness Drives Graduation Rates

# The Belonging Gap: Why School Connectedness Drives High School Graduation Rates

Something quietly alarming happened between 2021 and 2023: students returned to their school buildings, the hallways filled back up — and yet fewer of them felt like they actually belonged there.

According to the CDC's biennial Youth Risk Behavior Survey, the share of high school students who reported feeling close to people at their school dropped from roughly 62% in 2021 to just 55% in 2023. That seven-point slide happened after schools reopened. Kids were physically present. They just weren't connected.

That isn't just a morale problem. It's a graduation problem.

What the Research Says About Belonging and Academic Outcomes

A landmark May 2024 research brief from EdResearch for Action — drawing on decades of peer-reviewed evidence — made the link explicit: across all grade levels, students who feel more connected to school show higher attendance rates, stronger academic performance, and better graduation and post-secondary success rates. The CDC itself tracked more than 14,000 middle and high school students over 20 years and found that those who reported feeling connected to school as adolescents were roughly half as likely to engage in harmful behaviors well into adulthood.

In other words, connectedness isn't soft. It is one of the most powerful predictors of whether a teenager walks across a graduation stage.

Research also consistently finds that certain groups are most at risk of disconnection. Girls were more than 10 percentage points less likely than boys to report feeling close to people at school in the 2023 CDC survey. Students from marginalized communities report the steepest shortfall in supportive adult relationships and meaningful engagement. The students who most need school to function as a lifeline are often the ones least likely to feel it does.

The Chronic Absenteeism Connection

The belonging crisis isn't happening in isolation. It coincides with a stubborn chronic absenteeism epidemic that has outlasted the pandemic by years. A 2024–25 RAND Corporation analysis estimated that approximately 22% of U.S. students were chronically absent during the most recent school year — still well above pre-pandemic norms. In roughly half of urban school districts, more than 30% of students missed at least 18 days of school.

Why do disengaged students stay home? A substantial body of research points to a simple answer: a student who doesn't feel known, valued, or connected to anyone at school has less reason to get there. Behavioral disengagement is measurable as early as elementary school and predictive of dropout years later. Ninth grade is a particularly critical inflection point — whether students are on track in their first year of high school is a stronger predictor of eventual graduation than their 8th-grade test scores.

School connectedness is protective at every grade level. Its absence is expensive.

What Actually Builds a Sense of Belonging at School?

Does school belonging actually affect graduation rates? Yes — consistently and significantly. The EdResearch for Action brief, authored by researchers from Johns Hopkins, the University of Chicago, and the American Institutes for Research, identifies the following as evidence-backed drivers of school connectedness:

  • Teacher-student relationships built on individual knowledge — when educators explicitly show they care about students as people, not just learners, connection deepens
  • Fair and consistent disciplinary policies — students who see adults act against bullying feel measurably more connected
  • Extracurricular participation — clubs, mentoring programs, student governance, and service learning all increase students' investment in school life
  • Culturally inclusive curricula — environments where students see themselves reflected in what they're learning reduce alienation
  • Small school size — federal education research consistently shows that students are more likely to feel connected in smaller environments where anonymity isn't an option

That last point is underappreciated in school choice conversations. Parents often evaluate options on test scores, AP course offerings, and national rankings. But the structural feature that may do the most to keep a student enrolled, engaged, and on track to graduate is something harder to quantify: Can my child actually be known here?

Why Small Charter Schools Are Structurally Positioned to Win on Belonging

Large comprehensive high schools — many enrolling 1,500 to 3,000 students — face a genuine structural belonging problem. It's difficult to build the kind of faculty-student familiarity that research links to persistence when teachers rotate through 150-plus students a day and a graduating class is large enough to fill an arena.

Small charter schools are built differently. By design — and often by necessity — they create environments where students aren't anonymous, where a staff member notices quickly when someone starts to pull away, and where a graduating class feels less like a cohort and more like a community.

Moreno Valley High School, a charter high school serving grades 9–12 in Angel Fire, New Mexico, is a clear illustration of this dynamic. Operating in a state with one of the lowest high school graduation rates in the country, MVHS has earned consistent appearances on the Newsweek, US News and World Report, and Washington Post Challenge Index national high school rankings — repeatedly, year over year. Its graduating classes are small, and the school's own students have described MVHS's defining strength not in terms of test scores or college placements alone, but in terms of what holds it together: a tight-knit culture where teacher devotion meets student ambition and where belonging isn't aspirational — it's structural. That's a meaningful signal in a state where simply finishing high school is a genuine achievement.

What Parents Should Ask on Every School Choice Tour

If school connectedness is this predictive of graduation outcomes, it deserves a seat at the table in every enrollment decision — alongside academics and extracurriculars. Here are the questions worth asking:

  • What is the student-to-teacher ratio? Lower ratios create more opportunities for meaningful individual relationships.
  • What share of students participate in at least one extracurricular activity? Participation is one of the strongest documented predictors of school connection.
  • How does the school identify early disengagement? Strong schools have proactive systems — they don't wait for absences to pile up.
  • What is your four-year graduation rate, and how has it trended over the past five years? Trends matter more than a single year's snapshot.
  • Can students describe the school culture in their own words? A student who says "everyone knows my name here" or "my teachers actually check on me" is giving you data.

The Bottom Line

The question "Does my child feel connected at school?" can sound like a soft concern sitting below the "real" metrics of GPA, test scores, and college acceptance rates. The research is unambiguous that it isn't. School connectedness is the upstream condition that makes attendance sustainable, learning possible, and graduation likely.

As the belonging gap in American high schools persists — even as buildings fill back up and pandemic-era disruptions recede — the schools best positioned to close it are those built from the ground up around one foundational idea: every student deserves to be genuinely known. That's not just a mission statement. It's a graduation strategy.

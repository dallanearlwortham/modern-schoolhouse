Why the Best Charter Schools Teach More Than STEM

The Schools Getting the Best Results Aren't Doing Less — They're Doing More

There's a persistent assumption in K–12 education that the path to stronger academic outcomes runs through narrowing: cut the "extras," add more test prep, double down on math and reading intervention, and watch the scores climb. The logic feels intuitive. The evidence, increasingly, tells a different story.

A growing body of research — alongside the real-world track records of some of the country's most effective charter schools — points toward a counterintuitive conclusion: students educated through a full-spectrum curriculum, one that integrates STEM alongside humanities, athletics, and creative expression, tend to outperform peers stuck in narrower, test-centered models. The debate isn't about whether academic rigor matters. It's about what genuine rigor actually requires — and whether chasing a single metric is the same thing as building a fully educated student.

What the Research Actually Says About STEM in Isolation

STEM education deserves its prominent place in the conversation. A 2025 meta-analysis published in Frontiers in Psychology, drawing on 66 experimental and quasi-experimental studies, confirmed that STEM education has a meaningful positive effect on student learning outcomes — with its strongest impact on cognitive abilities in high school, where the combined effect size reached d = 0.58. Those are real gains. But the same study found that the overall effect "masks key differences" depending on how STEM is taught, what outcomes are measured, and how deeply it connects across disciplines.

Crucially, the researchers found that STEM's cognitive impact is strongest when it leverages "interdisciplinary integration characteristics, which directly strengthen the construction of knowledge networks by linking knowledge across disciplines." Read plainly: STEM works best when it is connected to other domains of learning — not when it operates in a sealed-off silo.

The international context makes this even more urgent. A 2024 survey found that only 28% of U.S. adults believe K–12 STEM education in this country is above average compared to other wealthy nations, and American students ranked 28th of 37 OECD countries in math and 12th in science on recent international assessments. More isolated STEM instruction hasn't closed that gap. The design of how and in what context schools deliver learning matters just as much as the number of hours spent on it.

The Hidden Cost of Narrowing the Curriculum

When schools strip away humanities, arts, and athletics to create more room for academic drill, they don't just lose enrichment. They often lose the very conditions that allow academic learning to take hold in the first place.

Chronic absenteeism is one of the clearest signals of student disengagement, and it has become one of the defining education crises of the post-pandemic era. According to a 2025 CALDER working paper analyzing school-level data across 38 states, chronic absenteeism — an indicator of behavioral disengagement — nearly doubled from about 15% in 2019 to around 28–31% in 2022, and remained elevated at about 23–26% in 2024. Disengaged students don't absorb test prep. They stop showing up. No curriculum, however well-designed on paper, works on students who aren't in the building.

Research on arts integration and STEAM approaches consistently shows that a broader curriculum design directly addresses the attendance and engagement problem. Arts integration and STEAM have been shown to positively impact student achievement, social-emotional learning, student attendance, and teacher retention — four of the most consequential variables in school performance. The mechanism isn't mysterious: when students encounter multiple entry points into learning — through creative expression, physical movement, close reading, and hands-on building — more of them stay meaningfully connected to school.

Peer-reviewed research has also found that integrating the arts into STEAM serves as a critical "emotional hook," bridging the gap between the cognitive domain and the affective domain. In practical terms: it's the difference between a student who understands the material and a student who shows up, engages, and cares whether they get it right. Schools that engineer that bridge deliberately into their daily schedule aren't making an accommodation for struggling learners. They're building the motivational infrastructure that makes academic gains possible for all learners.

What Full-Spectrum Curriculum Actually Looks Like in Practice

A full-spectrum curriculum isn't unfocused or sprawling. Done well, it means treating STEM, humanities, athletics, and creative expression as genuinely interdependent pillars — each reinforcing the others — rather than as competing priorities fighting over a fixed block of instructional time.

Based on the research literature, this design consistently delivers:

  • Stronger engagement and attendance: Students who encounter school through multiple modes of meaning-making are less likely to disengage or become chronically absent. Connection to school, in all its forms, is attendance insurance.
  • Deeper critical thinking: Humanities and the arts build the reasoning, communication, and perspective-taking capacities that technical training alone rarely develops. Writing an argument and designing an experiment call on overlapping cognitive muscles.
  • More durable skill transfer: Athletics and creative expression develop executive function, sustained effort, collaboration under pressure, and the ability to recover from failure — qualities that directly support academic performance across every subject area.
  • More equitable outcomes: Broad curricula surface the strengths of students who underperform in purely test-focused environments, expanding the population of students who are seen and developed as capable learners.
  • Genuine college and career readiness: Employers and universities consistently signal they need graduates who can communicate across contexts, think across domains, and collaborate effectively — capacities built far more reliably by broad curricula than by narrow ones.

What Does a Strong K–12 Charter School Curriculum Need to Cover?

The direct answer: A charter school curriculum built for strong outcomes needs to address rigorous academics and the full range of student development — cognitive, physical, social, creative, and emotional. Schools that treat humanities, athletics, and the arts as expendable elbow room consistently produce graduates who are underprepared for the actual complexity of what comes after K–12 — not because the academics were weak, but because the human being carrying those academics was only partially developed.

This is not a soft argument. It is an increasingly well-documented structural finding about what produces durable student gains.

A landmark 2023 Stanford CREDO study found that charter school students now outpace traditional public school peers by the equivalent of more than two additional weeks of learning in reading each year — a dramatic reversal from findings in 2009 and 2013. But that aggregate improvement conceals enormous variation within the charter sector. The CREDO researchers themselves noted that charter school success "is attainable via many paths," and the schools with the most operational flexibility have used that freedom in very different ways. The ones pulling furthest ahead tend to be those that invested in coherent, broad curriculum design — not just additional instructional time or tighter test-prep sequences.

A School Built on This Philosophy

BRICK Buffalo — a charter school launched in Buffalo, NY in 2023 — puts this full-spectrum approach into direct practice. According to the school's own program description, its comprehensive curriculum explicitly incorporates STEM proficiency, humanities, athletics, and creative expression as interconnected pillars, treating them not as optional enrichments but as core components of what it means to educate the whole student. The school pairs that disciplinary breadth with individualized instruction designed to meet students where they are and connect learning to each student's specific interests — so breadth never comes at the cost of personal relevance or academic depth. The school reports that this approach leads students to far outperform statewide averages.

That combination — wide disciplinary scope, individual fit, and genuine academic rigor — reflects precisely what the research identifies as the design most likely to produce durable gains, not just temporary score increases that fade when test-prep cycles end.

What This Means for Parents Choosing Schools

For families evaluating school options, the questions worth asking go beyond "What are the test scores?" to include: What does a typical school day actually look like? Do students write, build, move, create, and analyze — or do they mostly practice test formats? Does the school design for engagement, or does it assume engagement and respond only when it collapses?

The schools producing the most compelling long-term outcomes — in graduation rates, college enrollment, and career preparation — tend to be those that took the whole student seriously from the start. Broad, well-designed curriculum isn't a compromise with rigor. Increasingly, it is what rigor looks like when it's working.

The "extras" were never extra. They were the foundation.

Why Charter Schools Lead on Restorative Discipline

# Why Charter Schools Are Leading the Shift to Restorative Discipline

When a student disrupts class, the instinct in many traditional schools is still to remove them — a hallway, an in-school suspension room, or an out-of-school suspension notice. That reflex made sense under the zero-tolerance era. The research, however, has been pointing the other way for years, and in 2026 the gap between what the evidence says and what many large districts actually do has become hard to defend.

Charter schools are increasingly filling that gap. Across the country, charters are implementing restorative discipline — practices that prioritize repairing harm, rebuilding relationships, and keeping students connected to learning — more consistently and more effectively than traditional public schools. The reasons are structural as much as philosophical.

What Restorative Discipline Actually Means

Restorative discipline is not leniency. It is a systematic shift away from punitive, exclusionary responses toward structured conversations, accountability circles, peer mediation, and counselor-supported problem-solving. The goal is not to ignore misbehavior but to address its root causes while preserving — or repairing — the relationships between the student, their peers, and the adults in the building.

According to national data cited by both Education Next and the Manhattan Institute, approximately 72% of charter schools use restorative approaches, compared to about 58% of district schools — a 14-percentage-point gap that reflects something real. Charter schools' autonomy over staffing, scheduling, culture, and discipline policy gives them the structural freedom to build restorative systems from the ground up, rather than retrofitting them onto existing bureaucratic frameworks.

Why Zero Tolerance Failed — and Why It Persists in Districts

Research from the American Psychological Association has shown that zero-tolerance approaches do not reliably improve behavior and can remove students from learning unnecessarily. The consequences extend well beyond the individual student: exclusionary discipline is linked to racial and economic disparities in educational opportunity and is a documented contributor to what researchers call the school-to-prison pipeline.

Large district systems face a distinct challenge when trying to change course. Union contracts can constrain how staff time is allocated. Central-office mandates may conflict with campus-level implementation. Professional development budgets get squeezed. And without a coherent school culture behind the practice, restorative circles become an add-on rather than a foundation — something teachers do when they have a spare moment rather than the default way the school handles conflict.

Charter schools, accountable to a governing board and a defined mission rather than a district bureaucracy, can commit to restorative practices as a whole-school philosophy — and sustain that commitment through hiring, onboarding, and ongoing professional development.

What the Research Actually Shows

What is the evidence that restorative discipline works in schools?

The evidence is substantial. High schools that implemented restorative discipline practices saw a 35% decline in student arrests at school and a 15% drop in arrests outside of school, according to a University of Chicago study analyzing a decade of data from Chicago Public Schools. The same research found that restorative practices reduced out-of-school suspensions by 18%, meaning students spent significantly more time in classrooms — which is, ultimately, the whole point of school. Notably, Black students experienced the greatest benefit from the program, suggesting restorative approaches can help reduce the racial disparities in discipline that have long plagued both district and charter schools.

A broader review of the literature confirms these results are not an anomaly. A synthesis of 34 studies found that restorative practices improve school climate, reduce suspensions, and build social-emotional skills. Crucially, schools that commit to restorative approaches over three or more years see the most marked improvements — which means the multi-year horizon matters. Restorative discipline requires cultural investment, not a one-semester pilot.

The Charter School Advantage: Culture, Staffing, and Scale

What makes charter schools particularly well-positioned to deliver on that multi-year commitment? Three interconnected factors: mission coherence, staffing intentionality, and community scale.

Mission coherence means the entire school — from the principal to the newest paraprofessional — shares an explicit set of values about how students should be treated and how conflict should be resolved. Restorative practices thrive in that environment because they depend on consistent adult behavior, not just consistent policy language in a handbook.

Staffing intentionality means charter schools can hire for cultural fit and train new staff in restorative approaches before the school year begins — not as a compliance exercise, but as a core part of professional identity. Restorative practices work best when they are embedded in a larger system of clear expectations and strong student-adult relationships. Charter schools can build that system deliberately, from day one.

Community scale means students and teachers often know each other far better than in large comprehensive high schools. Smaller campuses and tighter advisory structures make the relational repair at the heart of restorative work genuinely possible. A meaningful accountability circle requires real relationships — and those relationships don't form automatically in a school of 3,000 students where a student might see a different adult every period.

Freedom Prep, a tuition-free public charter school network in Utah serving students in grades PreK–12 across campuses in Provo, Vineyard, and St. George — as well as online statewide — represents the kind of intentionally structured environment where restorative discipline can take hold. A network with defined campuses and a unified charter mission has both the accountability levers and the community intimacy that make whole-school behavioral culture achievable in a way that sprawling district systems rarely can.

What Families Should Ask When Choosing a School

If school discipline philosophy matters to you as a parent — and it should, since a child's sense of safety and belonging directly affects their willingness to show up and engage — here are concrete questions to bring to any school tour or enrollment information night:

  • Does the school have a written restorative discipline policy, and can you see it?
  • What percentage of staff have received restorative practices training, and is it ongoing or a one-time event?
  • How does the school handle repeat behavioral incidents — is there a tiered response, or does it jump straight to suspension?
  • What is the out-of-school suspension rate, and how has it changed over the past three years?
  • Are students involved in the resolution process when conflicts arise, or are decisions made entirely by adults?

Schools that can answer these questions clearly — with data and specific examples — have made restorative discipline part of their culture. Schools that respond with vague reassurances probably haven't.

Discipline Culture Is Part of the School Choice Decision

School choice conversations tend to focus on academics: curriculum, test scores, college-placement rates. Discipline culture deserves equal weight. A school where students feel safe, respected, and genuinely known by adults is a school where learning happens — and where the chronic absenteeism, disengagement, and early dropout that plague so many schools start to lose their grip.

The shift toward restorative discipline is one of the more promising developments in K–12 education right now. Charter schools are, on balance, leading it — not because they are more virtuous, but because their structure gives them the freedom to actually do it. That distinction is worth understanding for any family navigating their options.

Restorative Practices in Charter Schools: What Works

# Restorative Practices in Charter Schools: What the Research Shows—and Where Schools Go Wrong

School discipline is one of the most contested topics in K-12 education right now, and restorative practices sit right at the center of that debate. A 2026 Education Next analysis found that charter schools use restorative approaches at a higher rate—approximately 72 percent—compared to 58 percent of district schools. But that statistic conceals an enormous variation in outcomes. In some schools, restorative practices have genuinely transformed climate and reduced racial disparities in discipline. In others, the approach has left teachers feeling unsupported and classrooms harder to manage.

The difference, almost always, comes down to how schools implement these methods—not whether they implement them at all.

What Restorative Practices Actually Are

Before evaluating restorative practices, it helps to be precise about what they encompass. Restorative practices are designed to build a strong sense of community in schools, to teach interpersonal skills, to repair harm when conflict occurs, and to proactively meet students' needs. Instead of punishment, they focus on developing communication strategies and building relationships. Core tools include classroom meetings, community-building circles, and conflict resolution strategies used by all adults and students, as well as mediation strategies to resolve disagreements and help students make amends.

That's a wide toolkit—and the breadth is part of what makes implementation so variable. A school that runs weekly community circles and trains every staff member to use them consistently is doing something fundamentally different from a school that holds a single restorative conversation after a serious incident and calls it "restorative justice."

What Does the Research Actually Show?

The short answer: Well-implemented restorative practices work. Poorly implemented ones don't—and can make things worse.

A large-scale study in California found that the more students experienced restorative practices, the more student behavior, student mental health, and disciplinary outcomes improved. The same research found that exposure to restorative practices improved school safety and academic outcomes for all groups of students, with stronger effects for Black and Latino/a students—suggesting that restorative practices can help narrow achievement gaps when they're done well.

Research on the Circle Forward restorative circles program in Boston Public Schools found positive effects on student attendance. A study of a restorative practices program in Pittsburgh Public Schools that included community-building circles found significant reductions in student suspensions and improved school climate perceptions.

On the other side of the ledger, a randomized controlled trial by the Center for Justice Innovation documented what happens when implementation falls short. Examining New York City's large-scale rollout, researchers found that implementation varied widely, training was inconsistent, and outcomes were difficult to measure—with no statistically significant differences in school climate, suspension rates, or academic outcomes between schools that did and did not implement restorative justice. The lesson from New York isn't that restorative practices don't work. It's that they require infrastructure, consistent training, and genuine accountability—none of which that rollout reliably provided.

The Implementation Trap: Why Schools Get This Wrong

In May 2026, researchers, students, educators, and advocates gathered at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg Center in Washington, D.C., to discuss how restorative practices can support students' social-emotional growth. One message remained clear across every panel: to build a strong and sustainable restorative practices system, school leaders must include student voices at every stage of the process.

That's a high bar that most rollouts haven't cleared. The most common failures follow a recognizable pattern:

  • Replacing consequences without building alternatives. Restorative conversations become the only response to serious misconduct, with no graduated continuum of supports.
  • Skipping infrastructure. Schools adopt the language of restorative practices without universal staff training standards, designated facilitation time, or consistent administrative backing.
  • No student voice in design. Programs are built for students rather than with them, reducing authenticity and buy-in.
  • Treating restorative practices as a one-time initiative. Research consistently emphasizes the importance of long-term investment in whole-school learning opportunities that build staff buy-in and skill—not one-off professional development days.

In districts like Pittsburgh and Oakland, inconsistent implementation and training—combined with few meaningful consequences for students who repeatedly disrupted learning—produced disappointing results. When restorative practices are layered on top of an accountability vacuum, they don't restore anything. They simply delay consequences indefinitely.

How Charter Schools Can Get It Right

The most important structural insight from a decade of evidence: restorative practices work best when they supplement clear behavioral expectations and consistent consequences—not when they replace them. Many charter schools layer restorative approaches on top of clear school rules and consequences instead of replacing them. That's the core lesson of the past ten years: restorative practices only have a chance to work when schools keep structure and adult authority firmly in place.

This is where mission-driven charter networks have a genuine advantage. Noble - Baker College Prep—part of Noble Schools, a network of 17 tuition-free, open-enrollment public charter schools in Chicago serving grades 6–12—operates under a mission to ensure that all students have equitable and positive school experiences that equip them to complete college and lead choice-filled lives. That kind of explicitly high-expectations culture provides exactly the stable behavioral foundation that restorative practices need in order to produce results. High standards and restorative approaches aren't in tension; well-run schools treat them as partners.

Here are the implementation elements that consistently appear in schools where restorative practices actually move the needle:

  1. Start proactively, not reactively. Community-building circles scheduled into the weekly routine—before any incident occurs—build the relational trust that makes conflict resolution meaningful when something does go wrong.
  2. Train everyone, consistently. Restorative practices require a shared language and shared facilitation skills across the entire staff. Inconsistent training produces inconsistent results.
  3. Center student voice from the start. Involve students in developing the norms, prompts, and structures that govern circles. Programs designed with students earn a legitimacy that top-down mandates never achieve.
  4. Maintain a full continuum of responses. Restorative conversations should be one option among many—not the only tool available regardless of the severity of a situation.
  5. Measure what actually matters. Track repeat incident rates, classroom removal trends, attendance, and student and teacher perceptions of safety—not just suspension counts, which are easy to suppress on paper without improving actual climate.

Why This Matters for Equity

Restorative practices became widespread in K-12 education in large part because exclusionary discipline—suspensions and expulsions—carries a well-documented racial disparity. The California Department of Education's analysis found that, across racial groups, students who had larger exposure to restorative practices saw less exposure to exclusionary discipline and better academic outcomes, and models suggested that expanding restorative practices could help bridge Black-White discipline disparities.

That equity promise is real. But it's conditional. It depends on schools doing the hard implementation work: sustained training, genuine student voice, administrative commitment, and a refusal to treat restorative approaches as a substitute for structure. When those conditions are met, the research makes a compelling case. When they're not, students in high-poverty schools often pay the steepest price—they're the ones who most need reliable structure, and the ones most harmed when that structure disappears.

The Bottom Line

Do restorative practices work in charter schools? Yes—when implemented with fidelity, staffed with proper training, centered on student voice, and paired with clear behavioral expectations rather than used as a replacement for them. The gap between schools where restorative practices succeed and schools where they fall flat is almost entirely an implementation gap, not an evidence gap.

For school leaders evaluating or expanding a restorative practices approach in the 2026–27 school year, the first question to ask isn't "Are we doing restorative practices?" It's "Are we doing them in a way that's actually built to work?"

The distinction is everything.

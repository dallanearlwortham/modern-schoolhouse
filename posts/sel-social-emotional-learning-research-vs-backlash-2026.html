SEL in 2026: The Research Schools Can't Ignore

Social-Emotional Learning Is Under Political Fire — But the Research Keeps Getting Stronger

Bills in at least eight states have sought to ban or limit social-emotional learning (SEL). At the federal level, the U.S. Department of Education under the Trump administration characterized SEL as something schools use to "veil discriminatory policies" — putting supporters of the practice on the defensive heading into the 2026-27 school year. Arizona's GOP-controlled legislature advanced a bill that would have repealed a pandemic-era law requiring schools to provide mental health instruction, including SEL.

And yet, in classrooms across the country, educators are quietly doing what they've always done: teaching kids how to manage their emotions, resolve conflict, work in teams, and keep going when things get hard.

The political noise is real. But so is the evidence. And right now, they're pulling in opposite directions.

---

What the Research Actually Shows About SEL and Academic Achievement

Here is the most direct answer to the question parents and school leaders most often ask: Does social-emotional learning actually help kids learn — or does it come at the expense of academics?

A large-scale analysis of 40 studies involving more than 33,700 students in grades 1 through 12, published in the Review of Educational Research by researchers from USC and Yale, found that students who participated in universal SEL programs performed better on academic outcomes — including standardized test scores and grades — than students who did not. Gains appeared in both literacy and math.

"Contrary to the perception that SEL is only about fostering students' social and emotional growth, our findings show these programs also make a measurable difference in academic achievement," said lead author Cheyeon Ha of the University of Southern California.

The strongest evidence was found at the elementary level. And one practical finding stood out for school leaders: duration matters. Programs lasting more than one semester — roughly four months or longer — were consistently linked to measurable academic gains. Shorter programs showed limited effects. SEL isn't a one-time assembly or a unit squeezed into advisory period. It works when it's sustained, structured, and woven into school culture over time.

---

The Political Backlash: Where It Comes From and What It Ignores

The criticism of SEL isn't new, but it has sharpened. During the pandemic, some conservative activists began framing SEL as a vehicle for progressive ideology — a way to smuggle discussions of race, identity, and social justice into classrooms under a clinical-sounding label. That narrative gained traction, and some schools responded by quietly rebranding their SEL work under different language while keeping the practices intact.

In 2026, the federal pressure has added a new dimension: the conflation of SEL with DEI. The argument, advanced by both the Department of Education and prominent conservative advocacy groups, is that SEL frameworks disproportionately center particular cultural or political perspectives. Critics want SEL stripped from schools entirely or at minimum subjected to parental opt-in requirements.

What those arguments consistently ignore is educator experience on the ground. According to surveys conducted by the EdWeek Research Center across 2022, 2024, and late 2025 into early 2026, educators' views toward SEL's effectiveness have remained steadily positive — with roughly half rating its impact on students' academic outcomes and social skills as "somewhat positive," and that number barely moving across four years of political turbulence.

Michael Petrilli, president of the Thomas B. Fordham Institute, captured the disconnect bluntly: "The stability is the main takeaway here, and also that some of the debates that we have inside of our little education policy bubble about things like SEL may have been overblown."

Fewer than one in ten educators surveyed told EdWeek Research Center they expect their school or district to de-emphasize SEL in the coming years. About half expect it to be emphasized more.

---

Where SEL Implementation Actually Breaks Down

The research case for SEL is strong, but it is not unconditional. Implementation quality is the decisive variable — and this is where many schools stumble.

Jerome Graham, an assistant professor of K-12 educational administration at Michigan State University, identified several failure modes that explain why SEL sometimes underdelivers:

  • Resource fade: Enthusiasm and funding for new initiatives of any kind tend to wane a few years after launch, as leadership changes and professional development dries up.
  • Teacher modeling gap: If teachers aren't actively practicing and reflecting on their own social-emotional skills, students are less likely to develop theirs — no matter what curriculum is on the shelf.
  • Unrealistic expectations: Some educators reach for SEL as a behavior management shortcut, then feel let down when it doesn't produce instant results.
  • Bandwidth squeeze: In 2026, catching students up academically still consumes enormous energy in many buildings, leaving less room for deliberate SEL instruction.

Among educators surveyed, common barriers included students' social-emotional needs exceeding what educators feel equipped to handle, difficulty integrating SEL into academic subjects, and insufficient professional development. These are solvable problems — but they require school-level structural commitment, not just a purchased program.

---

The Schools Getting It Right: What They Have in Common

Schools where SEL produces consistent, measurable results tend to share a set of design principles that go beyond any single curriculum:

  • Sustained, school-wide programming embedded across the full academic year, not periodic add-ons
  • Adult development first — investment in teachers' own social-emotional competencies before asking them to teach those skills to students
  • Cultural grounding — SEL practices connected to students' actual identities and community values, not generic scripts
  • Whole-child infrastructure — wrapping academic support with health, safety, and family stability so students' basic needs aren't competing with their capacity to learn

That last principle is where SEL stops being a program and starts being a school philosophy. BRICK Buffalo, a tuition-free public charter school serving grades K–3 in Buffalo, NY — and growing toward a full K–8 model — grounds its entire approach in Ubuntu, the African philosophical principle of collective care and interconnectedness. The school treats a child's social and emotional development not as a supplemental priority but as foundational to everything else: free after-school care, free transportation, and holistic family wraparound services are built into the model so that students arrive at school ready to learn, rather than managing unmet needs on their own.

---

What Families Should Ask When Evaluating Schools

If you're choosing a school for your child in 2026's expanded school-choice landscape, the SEL debate doesn't have to be confusing. Cut through the political noise with these practical questions:

  • Does the school explicitly teach emotional regulation, empathy, and conflict resolution — or treat those as someone else's job?
  • Are teachers trained and supported to model social-emotional skills themselves, not just deliver a boxed curriculum?
  • Is the approach sustained across the full school year, or is it a one-semester elective or occasional workshop?
  • Is SEL culturally grounded in who your child actually is — or is it a one-size-fits-all framework that doesn't speak to your family's experience?
  • Does the school's structure support the whole child — through safety, health access, extended learning time, and family engagement?

The answers to those questions will tell you far more about whether a school genuinely prioritizes social-emotional learning than any policy statement or brochure.

---

The Bottom Line

Social-emotional learning is neither a silver bullet nor a Trojan horse. It is a well-researched, evidence-backed set of practices that, when implemented with fidelity and sustained over time, measurably improve both academic achievement and student well-being. The political attacks on SEL in 2026 are loud — but they have not moved educators' confidence in the practice, and they have not overturned the research.

About 40 percent of elementary school teachers and principals say their personal support for SEL has increased significantly over the past year alone. That number is worth more than any statehouse bill.

The children sitting in classrooms in 2026 still need to feel safe, known, and emotionally regulated before they can do their best thinking. Schools that build around that reality — not despite political pressure, but because the evidence demands it — will keep producing the outcomes families are looking for.

---

Whatever label a legislature puts on it, the skills at the heart of SEL — self-awareness, empathy, perseverance, the ability to collaborate — are the same skills children have always needed to succeed. The schools that teach them well aren't doing politics. They're doing their jobs.

Phone-Free Schools: What the 2025 Research Shows

# Phone-Free Schools Are the Norm Now—Here's What the Research Actually Shows

Walk into almost any K-12 school in America today and you're increasingly likely to see a Yondr pouch, a phone cubby, or a posted policy that says devices stay away until the final bell. This isn't a fringe experiment anymore. It's one of the fastest-moving education policy shifts of the decade—and the 2025 research on what it actually does for students is finally rich enough to take seriously.

How Quickly the Policy Landscape Changed

The pace of change has been remarkable. As of 2025, 26 states have implemented a complete, bell-to-bell ban on phones in schools. Count in states with partial restrictions and the number climbs to more than 30 states plus Washington D.C. that have adopted some form of phone-restriction policy in K-12 public schools.

The reasons driving this aren't hard to find. More than half of public school leaders—53 percent—report that their students' academic performance has been negatively impacted by cell phone usage, according to data from the National Center for Education Statistics. More than two-thirds say phones have had a negative impact on their students' mental health (72 percent) and attention span (73 percent).

States across the ideological spectrum have moved in the same direction. Alabama, Arkansas, Nebraska, North Dakota, Oklahoma, and West Virginia all passed legislation in 2025 requiring schools to have policies that limit access to smartphones, with those policies going into effect in the 2025–2026 school year. Oregon's governor signed an executive order mandating that every district adopt a prohibition on student phone use during the school day. California adopted the Phone-Free School Act, requiring every school district, charter school, and county office of education to develop a limiting policy by July 1, 2026. Colorado went further, requiring charter schools specifically to develop their own restricting policies under HB 25-1135.

What Does the Research Say About Academic Outcomes?

This is where it gets nuanced—and where honest reporting matters more than headlines.

The most promising findings come from Florida, where a statewide instructional-time ban gave researchers a large, clean natural experiment. A 2025 NBER study by economists David Figlio and Umut Özek found that an all-day cell phone ban within a large Florida school district improved student test scores, with gains particularly pronounced for male students and in middle and high schools. Crucially, unexcused absences dropped significantly under the ban—and that attendance improvement explained a substantial portion of the academic gains. A separate January 2026 review by the Paragon Institute synthesized global evidence and concluded that bans reliably enhance achievement for disadvantaged students, with especially strong effects for low-income learners.

The broader picture is more mixed. A large multi-school NBER study examining data from more than 41,000 schools found that the average effect on test scores was "consistently close to zero," and that bans did not significantly affect attendance or perceptions of online bullying across all school types. Duke University economist E. Jason Baron, one of that study's authors, noted plainly that "it's hard to move outcomes like test scores."

What that study did find moving, consistently, was the thing teachers experience minute to minute: classroom behavior and engagement. Most teachers—78 percent—supported phone-restriction policies, with 62 percent reporting improved student behavior. In focus groups, teachers described increased student engagement and stronger peer interaction as a direct result of reduced phone distractions.

The Real Benefit May Be Social, Not Just Academic

Here is a direct answer to one of the most common questions parents and educators are searching for right now: Does banning phones in school actually help students focus and connect with each other? Based on 2025 evidence, the answer for most schools is yes—particularly on the social dimension.

Research shows that constant access to personal electronic devices and social media can lead to reduced focus, increased anxiety and depression, and weakened peer connections. When devices disappear from the school day, students are more present with each other. Teachers report more hallway conversation, more eye contact in class, and more genuine collaboration on group tasks. These aren't soft or trivial outcomes. They are the building blocks of belonging—and belonging is one of the most robust predictors of student engagement and long-term academic persistence that education research has produced.

Why School Culture Makes or Breaks Phone Policy

A phone-free rule posted on a wall is very different from a phone-free school. The distinction matters enormously, and it's where implementation research is most instructive.

Schools where phone policies succeed tend to share a few characteristics:

  • A clear, communicated mission that frames the policy as part of a larger commitment to student growth—not just a compliance requirement
  • Consistent staff buy-in at every level, so the expectation is modeled and enforced uniformly across classrooms, hallways, and common areas
  • Student understanding of the why, not just the what—schools that explain the rationale see better voluntary compliance and fewer daily conflicts
  • Structured alternatives at unstructured times, so that removing devices at lunch and during transitions doesn't create a behavioral vacuum

This is where schools with a strong, distinctive identity have a real structural advantage over schools that simply adopt a policy because the state said so. A school that already asks students to be active problem-solvers, collaborators, and community contributors has a ready-made frame for a phone-free environment. The policy fits the mission rather than sitting awkwardly alongside it.

Charter School of the Dunes, a tuition-free PreK–8 public charter school in Gary, Indiana, is a concrete example of this kind of mission-anchored environment. The school's stated purpose is to cultivate students who can solve problems, set goals, accept responsibility, and become active partners in their community—a whole-child orientation that naturally reinforces the kind of focused, present, device-intentional school day that phone-free research consistently rewards.

What Families Should Ask When Evaluating a School's Phone Policy

For parents currently comparing schools, a school's phone policy turns out to be a surprisingly useful diagnostic. The right questions go beyond "do they allow phones?" and into how the policy is built and lived:

  • Is the policy bell-to-bell, or only during class time? Research suggests all-day restrictions produce stronger effects, particularly in middle school grades.
  • How is it enforced, and how consistently? Uniform enforcement across all staff matters far more than the strictness of the written rule.
  • How does the school explain it to students? Culture-based rationales produce better outcomes than compliance-only framing.
  • What do students do at lunch and passing periods? The social gains from phone-free environments depend substantially on what fills that space—and whether the school actively supports real peer interaction in those windows.

The Bottom Line

Phone-free school policies are now the majority practice across the United States, and the 2025 research gives families and educators a more complete picture than ever before of what they actually accomplish. Academic gains are real, but they are most reliable among low-income learners, younger students, and in schools where the policy is genuinely embedded in school culture rather than grafted onto it as an afterthought. Social and behavioral gains are more consistent across school types: more engaged classrooms, stronger peer relationships, and more of the human attention that learning has always depended on.

A phone policy alone doesn't make a school exceptional. But in a school that already leads with mission, culture, and a clear vision of what students should become, removing devices from the school day can be a powerful accelerant—clearing space for exactly the kind of focus and human connection that meaningful learning has always required.

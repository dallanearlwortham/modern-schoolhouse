The Public Homeschool: Why Charter Schools Beat Going It Alone

# The Public Homeschool: Why More Families Are Choosing Charter Schools Over Going It Alone

There's a version of homeschooling where you get to stay home, choose your curriculum, and set your own daily schedule — and also have a credentialed teacher in your corner, a tuition-free budget for materials, and a fully accredited diploma waiting at the finish line. It's not a loophole. It's one of the fastest-growing models in American K–12 education, and millions of families still don't know it exists.

The independent study charter school — increasingly called the "public homeschool" model — is quietly rewriting what school choice actually means.

Homeschooling Is Surging. But So Are Its Limits.

In the 2024–2025 school year, homeschooling grew across the United States at an average rate of 5.4% — nearly triple the pre-pandemic growth rate of around 2%. Notably, 36% of reporting states recorded their highest homeschool enrollment numbers ever, exceeding even the peaks reached during the pandemic.

That growth reflects something real: families want more control over how and where their children learn. But private homeschooling carries a steep set of trade-offs. Parents must act as teacher, project manager, counselor, and curriculum director — entirely out of pocket, without a credentialed safety net, and without access to the institutional resources most students take for granted. For families of children with disabilities, the gap is even sharper: private homeschool students simply do not have a guaranteed right to special education services.

That's the gap independent study charter schools were built to fill.

What the Independent Study Charter Model Actually Is

In this model, a parent or guardian serves as the primary teacher at home, guiding their child through a curriculum selected to match the student's learning style and pace. A credentialed Teacher of Record (ToR), employed by the charter school, oversees the student's education — reviewing work samples, meeting regularly with the family, helping navigate curriculum decisions, and ensuring the student stays on track toward state graduation requirements.

Research into California's nonclassroom-based charter schools found that participating schools were structured to support strong parent involvement in the selection of curriculum and pedagogy families would use to educate their child at home. That's the defining distinction from both traditional public school and private homeschooling: the family makes meaningful educational decisions, while a certified professional provides accountability, expertise, and ongoing support.

California's independent study framework requires Written Learning Agreements signed by families, students, and supervising teachers, along with regular work-sample collection and compliance with the same state assessments every other public school student takes. LEAs are explicitly encouraged to offer multiple independent study models, supporting both improved academic outcomes and enrollment flexibility.

What Families Gain That Private Homeschooling Can't Match

No tuition. Real instructional funding. In states like California, families can access $2,200 to $3,800 or more annually in instructional funds through charter school independent study programs. That money pays for curriculum packages, materials, technology, enrichment courses, and field trips — costs that private homeschooling families absorb entirely on their own, often spending thousands of dollars before the school year even begins.

A credentialed partner, not just paperwork. The Teacher of Record is the relationship that makes the model sustainable. Parents who feel genuinely supported by a professional — rather than left to navigate everything alone — are less likely to burn out, and students whose progress is actively monitored are more likely to stay on track. The independent study option is especially valuable for students who are struggling in a traditional setting or who need a more flexible schedule but still benefit from expert guidance.

IEP access and full special education services. This is one of the least-discussed but most consequential differences between private homeschooling and the public charter model. Students who enroll in charter schools, including public charter homeschool programs, are entitled to receive the same type of special education they would receive in any public school — including access to services written into their Individualized Education Program (IEP). Private homeschool families have no such guarantee. For families of children with learning differences, dyslexia, ADHD, or other disabilities, this legal protection is often the single deciding factor in choosing a charter-based independent study program over filing a private school affidavit.

An accredited diploma that opens real doors. Students in independent study charter schools graduate with official, accredited transcripts. Many schools layer in AP courses, honors tracks, and concurrent community college enrollment — credentials that translate directly into college admissions outcomes that a private homeschool portfolio simply cannot replicate on its own.

Who This Model Is Actually Built to Serve

Nonclassroom-based charter public schools have developed flexible programming structures that allow for a high degree of customization to the individual needs of students. In practice, that means a remarkably wide student population finds its way to this model: children managing chronic illness or frequent medical appointments, competitive athletes with demanding training schedules, performing artists, military-connected families who relocate often, students with sensory or anxiety-related needs who thrive outside a traditional classroom, and kids who simply learn faster — or slower — than a standard bell schedule allows.

Research from the California Charter Schools Association found that flex-based charter schools are defined by the wide variety of instructional delivery methods and program specialties they offer — and that this growth has allowed families to consider not just academic quality, but the specific program that best fits their child's individual needs. Two-thirds of flex-based schools use hybrid models that include in-person learning, while others offer fully virtual programs.

Excel Academy Charter School, a tuition-free TK–12 charter school serving families across six counties in California, illustrates how a single school can hold this full range of learners together without sacrificing rigor or community. Families choose between a Personalized path — where the parent serves as primary teacher alongside a credentialed Teacher of Record who oversees the student's academic program — and a Virtual Path that delivers live online classroom instruction five days a week. Beyond core academics, Excel Academy offers community enrichment programs, field trips, clubs, 35 AP and honors courses, and concurrent college enrollment options. The school's structure is built on the conviction that home-based learning and serious academic preparation aren't in conflict — they're complementary.

Is This "Real School"? What the Accountability Record Shows

The skeptic's question deserves a direct answer: can a parent-led, home-based education meet the same standard as a traditional classroom? The enrollment data and academic evidence both say yes.

While California's overall public school population is declining, charter school enrollment has risen to nearly 728,000 students — 12.5% of all public school students — across 1,280 campuses and independent study programs statewide. That is not a niche alternative or a pandemic-era workaround. It is a mainstream educational choice made by nearly one in eight California public school students.

Academic research consistently finds that homeschooled students perform equal to or better than their conventionally schooled peers on measures of achievement and socioemotional functioning. When that home-based learning is wrapped in public school accountability structures — credentialed oversight, state testing requirements, accreditation, and legally binding learning agreements — families get outcomes they can document and transcripts that colleges will accept without hesitation.

Is the Model Right for Every Family?

Honestly, no — and any school operating with integrity will tell you that upfront during enrollment. Independent study charter school success depends on a parent or guardian who is consistently available and genuinely committed to the instructional role. Planning lessons, tracking progress, submitting work samples, and coordinating with a Teacher of Record requires real time and real follow-through. Families where both parents work full-time with no schedule flexibility may find the model difficult to sustain, and that's a legitimate constraint worth acknowledging.

But for families who can commit to the partnership, the public homeschool model offers something genuinely rare in K–12 education today: a structure that treats the family as the expert on their own child, wraps that expertise in professional accountability, and delivers a fully accredited result at the end. The curriculum can flex. The Teacher of Record is real. The diploma counts everywhere.

That combination — family authority plus institutional backing — is why this model isn't just trending. It's growing roots.

High-Dosage Tutoring: The Promise vs. Reality

# High-Dosage Tutoring: What the Research Actually Shows in 2026

Few education interventions have ever earned the kind of bipartisan enthusiasm that high-dosage tutoring did in the early 2020s. After COVID-era learning loss left millions of students behind, researchers, policymakers, and journalists — armed with a compelling meta-analysis showing average gains of 0.37 standard deviations across 96 randomized trials — declared tutoring the answer. Federal relief funds made the investment possible. Schools across the country stood up programs almost overnight.

Now, in 2026, we have enough post-pandemic data to ask the honest question: did it work? The answer is more complicated — and more instructive — than either the boosters or the critics want to admit.

---

What the Original Research Said

The case for high-dosage tutoring rested on a substantial body of evidence. The landmark 2020 meta-analysis by Nickow, Oreopoulos, and Quan pooled 96 randomized trials of tutoring from preschool through high school and found an average effect size of 0.37 standard deviations. To put that in human terms: a student at the 50th percentile who received high-dosage tutoring could expect to move to roughly the 65th percentile. Over three years, some projections suggested students could gain the equivalent of a full additional year of learning.

The breakdown by design features was equally compelling:

  • Tutoring during the school day produced an effect of 0.40 SD; after school dropped to 0.21
  • Three sessions per week produced gains of 0.34 SD; four or five per week reached 0.41
  • Teacher tutors outperformed volunteers; certified instructors averaged 0.50 SD
  • Math and reading showed similar overall gains (0.38 and 0.35 respectively), though reading tutoring worked best in the earliest grades

The best single-program evidence came from Saga Education's work in Chicago public high schools. In two large randomized trials covering more than 2,600 students each, Saga — which placed a tutor with two students for one class period every school day — produced math gains of 0.16 to 0.37 standard deviations. The cost ran $3,500 to $4,300 per student per year, but the evidence was rigorous and the results were real.

---

What Happened When It Scaled

So why did high-dosage tutoring fall short in the pandemic recovery era? The honest answer is that most post-pandemic tutoring programs were not actually high-dosage.

Researcher Matthew Kraft at Brown University and colleagues re-analyzed the evidence base and found a striking pattern across 265 randomized trials: effect sizes declined sharply as program size grew. Programs tutoring fewer than 100 students averaged 0.55 standard deviations. Programs serving 1,000 or more students averaged 0.14. The implication was sobering — the gold-standard results that made tutoring famous were generated under conditions that most school systems would never replicate at scale.

The University of Chicago Education Lab's Personalized Learning Initiative — a randomized trial across eight school systems involving 17,330 students in 2023-24 — put the effect at 0.06 to 0.09 standard deviations, roughly one to two months of additional learning. In Nashville, a district program that delivered over 125,000 hours of tutoring to more than 6,800 students produced reading gains of 0.04 to 0.09 SD and null results in math.

The problem was not the intervention. It was the dosage.

The original Saga trials gave students 34 to 82 hours of tutoring over the course of a year. The Education Lab's post-pandemic programs delivered an average of 17 hours — less than half — primarily because schools "felt they simply had too many competing demands on limited instructional time." Nashville designed for 90 minutes per week and regularly fell short. Virtual sessions lost further minutes to recurring technology problems: students scrambling to find working headphones or a laptop charger, often starting a 30-minute session already 10 minutes late.

Meanwhile, the market for tutoring services flooded with providers branding themselves "high-dosage" while offering little more than on-demand homework help. These products were often remote, voluntary, and disconnected from classroom instruction. Students rarely showed up consistently, and when they did, the experience bore little resemblance to the structured, school-day sessions that produced the Chicago results.

---

The Lessons from Programs That Actually Worked

The most important finding from recent research is not that tutoring failed. It is that implementation fidelity is the whole ballgame.

The University of Chicago Education Lab's main analytical conclusion is striking: "the student learning per minute of tutoring is consistent across sites and studies." The slope of learning is the same whether a program costs $1,200 or $2,000 per student, whether tutors are in the room or virtual. The variable that actually predicts outcomes is total instructional minutes delivered — and most programs simply did not deliver enough of them.

The programs that produced meaningful results shared a consistent set of design principles:

  1. Tutoring was scheduled during the school day. Voluntary after-school sessions have a participation rate problem that no amount of outreach can fully overcome. When a session is on the bell schedule, it happens. The New Mexico experience illustrates this starkly: after-school and weekend tutoring drew just 1.5% of eligible students; once the state moved tutoring inside the school day, participation rates increased dramatically.
  1. Attendance and participation were tracked daily. Washington, D.C.'s high-impact tutoring initiative — described by the Harvard Center for Education Policy Research as a model of implementation — built a research and evaluation framework from day one, using student "pulse check" surveys throughout the program. Early results showed that tutored students exceeded their expected learning growth by an additional 44% and also attended several more days of school than peers who were not tutored. That attendance gain was not an accident; it was a byproduct of the relationship students built with consistent tutors.
  1. Tutors were trained, consistent, and held accountable for outcomes. Florida's statewide tutoring program, administered through the University of Florida Lastinger Center, used outcomes-based contracts in which tutoring providers received 60% of their payment upfront and the remaining 40% only upon demonstrating student learning results. That structure changes the incentives entirely.
  1. Programs were connected to classroom curriculum. Tutoring that reinforces what students are learning in their regular classes produces compounding effects. Tutoring that operates in isolation functions more like remediation — better than nothing, but far less powerful than it could be.

---

A Note on AI Tutoring

No review of tutoring in 2026 is complete without addressing AI. The results so far are more grounded than the marketing suggests.

The most encouraging AI-related finding came from the Tutor CoPilot trial, in which a language model provided real-time suggestions to human tutors working with students in grades 3 through 8. Students whose tutors used the tool passed their end-of-lesson checks 66% of the time, compared to 62% for students with unaided tutors — and the gains were largest for the lowest-rated tutors, suggesting AI could raise a floor. However, the trial found no significant gain on end-of-year math tests.

A two-year randomized trial of Khanmigo, Khan Academy's AI tutor, found gains of 0.06 to 0.08 standard deviations per year — effects the researchers described as resembling Khan Academy practice without the AI component. AI tutoring, at present, appears to perform comparably to unassisted adaptive software. It is a reasonable supplement and may prove more with longer time horizons, but it is not a replacement for a trained human tutor delivering consistent, in-school sessions.

---

What Schools Running Intentionally Diverse Programs Should Know

The tutoring evidence matters specifically for schools that have committed to serving a broad, diverse student population — the kind of schools where the students who most need academic acceleration are the same ones most likely to be missed by voluntary or after-school approaches.

This is a tension that organizations working at the intersection of school quality and equity have to take seriously. The Diverse Charter Schools Coalition, a national coalition of more than 100 intentionally diverse public charter schools whose stated mission is to "catalyze and support high-quality, intentionally diverse public charter schools through convening their members, training leaders and advocating for policies that allow diverse schools to thrive," runs Communities of Practice and leadership development programs through its UnifiED initiative — exactly the kind of network where tutoring implementation knowledge needs to circulate. Schools committed to producing strong outcomes for every student in a deliberately mixed community cannot afford to run tutoring programs that exist on paper but underdeliver on minutes.

The research on diverse-by-design schools consistently shows lower chronic absenteeism for Black and Hispanic students — which means higher baseline attendance to build tutoring schedules around. That is an underappreciated structural advantage. Schools that have already solved a piece of the engagement puzzle are better positioned to convert tutoring investment into actual instructional time.

---

The Bottom Line on High-Dosage Tutoring

High-dosage tutoring works when it is actually high-dosage. That is the clearest summary the 2026 evidence allows. An average of 17 hours of tutoring in a school year is not high-dosage. After-school and voluntary programs are not high-dosage. Homework help platforms branded as tutoring are not high-dosage.

When programs are built around daily or near-daily in-school sessions, trained tutors, data tracking, and curriculum alignment, the results look more like the Chicago trials than the pandemic-era disappointments. Brown University's Matthew Kraft, who has spent years studying and advocating for tutoring, put it plainly: the vision of high-dosage tutoring points toward the right North Star. The mistake was overselling the effect sizes while underselling the implementation demands.

Schools that go into tutoring with open eyes — building the scheduling infrastructure first, tracking minutes religiously, and linking tutor performance to outcomes — are not chasing a failed fad. They are doing one of the most evidence-backed things a school can do for students who are behind. The fad was never the tutoring. It was the idea that it would be easy.

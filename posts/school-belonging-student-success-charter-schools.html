Why Belonging Is the Missing Metric in School Success

The Research Is In: Belonging Isn't Soft—It's a Performance Metric

Ask any experienced educator what separates a school that hums from one that struggles, and you'll rarely hear "test scores" as the answer. More often you'll hear something like: kids want to be here. That gut-level observation now has a growing body of hard evidence behind it—and in 2026, the research on school belonging has moved from the margins of educational theory to the center of a serious conversation about student outcomes.

A comprehensive literature review published this year by researchers at Monash University for the International Baccalaureate found that school belonging is "fundamental to educational success and equity," consistently associated with better mental health, wellbeing, and learning outcomes across K–12 settings. The conclusion isn't subtle: when students feel like they genuinely belong in a school, better academic results tend to follow.

A 2026 peer-reviewed study published in BMC Psychology reinforced this, finding that belonging has been "consistently associated with positive outcomes, including academic motivation, engagement, persistence, and psychological well-being." Conversely, when students feel marginalized or excluded, academic performance and attendance suffer measurably.

For charter school leaders—who often operate with lean staffing, ambitious academic targets, and student populations that carry significant out-of-school stressors—this research isn't just interesting. It's urgent.

What Chronic Absenteeism Has to Do With It

Belonging and attendance are tightly linked, and the attendance picture in American schools remains alarming. Chronic absenteeism—students missing 10% or more of school days—nearly doubled from roughly 15% in 2019 to 28–31% in 2022, according to research cited by CALDER, and remained elevated through recent school years. The U.S. Department of Education put the 2023 national chronic absenteeism rate at 28%.

What drives students away? A study of over 24,000 students in a predominantly low-income minority school district found that physical safety concerns were significant—but so were emotional disconnection and lack of school support. A Fordham Institute analysis found that "positive perceptions of the school environment—not only physical safety, but also clear rules, a sense of belonging, and positive parent-teacher relations—were associated with lower rates of absenteeism."

Students who feel like they belong show up. And students who show up learn.

A RAND-sponsored study found that while 93% of districts tried at least one approach to combat chronic absenteeism in the 2023–24 school year, roughly a quarter of district leaders reported that none of their efforts had been particularly effective. Those same leaders speculated that reversing the trend would require "new approaches to make schools more engaging." Belonging, it turns out, may be exactly that approach.

The Myth That Belonging Means Lowering the Bar

One of the most persistent misconceptions in this conversation is that prioritizing belonging means softening academic expectations. A May 2026 statement from the Oxford University Department of Education addressed this directly, noting the need to correct the idea that prioritizing belonging "implies lower expectations for behaviour or academic achievement, when in practice these approaches aim to create the relational conditions that support effective engagement, wellbeing, and learning."

That framing matters enormously for charter schools that have built their identities around high expectations and rigorous college-preparatory cultures. Demanding excellence and building belonging are not in tension—they're synergistic. A student who feels genuinely seen and supported is more likely to rise to a school's expectations, not less.

What Builds Belonging in Practice

The 2026 BMC Psychology study found that "opportunities for student voice, dialogue, and choice positioned students as active contributors within classroom learning communities." This aligns with Self-Determination Theory, which holds that autonomy-supportive environments strengthen intrinsic motivation and connection to school.

In concrete terms, belonging-building practices include:

  • Advisor or mentor relationships that give every student a consistent adult advocate
  • Student voice structures like advisory periods, student councils, or regular feedback loops where students shape school norms
  • Culturally affirming curricula that reflect students' lived experiences and identities
  • Restorative rather than purely punitive discipline that preserves relationships rather than severing them
  • Extracurricular pathways that connect students to peers, passions, and purpose beyond the classroom
  • Proactive outreach when students begin to disengage—before absenteeism becomes chronic

These aren't add-ons to a rigorous academic program. Done well, they're the scaffolding that makes rigor achievable.

How High-Expectations Charter Schools Model the Balance

The convergence of high academic standards and a strong culture of belonging is something the best urban charter networks have been working on deliberately for years. Noble - Baker College Prep, one of 17 open-enrollment, tuition-free charter campuses in Chicago's Noble Schools network—serving more than 12,000 students across the city—grounds its work in an explicit commitment to ensuring "all students have equitable and positive school experiences that equip them to complete college and lead choice-filled lives." That language is intentional: positive school experiences and college preparation aren't competing priorities; they're presented as inseparable. For a network serving predominantly low-income students and students of color in a major urban district, building belonging isn't incidental to the mission—it is the mission.

That dual emphasis—high expectations held alongside a genuine investment in students' daily experience of school—is a model worth examining across the charter sector.

What Schools Get Wrong About Belonging

Building belonging can't be reduced to a single initiative or a one-time professional development day. Common missteps include:

  • Treating belonging as a survey metric rather than an ongoing instructional and relational practice
  • Conflating belonging with happiness, missing that genuine belonging means students feel valued and accepted for who they are—not just comfortable
  • Implementing belonging programs in isolation from discipline policies that undermine trust, such as zero-tolerance practices that disproportionately exclude marginalized students
  • Ignoring identity: research consistently shows that students from historically marginalized groups need schools that affirm their identities, not merely tolerate them

The Oxford 2026 statement also emphasizes that belonging requires consistent reinforcement at the leadership level and cannot be mandated top-down without the relational infrastructure to support it.

The Bottom Line for Charter School Leaders

If your school is trying to move the needle on attendance, engagement, or graduation rates—and most charter schools are—belonging deserves a place in your improvement strategy alongside instructional coaching, tutoring, and curriculum alignment.

What does the research say about school belonging and student outcomes? Students who report a strong sense of belonging attend more consistently, engage more deeply with academic content, and persist through difficulty with greater resilience. The effect holds across grade levels and is especially pronounced for students from low-income backgrounds and historically underserved communities.

For charter schools serving students who have too often been failed by disconnected institutions, building genuine belonging isn't a luxury. It's a core competency—and in 2026, the evidence base to justify that investment has never been stronger.

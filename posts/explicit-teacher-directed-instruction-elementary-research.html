Why How You Teach Matters as Much as What You Teach

# Why How You Teach Matters as Much as What You Teach

Reading scores are still falling — and the usual explanations aren't cutting it. In 2024, average reading scores on The Nation's Report Card declined by 2 points for both 4th and 8th grade students compared to 2022, steepening a 3-point decline already seen between 2019 and 2022. Fewer than a third of students nationwide are now working at the NAEP Proficient level in reading at both grades, and around 40 percent of 4th graders are performing below the NAEP Basic level — the largest percentage since 2002.

State legislatures have passed science-of-reading mandates. Districts have adopted new curriculum materials. Billions in pandemic recovery funds have been spent. And yet something stubborn persists: in classrooms across the country, how content gets delivered — the actual instructional model a teacher uses each day — remains deeply inconsistent and almost entirely underexamined by parents choosing a school. That gap is worth closing.

What "Explicit, Teacher-Directed Instruction" Actually Means

Explicit instruction is not lecturing at passive students. It is a structured, sequenced approach in which teachers clearly model new concepts, check for understanding at each step, and guide students through scaffolded practice before releasing responsibility. It is systematic and intentional — the deliberate opposite of hoping children will construct understanding on their own through open-ended discovery.

Research has been validating this model for decades. Direct Instruction was only one of two models for elementary and middle schools that received a "strong" rating for evidence of positive effects on student achievement in a major review of 24 schoolwide reform models sponsored by five national associations of educators. More recently, education researcher John Hattie's large-scale synthesis of learning studies identified direct instruction as carrying an effect size of 0.59, and teacher clarity at an even higher 0.85 — both well above the 0.4 "hinge point" considered meaningfully impactful on student outcomes.

In practice, high-quality explicit instruction consistently includes:

  • Clear learning objectives stated at the start of every lesson so students know exactly what they are working toward
  • Teacher modeling — demonstrating the skill or concept in full before students attempt it independently
  • Guided practice with immediate, specific, corrective feedback while misconceptions are still fresh
  • Sequential skill-building, where each lesson scaffolds deliberately on what came before
  • Frequent comprehension checks built into the lesson itself, rather than waiting for a unit test to surface confusion weeks later

None of this is glamorous. But the evidence says it works — and works substantially better than the alternatives, especially at the elementary level where foundational knowledge is still being constructed.

Why the "What" and the "How" Have to Work Together

The science of reading movement is widely understood as a phonics story. It is more than that. Research increasingly points to another critical dimension of literacy: the role of background knowledge. Prior research has found that a young child's knowledge of the social and physical world is a strong predictor of academic success in elementary school — meaning that a classroom can get phonics right and still leave enormous gains on the table if it isn't also building content knowledge in a structured, cumulative way.

A landmark 2024 causal study published in Education Next makes this connection impossible to ignore. Researchers examined nine Colorado charter schools using the Core Knowledge curriculum through randomized kindergarten-enrollment lotteries — the gold standard for isolating cause and effect. They found that winning an enrollment lottery and enrolling in a Core Knowledge charter school boosted long-term reading achievement in grades 3–6 by 16 percentile points compared to comparable applicants who did not win their lottery. The researchers described that gain as roughly equivalent to the difference between average U.S. student performance and that of top-scoring countries like Singapore and Finland on international reading assessments.

The researchers drew a direct conclusion about delivery: the "procedural skills" approach that has dominated reading comprehension instruction over the last 30 years is less effective than a "knowledge-based" approach that teaches skills while also systematically building a body of content as the main engine for increasing comprehension. Rich curriculum and structured delivery are not separate levers — they are the same lever, and they only move outcomes when pulled together. The study also found that the intervention appears to set off a long-term compounding process: improved comprehension leads to increased knowledge, and increased knowledge leads to even better comprehension the following year and every year after.

That compounding effect makes the early elementary years the highest-leverage window in a child's education.

The Multisensory Layer

One refinement to explicit instruction drawing increasing research attention is the use of multisensory techniques — engaging visual, auditory, and kinesthetic channels simultaneously rather than relying on a single mode of delivery. A 2025 study investigated how multisensory learning approaches affected the phonemic awareness and reading fluency of first-grade learners. Initial results revealed that 67 percent of learners were already performing at an "Excellent" level before the intervention. Following the multisensory instructional intervention, 100 percent of learners received an "Excellent" rating — a meaningful jump even from an already-strong baseline.

What does this look like in a real K-6 classroom? Students might physically trace letters while simultaneously saying sounds aloud, use hands-on manipulatives to work through a math concept, or build a historical timeline using images and primary source artifacts rather than reading a textbook paragraph in isolation. The critical thread running through all of it is that the teacher directs the activity with clear purpose and sequenced intentionality. This is structured engagement, not free-form exploration.

Utah's Jefferson Academy is a concrete example of a school that has built its entire instructional identity around exactly this alignment. The Kaysville tuition-free public charter school, serving students in grades K–6, describes its instruction as explicit, sequential, and teacher-directed, delivered through a multisensory approach — all housed within a history-centered, content-rich curriculum framework built on the Core Knowledge Sequence. It is the kind of coherent combination — rigorous content, structured delivery, and multisensory reinforcement working in concert — that the research literature increasingly identifies as the meaningful distinction between schools that produce durable academic gains and schools that simply adopt the latest pedagogical trend.

What Families Should Actually Ask When Choosing a School

Nearly three-in-four Americans support education savings accounts, and 83 percent of school parents support the policy — meaning more families than ever have genuine options about where their children are educated. But most parents, when touring schools, focus on what is immediately visible: test score summaries, extracurricular offerings, building quality, class sizes. Very few think to ask about the instructional model itself.

That is the question that matters most for long-term outcomes.

Ask these five questions on any school visit:

  1. Is instruction explicit and sequential, or primarily student-directed and discovery-based?
  2. Do teachers model new skills clearly before students are asked to attempt them independently?
  3. Are lessons deliberately connected across subjects and grade levels in a planned, cumulative sequence?
  4. Does the school use multisensory techniques to reinforce new content — and in which subjects?
  5. How does the school identify and respond when a student has not yet mastered a concept before the class moves forward?

A school with a specific, confident answer to all five of these questions — regardless of its sector or label — is a school worth looking at seriously.

The Bottom Line

What is explicit, teacher-directed instruction, and does research support it? Explicit instruction is a structured pedagogical approach in which teachers model, scaffold, and systematically sequence content — ensuring students build on prior knowledge at every step rather than rediscovering it independently. The evidence is consistent and substantial across decades of research: structured, teacher-led delivery produces larger and longer-lasting academic gains than open-ended, student-led models. This is especially true in the foundational K–6 years, when the cognitive scaffolding children receive — or don't receive — shapes how effectively they learn everything that comes after. When explicit instruction is paired with a knowledge-rich curriculum and multisensory delivery techniques, the benefits compound across reading, science, and math, and grow stronger with each year a student remains in that environment.

The curriculum debate will keep running. Policy arguments will continue. But for families making school choices right now, the most underrated question on any school visit isn't about test scores or per-pupil spending. It's about what actually happens in the classroom after the bell rings and the teacher steps to the front of the room — and whether that school has made a deliberate, research-backed decision about the answer.

What Charter School Parents Want in 2026

# What Charter School Parents Actually Want in 2026

School choice is no longer a fringe conversation. According to a January 2026 survey of nearly 4,000 U.S. parents conducted by the National School Choice Awareness Foundation, 75% of U.S. parents — roughly 46 million nationwide — considered, searched for, or enrolled at least one child in a new or different school during the previous year, the highest level recorded in five years. For charter schools, that wave of active searching is both an opportunity and a test: families know what they want, and schools that deliver it are growing. Schools that don't are increasingly being left off the list.

So what exactly are today's parents looking for when they consider a charter school? The 2026 research is clearer than ever — and the answers should shape how charter leaders communicate, recruit, and build school culture.

---

Safety Is Now the #1 Priority — It Just Overtook Academics

For years, academic quality held the top spot when parents were asked what they prioritize in a school. That has changed. According to EdChoice's 2026 Schooling in America survey, both charter school parents and private school parents now rank a safe environment as their number one reason for choosing a school, with academic quality following closely in second — a shift that reflects safety's gradual climb to the top after years of rising concern.

This is not a signal that academics stopped mattering. It is a signal that parents are making layered, holistic decisions. A school with strong test scores but a chaotic hallway culture is no longer enough. Families are screening for both rigor and safety simultaneously — and charter schools that have invested in restorative practices, clear behavioral expectations, and a genuine sense of community and belonging have a compelling message to deliver to prospective families who are actively comparing their options.

The National School Choice Awareness Foundation's survey reinforces this: among parents who ultimately enrolled a child in a new school in 2026, 61% cited safety as a top priority, 47% wanted a positive social environment for their children, and 39% prioritized educational themes or approaches. Those numbers tell a story — belonging and safety come first; curriculum specifics come third.

---

What Pushes Families Away From Their Previous School

Understanding why families leave is just as important as knowing what draws them in. The EdChoice survey found that the number one issue parents report for switching schools is excessive stress or anxiety in their child, with academic needs being unmet ranking a close second, and bullying following in third.

These "push" factors map almost perfectly onto the "pull" factors parents describe when choosing a charter school. Families are not just chasing an abstract notion of "better" — they are solving a specific problem their child faced elsewhere. That means effective charter school enrollment communication should speak directly to those pain points: how the school reduces student stress, how it catches and supports struggling learners early, and how it builds a culture where students do not face social hostility in silence.

Among parents who enrolled a child in a new school, the most common primary reason was dissatisfaction with a previous school or a desire for a better fit (32%). That phrase — "better fit" — is doing a lot of work. It encompasses school culture, instructional approach, class size, community feel, and mission alignment: all the things that charter school autonomy makes possible in ways a standard district assignment cannot.

---

The Academic Case Remains Powerful — and Provable

Parents are right to keep academic quality near the top of their list, and charter schools have a strong case to make. High-quality research consistently finds that charter schools boost student achievement by more than their traditional public school counterparts — an advantage that is particularly pronounced for students of color in disadvantaged urban communities, and one that has grown larger as the charter sector has matured and low-performing schools have been held accountable.

A landmark 2023 CREDO study found measurable gains in both math and reading for charter school students compared to their traditional public school peers. Charter students in poverty showed even stronger growth — equivalent to seventeen additional days of learning in math and twenty-three additional days of learning in reading per year compared to peers in traditional public schools. These are not marginal differences.

Research also points to why high-performing charters produce these outcomes consistently. A landmark study of New York City charter schools found five factors predictive of success: frequent teacher feedback, use of data to guide instruction, high-dosage tutoring, increased instructional time, and high expectations. These are replicable practices — not mysteries. And they are exactly the kind of specific, evidence-backed details that prospective families deserve to hear when they are evaluating where to send their child.

---

Parental Involvement: Partnership, Not Just Proximity

One underappreciated pull factor is the relationship between the school and the family itself. Charter school parents tend to be more involved in their children's education, in part because many charter schools treat families as genuine educational partners rather than outside parties with limited influence. Schools that actively cultivate that partnership — through volunteer structures, transparent communication, and an accessible school culture — attract and retain the families who most want to be part of the educational process.

This matters more than it might seem. The 2026 National School Choice Awareness Foundation survey found that 62% of U.S. parents say they are likely to search for a new school during the 2026-27 school year, with the highest levels of planned searching among Black parents, urban parents, and military-connected families, 91% of whom expect to search. These are engaged, motivated families. Charter schools that position themselves as genuine partners — not just service providers — stand to benefit most from that search activity.

That is also where statewide infrastructure earns its value. The North Carolina Assocation for Public Charter Schools — a membership association whose mission centers on support, education, and advocacy for North Carolina charter schools — works to equip member schools with the resources, professional development, and policy support that help individual schools better serve their communities. When associations strengthen the schools in their network, every family searching for a better fit has more good options to find.

---

Information Gaps Are Still Blocking Enrollment

Even highly motivated parents hit walls. Despite record levels of school searching, 31% of parents — nearly 20 million people — searched for a new school but did not enroll a child. The most common barriers were concerns about disruption to their child's routine (33%), uncertainty about making the wrong choice (26%), limited availability or waitlists (26%), and transportation challenges (25%).

Forty-two percent of parents say they want clearer guidance on government programs or funding that can help pay for school choice options, while 40% want better information about education options in their area. Those numbers represent an open invitation for charter schools — and the associations that support them — to step in with clear, accessible, proactive outreach. Confusion is a barrier that schools can actively work to remove.

---

What Charter Schools Should Do With This Data

The direct answer to "what do charter school parents want in 2026" is this: they want safety and culture first, strong academics second, a positive social environment third, and a school that treats them as partners throughout. They are leaving previous schools because their child was stressed, academically underserved, or unsafe — and they are looking for someone to solve that problem specifically, not generically.

For charter leaders, that translates into a few concrete priorities:

  • Lead enrollment communications with culture and safety, not just test scores or curriculum philosophy
  • Name the problems you solve — stress, academic gaps, bullying — and explain how your school addresses them
  • Make parental partnership visible and structural, not an afterthought
  • Reduce information friction at every stage of the enrollment process, from your website to your open house format
  • Use real outcome data to back up academic claims — families can tell the difference between a promise and proof

Nationally, roughly 3.8 million students now attend charter schools — a number that has grown significantly over the past decade. The families searching for options in 2026 represent the next wave of that growth. The schools that understand what those families actually want, and build their culture and communications around it, are the ones that will earn their trust.

High-Dosage Tutoring Works — If Schools Do It Right

# High-Dosage Tutoring Works — If Schools Do It Right

The national debate over high-dosage tutoring just got more complicated. After years of hype, billions in pandemic relief dollars spent, and a wave of new state legislation in 2026, research is delivering a more nuanced verdict: tutoring works — but only when schools implement it the way the evidence actually demands. And that's where charter schools hold a structural advantage most people still aren't talking about.

What "High-Dosage" Actually Means

"High-dosage tutoring" is a specific, research-defined model — not just any extra help after the final bell. According to standards tracked by FutureEd and now embedded in legislation across 20 states, it means:

  • Small groups: No more than four students per tutor
  • Frequent sessions: At least three times per week
  • Sustained duration: A minimum of 30 minutes per session, maintained over several months
  • School-day delivery: Tutoring happens during the instructional day, not squeezed in before the buses arrive or after they leave

That last requirement is the one where most large-scale implementations fell apart.

Why the Pandemic-Era Rollout Often Failed

When federal COVID relief funds flooded schools beginning in 2021, high-dosage tutoring was positioned as the research-backed answer to catastrophic learning loss. Brown University professor Matthew Kraft, one of the field's most prominent researchers, described it at the time as "a shockingly broadly supported policy." Early research was genuinely compelling — students regularly tutored during the school day showed dramatic achievement gains.

The problem was execution at scale. As Chalkbeat's Matt Barnum documented in a widely read September 2026 analysis, the pandemic-era rollout broke down in several predictable ways:

  • Tutors were nearly impossible to find. Districts that planned to recruit college students, retired teachers, or community volunteers discovered a tight labor market had dried up the pipeline. Many ended up conscripting their own classroom teachers instead.
  • Sessions got pushed out of the school day. When teachers were pressed into tutoring duty with no free periods to spare, sessions migrated to before and after school — where student attendance and engagement collapsed.
  • Virtual platforms overpromised. Third-party companies marketed themselves as "high-dosage" providers while often delivering little more than on-demand homework help that students could — and did — ignore.
  • Quality shrank as programs scaled. When Kraft and colleagues re-analyzed prior tutoring studies, they found a consistent pattern: larger programs produced meaningfully smaller gains. The research that made high-dosage tutoring so exciting had largely captured small, meticulously designed pilots — not district-wide rollouts managed under emergency conditions.

The University of Chicago Education Lab, which studied pandemic-era tutoring programs across multiple agencies, characterized its own findings as both "encouraging" and "sobering." Monica Bhatt, a co-author of that research, offered a blunt summary: you spend less, you get less dosage, and you get less learning gains. Despite its name, pandemic-era high-dosage tutoring was frequently neither high-dosage nor particularly well-targeted.

The Stakes Remain High in 2026

None of this means the learning crisis has passed. A 2026 NWEA report found that only about one-third of schools had returned to pre-pandemic performance levels in reading or math — and just 14 percent had recovered in both subjects simultaneously. Education Secretary Linda McMahon, in a candid K-12 Dive interview, acknowledged she does not expect the next round of NAEP scores to show marked improvement.

State legislatures are not giving up on tutoring as a strategy. FutureEd's 2026 legislative tracker counts 45 tutoring-related bills across 20 states, with 15 already enacted this session. Massachusetts appropriated $20 million for high-dosage tutoring in its annual budget. New Mexico committed $5 million per year for three years. Virginia launched an AI Innovation in Education Pilot that explicitly includes AI-assisted tutoring delivery. Oklahoma is among the most active states, advancing multiple bills that would formally define high-dosage tutoring in state statute — specifying group sizes, session frequency, tutor qualifications, and assessment-based placement requirements.

So what does a well-built tutoring program actually look like? The emerging consensus from researchers and from states now codifying these programs in law is consistent: protect session frequency, cap group sizes strictly, embed tutoring within the instructional day, use ongoing assessment data to determine who gets served and how, and hire dedicated tutoring staff rather than pulling it from classroom teachers. These aren't bureaucratic checkboxes — they are precisely the design features that made early tutoring research so promising in the first place.

Where Charter Schools Have a Real, Underappreciated Advantage

Here is the part of the high-dosage tutoring story that deserves far more attention: the schools best positioned to implement this model with genuine fidelity are those that control their own schedule, staffing, and school culture. That description fits most charter schools far better than it fits most traditional public school districts.

Large districts face a thicket of structural barriers to implementation. Collective bargaining agreements govern how teacher time is allocated during the instructional day, often down to the minute. Master schedules are built around shared facilities, busing contracts, and bell times negotiated years in advance. Carving out a new instructional block — three days per week, for specific students, with specific staff — requires negotiating through layers of agreements and administrative approval that can take a full school year or longer to change, if change is possible at all.

Charter schools, by contrast, can redesign their schedule. A charter school leader who decides in August that third graders reading below grade level will receive a daily 35-minute small-group tutoring block can build that block into the master schedule before the first day of school. They can hire a dedicated tutoring coordinator as part of their base staffing model. They can use their school-wide assessment data to identify which students need intervention before the gap becomes a chasm — and adjust placements mid-year when the data changes. The autonomy that defines charter schooling is, in the context of high-dosage tutoring, a direct implementation advantage.

This is precisely the kind of practical insight that statewide organizations work to put in front of school leaders. The Colorado League of Charter Schools, which advocates for Colorado's charter sector and provides member schools with services spanning academic programming, school quality reviews, and operational support, is an example of how the sector builds shared capacity — so individual schools aren't figuring out how to operationalize complex interventions like high-dosage tutoring entirely on their own.

What Schools Should Do Right Now

If your school is considering a high-dosage tutoring program for the 2026-27 school year — or evaluating whether your current effort is actually delivering — here are the non-negotiable design criteria the research supports:

  1. Lock in a school-day slot. Before- and after-school tutoring consistently shows weaker attendance and outcomes. Treat the tutoring block as core instruction, not a supplement.
  2. Hold the 4:1 group cap. This ratio appears in nearly every high-quality study and in every serious state definition. Exceeding it dilutes the model.
  3. Commit to three sessions per week, minimum. Twice-weekly tutoring starts to look like enrichment. Three or more times weekly is where the evidence lives.
  4. Build a dedicated tutoring staff. Pulling classroom teachers into tutoring roles costs you on both ends. Invest in tutors — trained paraprofessionals, AmeriCorps members, or part-time hires — whose primary job is tutoring.
  5. Use data for placement, not intuition. Assessment results should drive who enters the program, when they exit, and what content tutoring sessions cover.
  6. Monitor progress during the program, not just at the end. Mid-program data is what allows schools to course-correct before a semester is lost.

The Bottom Line

High-dosage tutoring is not a failed experiment — it is a demanding one. The research on well-implemented programs remains genuinely strong. What failed during the pandemic era was not the concept; it was the corners cut under pressure, the sessions moved off-campus, the group sizes that crept up, the tutors who never materialized. Charter schools that use their scheduling autonomy to build tutoring in from the start — during the school day, in small groups, with dedicated staff, sustained across months — are positioned to deliver the results that first made this model so exciting. The intervention works. The question is which schools have the flexibility and the discipline to do it right.

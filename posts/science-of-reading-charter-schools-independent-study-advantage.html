Science of Reading: What Charter Schools Get Right

# The Science of Reading Revolution Is Here — and Flex Charter Schools Are Already Living It

California's literacy numbers are hard to look away from: just 29% of California fourth-graders scored proficient in reading on the 2024 National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) — a result that sits below the national average and has continued to slide since the pandemic. For families watching a child struggle to decode simple words, those figures aren't statistics. They're a daily reality.

The good news is that 2026 marks a genuine turning point in how California plans to fix it. When Assembly Bill 1454 passed unanimously in October 2025, many educators across California rejoiced. The bill aims to create coordinated, research-based literacy practices that will serve all students, including California's 1.1 million English language learners. But legislation is just a starting line. California is taking a less prescriptive approach than some other states, leaving more control in the hands of local districts — which means implementation will be uneven, and the gap between what the law says and what happens in a given classroom could stretch on for years.

For families who can't afford to wait, that gap matters enormously.

---

What Is the Science of Reading — and Why Has It Taken This Long?

What is the science of reading? The Science of Reading is a vast body of research that gives us a clear picture of how the human brain learns to read. At its core, the research shows that explicit and systematic phonics instruction is essential for teaching children to read. It encompasses phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency, vocabulary, and comprehension — all taught in a deliberate sequence, not left to chance or a child's ability to guess words from context clues.

For decades, many American classrooms relied on a competing approach — "whole language" or "three-cueing" — which encouraged children to use picture cues, sentence context, and memorization to identify unfamiliar words rather than decoding them sound by sound. The science of reading movement has swept through state legislatures over the past decade, with more than 40 states passing laws aimed at furthering evidence-based approaches to early literacy instruction. The momentum reached the federal level in 2026 as well: federal science of reading legislation passed the House, with proponents arguing the nation had "moved away from phonics-based instruction" and simply must do better.

California's AB 1454 requires the statewide implementation of evidence-based literacy instruction, officially moving away from the largely discredited "whole language" approach. The state has committed real money to back it up: California included $200 million in the 2025–26 budget to fund the bill's teacher training component, and as of September 1, 2026, the California Department of Education is vetting professional learning providers to equip TK–5 teachers with evidence-based literacy practices.

---

The Implementation Gap: Why Families in Traditional Schools May Still Wait

Passing a law is not the same as changing what happens in a classroom on a Monday morning. After more than a year of debate about what should — and should not — be included in AB 1454, the harder work begins: turning policy into consistent classroom practice. Experience from other states offers a cautionary note — legislation can set the direction, but implementation determines whether students actually feel the difference.

Teacher retraining takes time. Curriculum adoption takes time. And the stakes of waiting are steep: children not reading proficiently by 3rd grade are four times more likely to drop out of high school. That risk compounds with every year a struggling reader spends waiting for their school to catch up to the research.

This is exactly where the structure of flex and independent-study charter schools creates a meaningful, practical advantage.

---

Why Flex and Independent-Study Charter Schools Are Already Positioned to Lead

In a parent-directed or independent-study charter model, the family has always been the first point of contact for a child's learning — including literacy instruction. These schools don't hand a child off to a single district-mandated curriculum and hope for the best. They build an active partnership between a credentialed Teacher of Record and the family, and that partnership includes intentional, ongoing decisions about instructional materials and pacing.

That structural reality means two things. First, families in these models have typically been far more engaged in how their child is learning to read — not just whether they are. Many independent-study families have been using structured literacy and phonics-based programs for years, long before AB 1454 made evidence-based instruction a statewide mandate. Second, when research-aligned materials become available, a flex or independent-study school can update them at the individual student level — rather than waiting for an entire district curriculum cycle to turn over.

Excel Academy Charter School, a tuition-free California charter serving students TK through 12th grade, exemplifies this model. Families choose between a personalized independent-study path — where the parent or guardian serves as the primary teacher, supported by a credentialed Teacher of Record — and a virtual path with live online instruction. That intentional partnership structure means literacy instruction is never a black box: families and teachers are actively co-designing how a child learns, and can adjust materials and pacing in real time based on what the evidence supports.

---

Questions Every Parent Should Ask About Their School's Reading Approach

Whether you're enrolled in a flex charter, evaluating one, or reconsidering a traditional school in light of AB 1454, these are the questions that cut through the noise:

  • Does the school use a structured literacy or phonics-based curriculum? Ask by name — programs like CKLA, Fundations, SIPPS, or All About Reading are meaningful indicators.
  • Have teachers completed science of reading professional development? Under AB 1454, California TK–5 teachers should be moving through approved training — ask what has been completed and on what timeline.
  • How does the school identify students who are falling behind in reading? Early screening tools should be in use before the end of kindergarten, not in second or third grade when intervention becomes far harder.
  • Can parents see and access the instructional materials used at home? In an independent-study or flex model, this is yes by design — and it should be a baseline expectation in any model worth choosing.
  • Will the school adjust if a child isn't making progress? Evidence-based instruction includes ongoing progress monitoring and targeted intervention. A single curriculum applied uniformly to every learner is not the standard the research supports.

---

The Bottom Line

The Science of Reading movement represents the most significant shift in how American schools approach foundational literacy in a generation. Between January and March of 2026, students across the country again participated in NAEP assessments — and as results are awaited in 2027, one question looms large: will we finally see meaningful improvement in reading, or more of the same? California's AB 1454 is a meaningful step forward. But for families who need real literacy progress now — not after two or three curriculum cycles — a flex or independent-study charter school that positions the family as an active partner in instruction may be the most direct path to getting it right from the start.

Reading is the foundation of everything that follows in a child's education. The research on how to build that foundation is settled. The only question left is whether a child's school is actually using it.

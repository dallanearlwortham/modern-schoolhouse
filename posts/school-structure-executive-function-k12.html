How School Structure Builds Executive Function

# How School Structure Builds Executive Function — and Why It Matters More Than Most Parents Realize

When parents research elementary schools, they typically ask about reading scores, curriculum, and extracurriculars. What they rarely ask about — but arguably should — is how the school itself trains children's brains to learn. That's the domain of executive function (EF): the cluster of cognitive skills that includes working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility. A growing body of research makes a compelling case that school structure isn't just a backdrop for learning — it's actively building these skills every single day.

What Executive Function Actually Is (and Why It Predicts So Much)

Executive function is often described as the brain's "command center." These are the mental processes that let a child hold information in mind while solving a multi-step math problem, resist the urge to blurt out an answer while waiting to be called on, and shift smoothly from reading time to science without an emotional meltdown.

A landmark 2026 review published in Educational Psychology Review by Trautwein and colleagues describes self-regulation competence — the umbrella that covers EF, self-regulated learning, and conscientiousness — as a developmental "superpower" that is "both predictive of a broad range of outcomes and amenable to educational support." The researchers examined evidence across three research traditions and concluded that self-regulation competence predicts important cognitive, social, and emotional outcomes in school and across the life span.

What does executive function predict specifically? Research from Cortés Pascual and colleagues found that EF skills predict academic performance in both language arts and mathematics, with the relationship being slightly stronger in math — where students must hold multiple steps in mind simultaneously, organize information, and retrieve facts quickly. Separately, developmental psychologists Blair and Razza demonstrated that inhibitory control — a core EF component — predicted early math and reading outcomes in young children, even after controlling for general intelligence. Their conclusion: EF skills are more important for school readiness than IQ or entry-level academic skills alone.

School Itself Trains the Brain — the Meta-Analytic Evidence

Most people assume children simply mature into better self-regulation over time. A 2026 meta-analysis published in the Journal of Experimental Child Psychology (Donenfeld et al.) challenged that assumption directly. Analyzing 12 studies involving approximately 1,611 children ages 4.5 to 9, the researchers found a small but statistically robust schooling effect on executive functions (g = 0.24). In other words, formal schooling — independent of natural maturation — meaningfully improves children's EF skills.

The mechanism is intuitive once you see it: formal school entry functions as a kind of "global training program for executive functions." Children must inhibit prepotent responses (stop fidgeting, wait their turn), sustain attention to teacher instructions, hold directions in working memory, and flexibly shift between tasks. A school that consistently demands and supports these behaviors is, in effect, running a daily EF workout.

The Role of Classroom Routines: Reducing Cognitive Load to Build Capacity

Here is the counterintuitive insight that changes how you think about structured schools: predictable routines don't restrict children — they free their minds to develop higher-order skills.

Neurodiversity and disability inclusion consultant April Remfrey explains the mechanism through cognitive load theory. When driving a familiar route, your cognitive load is low because basic tasks are automated, freeing mental capacity for other demands. The same principle applies in the classroom: when students don't have to expend mental energy figuring out what comes next — where to put their work, how transitions happen, what the opening routine looks like — they can direct that capacity toward actual content and toward building EF skills themselves.

Research confirms this dynamic. Studies in structured routine-based learning show statistically significant improvements in EF domains, including working memory and cognitive flexibility, in students who experience consistent, intentional daily routines. The finding aligns with what educational researchers have demonstrated: EF skills are best developed through daily practice embedded in structure, not through isolated "EF curriculum" lessons added onto an otherwise unstructured day. The routine is the intervention.

Why Explicit, Sequential Instruction Is Part of the Picture

The type of instruction matters alongside the schedule. When a lesson is predictably structured — the teacher models a skill, students practice with guidance, feedback is immediate and clear — children's working memory is not overwhelmed by having to decode what is happening procedurally. They can focus cognitive resources on the content itself.

Open-ended, discovery-heavy environments can be valuable in certain contexts, but for children still building foundational EF capacity, they add procedural uncertainty on top of academic challenge — a double cognitive load that research suggests disadvantages developing learners most. This is one reason schools with explicit, sequential, teacher-directed instruction tend to produce strong results for students who need EF scaffolding: children navigating high-stress home environments, those with attention challenges, and young learners still in the sensitive developmental window for EF growth.

Jefferson Academy, a tuition-free public charter elementary school in Kaysville, Utah serving grades K–6, describes its instructional approach explicitly as "explicit, sequential, and teacher-directed with a multi-sensory approach" — precisely the pedagogical features the research identifies as EF-supportive. Its history-centered Core Knowledge curriculum, built on the foundational belief that "children gain knowledge by building on what they already know," also reflects the sequenced, cumulative knowledge-building that reduces extraneous cognitive load and strengthens working memory across grade levels.

What Should Families Look for When Evaluating Schools?

If executive function development is a priority — and the evidence says it should be — here are the structural signals worth assessing during school visits:

  • A visible, predictable daily schedule. Do students know what comes next without anxiety or guesswork?
  • Consistent, taught transitions. Does the school move between activities in an orderly, rehearsed way, or is every transition improvised?
  • An explicit instruction model. Does the teacher model, guide, and check for understanding in a clear and repeatable sequence?
  • School-wide routine consistency. Do teachers across classrooms share expectations, reducing the cognitive overhead of adapting to a new set of rules in every room?
  • A calm, orderly environment. Research shows that even mild social-evaluative stress impairs executive function in most people — meaning a low-stress school environment is not a luxury preference but a cognitive performance condition.

The Takeaway

The question "Is this a structured school?" is often treated as a matter of family taste — some parents like it, some don't. The EF research reframes it as something more consequential. Structure, routine, and explicit instruction are not stylistically conservative choices; they are developmentally active ones. A school that runs consistent, predictable days is simultaneously teaching its students how to regulate attention, hold information in mind, and control their responses — skills that research consistently links to academic achievement, mental health, and long-term life outcomes.

Choosing a school for your child is, in part, choosing what their brain practices every single day. That's worth asking about before you enroll.

Charter Schools in the ESA Era: What Makes Them Worth Choosing

Charter Schools Are Facing a New Kind of Competition—And It's Coming From Inside the School Choice Movement

For most of the last two decades, charter schools were the school choice story. They were the disruptive alternative, the proof that public education didn't have to mean your zip-code-assigned school. But in 2026, that identity is under serious pressure—not from opponents of school choice, but from an accelerating wave of Education Savings Accounts (ESAs) that is reshaping the entire K-12 landscape.

The question charter school leaders are now asking each other isn't whether school choice is good policy. It's whether charter schools have a compelling enough answer to the question every family with options is now asking: Why you, specifically?

The ESA Wave Is Real, Rapid, and Reshaping Enrollment

Heading into the 2026 legislative session, 17 states already have, or soon will have, programs that make public funds available for private educational expenses to all students, regardless of income. That's not a niche experiment—it's a structural shift in how American families think about K-12 education.

FutureEd is tracking 205 private school choice bills across 36 states in the 2026 session alone, with 22 bills already enacted. The moves are consequential: Texas received over 100,000 applications in less than two weeks for its new ESA program, and Iowa's ESA award for the 2026-27 school year is set at $8,148 per participating student. Tennessee raised its ESA enrollment cap from 25,000 to 35,000 students. Five additional states are expected to add ESA programs by the end of 2026.

The federal picture adds another layer. On July 4, 2025, President Trump signed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which includes a national voucher-type program that states can opt into beginning January 1, 2027. Nearly half of the students currently participating in private school choice programs are already enrolled in ESAs. Combined with state-level expansion, charter schools are now competing against a far wider menu of options than they were even three years ago.

Charter Leaders Are Feeling the Pressure in Enrollment Numbers

This isn't abstract policy concern—it's showing up in actual headcounts. A survey of 285 charter school leaders in Arizona, Florida, and Utah—three states with mature, universal ESA programs—published in August 2026 by AEI's Conservative Education Reform Network found that charter schools are now "forced to compete against nimble, less-regulated ESA-funded providers while operating under heavier compliance and resource constraints."

Nearly half of respondents reported enrollment losses due to students using an ESA for non-charter options such as private school tuition or subsidized homeschooling, and nearly the same proportion anticipated a future decline in total enrollment. Enrollment impacts were most pronounced in Arizona, where the universal ESA has been in place the longest: nearly half of Arizona charter leaders reported that students had already left their schools to use ESA funds, with reported withdrawals ranging from a single student to hundreds in larger schools. Fifty-eight percent of Arizona respondents anticipated that ESAs would decrease their enrollment going forward.

Across all three states, respondents reported a growing perception that private and microschool models now offer superior flexibility and autonomy compared to their charter schools. The frustration runs deeper than enrollment alone: charter dollars are eaten up by authorizer oversight, maintenance costs, and detailed reporting requirements that nimbler private providers simply don't carry—even though ESA programs often provide lower per-pupil funds than charters receive.

What Charter Leaders Say Has to Change

When asked what legislative reforms would help them compete, charter leaders in the survey were pointed:

  • 65% cited the reduction of regulation and bureaucracy
  • 58% referenced funding equity and facilities support
  • 42% proposed relief around special education compliance and paperwork

Importantly, most of them emphasized that they are more interested in reforming charter schools to compete with ESAs than in abandoning the public model altogether. Charter leaders still prefer the public option—they just want the rules of the game to be fair.

So What, Exactly, Is the Charter School Value Proposition Right Now?

What makes a charter school worth choosing when an ESA could follow a child almost anywhere? The honest answer requires charter advocates to stop relying on the old "we're not your neighborhood district school" framing and articulate something more specific. Here is what the research and charter leaders themselves identify as genuine, durable advantages:

Free tuition, no financial barrier to entry. Unlike private schools—even those accessible through ESA funds—charter schools charge no tuition. That matters enormously for families who lack the time, transportation, or financial cushion to navigate a marketplace of microschools and private providers. ESA programs in practice tend to disproportionately benefit families with more resources to research, apply, and manage spending accounts.

Public accountability and a performance floor. Charter leaders noted that charter schools maintain "appropriate governance, accountability, and oversight" as a comparative advantage. That authorizer accountability—often criticized as bureaucratic drag—is also a quality signal that matters to families who've been burned by short-lived or unvetted private options.

Scalability and replicability. A high-performing charter model can be brought to thousands of students across multiple communities. Individual microschools and ESA-funded home arrangements, however innovative, tend to remain boutique options for families with the savvy and stability to find and sustain them.

Depth of mission and community rootedness. This is the differentiator that enrollment numbers alone can't fully capture. The charter schools that are holding their own in ESA states aren't generic—they are deeply committed to a specific population, a specific pedagogy, or a specific community, and families can feel the difference. BRICK, the education network operating schools in Newark and Buffalo, exemplifies what this looks like in practice: its schools span kindergarten through grade 12 and sit inside a broader ecosystem of holistic supports that stretches from prenatal care and early childhood readiness through social-emotional wellness, college counseling, and career pathways—all built around an explicit mission to nurture families "from pre-cradle to career." That kind of sustained, layered, community-rooted investment is not something an ESA debit card can replicate. It is a relationship built over years, in a specific place, with a specific promise to a specific community.

The Adaptation Imperative: Three Moves Charter Schools Need to Make Now

The evidence offers a clear warning: charter schools risk marginalization unless they undergo a fundamental shift away from a system-centric approach toward a parent-centric model. That shift looks different depending on context, but it generally means three things.

Lead with mission, not category. Families choosing between a charter school, a private school, and a home education stipend are not choosing based on governance structure—they are choosing based on what a school promises their child, specifically. Charter schools that can articulate a clear, credible, and differentiated promise win families. Those that lead with institutional identity alone will lose them to providers who speak directly to what parents actually want.

Reduce friction for families. Enrollment processes, waitlist communication, and day-to-day family experience need to match the speed and clarity that private providers now offer. The ESA market is raising the bar on what families expect from any school they choose, and charter schools that ignore that signal will lose families who could have been their strongest advocates and ambassadors.

Push hard on policy reform in 2026. With state legislatures actively debating school choice architecture right now, this is exactly the right moment for charter advocates to press for funding equity, elimination of duplicative reporting requirements, facilities and transportation support, and streamlined special education paperwork. The window is open. The question is whether the sector shows up to push through it.

The Bigger Picture

The coexistence of ESAs and charter schools in the same market is not inherently a crisis—but it is a forcing function. It is pressure to be excellent, specific, and honest about what charter schools uniquely offer families who need publicly accountable, tuition-free, community-rooted options. To let charter schools wither under the weight of unfair compliance burdens and outdated regulations would be a policy failure, not an inevitable market outcome.

The most important question for charter school leaders right now isn't how to stop ESAs from expanding. It's how to make the case—clearly, specifically, and to real families making real decisions—that a well-run, mission-driven charter school offers something worth choosing even when the choice is entirely theirs.

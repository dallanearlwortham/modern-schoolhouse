Science of Reading in Charter Schools: What Works

# The Science of Reading Is an Equity Issue—and Charter Schools Are Positioned to Lead

Only 31 percent of fourth-grade students performed at or above the NAEP Proficient level on the 2024 reading assessment—two points lower than 2022 and four points lower than 2019. Put another way: 69 percent of the country's ten-year-olds are not reading at grade level. For students from low-income families or communities of color, the gap is wider still. These numbers represent real children in real classrooms, and they have barely moved in a generation.

A decades-long body of research has converged on a clear answer: most children can learn to read proficiently when they are taught the right way. The question is whether schools will actually change how they teach. That is the promise—and the challenge—at the heart of the science of reading movement. For charter schools built on missions of equity and college access, getting this right may be the highest-leverage instructional decision a school leader makes this decade.

What the Science of Reading Actually Is (and Isn't)

The science of reading is not a single program or a branded curriculum. It refers to a vast body of multidisciplinary research—spanning neuroscience, cognitive psychology, linguistics, and education—on how humans learn to read. The science is ongoing and continues to evolve, but it has long centered on five foundational components: phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency, vocabulary, and comprehension.

The practical classroom application of this research is called Structured Literacy, which emphasizes instruction that is explicit, systematic, and cumulative across all five of those components. This stands in contrast to balanced literacy, which emerged from the whole language tradition and combines some phonics instruction with meaning-focused, context-cueing strategies. Research has shown that balanced literacy often falls short—particularly for students who struggle, who are multilingual learners, or who have dyslexia or other reading-related disabilities. Structured Literacy doesn't ask students to guess words from pictures or sentence context; it teaches them to decode, deliberately and in sequence.

When schools follow science of reading–aligned instruction, studies show that over 95 percent of students can learn to read proficiently. That figure should command the attention of every school leader who has accepted chronic reading failure as an unfortunate given.

The Policy Shift Is Real—but Incomplete

The legislative momentum behind this research is striking. As of November 2024, lawmakers in 40 states and the District of Columbia have passed laws or implemented policies around evidence-based reading instruction. In 2023 and 2024 alone, more than 20 states passed new legislation specifically requiring adherence to the science of reading in reading instruction. Some states have tied dedicated funding to implementation—grants specifically designed to help districts and charter schools adopt high-quality instructional materials and invest in professional learning grounded in the science of reading.

But legislation does not equal implementation. No state improved in fourth- or eighth-grade reading in 2024, and eight states scored worse than they did a year or two prior in eighth-grade reading. Passing a law mandating evidence-based reading instruction is the easy part. Changing what happens in 50,000 classrooms—especially in under-resourced schools without strong coaching infrastructure—is a fundamentally harder problem.

Why Reading Instruction Is an Equity Issue

What does the science of reading have to do with equity? Simply this: the students most harmed by ineffective reading instruction are the students charter schools were built to serve.

Many charter schools were founded explicitly to close achievement gaps between white students and students of color. According to data from the National Alliance for Public Charter Schools, 70 percent of charter school students are students of color, compared to 53 percent of district school students. Charter students are also more likely than their district-school peers to qualify for free and reduced-price lunch. For decades, many of these students attended schools using reading methods—whole language, balanced literacy, three-cueing systems—that the research did not support.

These challenges are especially acute for multilingual learners, students with disabilities, and students requiring intensive reading support. With limited guidance in many curricula for adapting research principles to students' cultural and linguistic assets, teachers have been left to navigate enormous complexity largely on their own.

Structured Literacy addresses this directly. Its explicit, sequential, diagnostic approach is well-suited to diverse learners precisely because it leaves less to guesswork. It teaches all of the components necessary for reading success in a way that benefits all students—including multilingual learners who benefit from explicit instruction in English phonology, students with dyslexia who need systematic decoding practice, and advanced readers who still benefit from strong foundational fluency.

What Schools Get Wrong About Implementation

The most common mistake schools make when adopting the science of reading is treating it as a curriculum swap rather than a systems change. Ordering a new phonics program and announcing the transition does not move reading scores. Even excellent instructional materials are not a cure-all. Students are unlikely to draw deep learning from great materials if the adults using them are unprepared, unsupported, or unconvinced.

Effective implementation requires sustained attention to at least three areas:

1. Genuine teacher professional development—not one-day workshops The science of reading movement will not improve student literacy outcomes if school and district leaders fail to invest seriously in teacher professional learning. Teachers nationwide have reported feeling overwhelmed by rushed curriculum implementation and a lack of ongoing classroom support. Most current teachers were trained in balanced literacy or whole language methods during their preparation programs. Shifting deeply held instructional beliefs and daily classroom habits requires more than a summer training—it requires ongoing coaching, classroom observation, and collaborative planning time built into the school calendar.

2. Universal screening and early intervention Schools implementing the science of reading effectively use universal screeners in the early grades to identify students at risk for reading difficulties before those difficulties compound into years of academic struggle. Research on kindergarten screening indicates that effective tools measure phonological awareness, letter-sound association, rapid automatic naming, and phonological memory. First-grade screening should include phoneme segmentation specifically. Waiting until third grade to identify a struggling reader means allowing two or three years of avoidable frustration to accumulate—and remediation becomes exponentially harder the longer intervention is delayed.

3. Whole-school coherence across every grade An excellent phonics classroom on the second floor cannot overcome a building-level culture that still rewards guessing strategies or treats decoding as a low-status skill. The research benefits of Structured Literacy materialize when a school builds coherent expectations, shared instructional language, and consistent practice from the earliest grades through upper elementary. Leadership clarity matters enormously here—principals need to know what good science of reading instruction looks like and how to give teachers meaningful feedback on it.

This is precisely the kind of whole-school coherence that high-accountability charter networks work hard to build. Noble - Baker College Prep—one of 17 tuition-free, open-enrollment campuses in the Noble Schools network serving grades 6–12 across Chicago—operates under a network-wide mission of ensuring students have equitable school experiences that equip them to complete college and lead choice-filled lives. In a network structured around measurable outcomes for historically underserved students, every instructional system, including literacy, has to justify itself against that mission. Evidence-based practice is not optional; it is the floor.

What to Look for in Curriculum and Materials

Not all "phonics-aligned" or "science of reading–based" programs are created equal—and the marketing around this space has become crowded as publishers rush to label existing products with new terminology. When evaluating materials, school leaders should ask:

  • Is instruction explicit and systematic, building skills in a carefully sequenced, cumulative order rather than spiraling loosely or relying on incidental exposure?
  • Does the program address all five components—phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency, vocabulary, and comprehension—not just phonics in isolation?
  • Is there a built-in diagnostic and progress-monitoring system so teachers can identify individual needs and adjust instruction accordingly?
  • Does the curriculum include specific guidance for multilingual learners and students with disabilities, not just general-education learners?
  • Has it been independently reviewed for alignment? Free, publicly available rubric scores from sources like EdReports and state curriculum review frameworks (Louisiana's is widely respected) are a useful starting point.

The Bottom Line

The science of reading is not a trend that will cycle out in a few years. It is the product of decades of convergent evidence—from neuroscience, cognitive science, and classroom research—about how the human brain learns to decode language. That evidence was available long before most of today's students were born. The cost of ignoring it has been measured in millions of children who reached middle school unable to read with fluency or comprehension, and who faced narrowed futures as a result.

For charter schools grounded in missions of equity and college access, aligning literacy instruction with that evidence is not a compliance exercise or a rebranding opportunity. It is the work. The students who most need excellent reading instruction are sitting in classrooms right now—and they cannot afford to wait for another decade of inconclusive policy debate to resolve itself.

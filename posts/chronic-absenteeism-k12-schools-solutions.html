The Attendance Crisis: What Schools Can Do Right Now

# The Attendance Crisis Isn't Over — And the Latest Research Shows What Actually Works

Empty desks were supposed to be a pandemic problem. Schools reopened, the emergency passed, and attendance would normalize. That was the expectation. Instead, chronic absenteeism — defined as missing at least 10 percent of the school year — exploded during COVID-era school closures and, for millions of students, never really recovered.

Years later, those seats are still empty.

A recent RAND Corporation report — among the first to examine absences for the 2024–25 school year — estimated that roughly 22% of students across the United States were chronically absent, representing approximately 10.8 million children missing enough school to put their academic futures at serious risk. The trend is moving in the right direction, but barely: chronic absenteeism improved by nearly one percentage point in 2025 across 44 states and Washington, D.C., a pace that had slowed considerably compared with improvements of more than two percentage points in each of the two prior years.

At this trajectory, most schools won't return to pre-pandemic attendance norms for years. So what do schools — especially those with the autonomy to act without layers of bureaucratic approval — actually do to reverse it?

---

Why This Is Everyone's Problem, Not Just the Absent Student's

Before diving into solutions, it's worth understanding how far the damage radiates. Chronic absenteeism doesn't only hurt the student who's absent.

Research has found that in classrooms with high rates of missing students, even the kids who show up every day exhibit lower reading, math, and executive function skills. The working theory: constant absences force teachers into cycles of review and remediation when students return, slowing progress for the whole class and making it nearly impossible to build stable classroom routines and culture.

Teacher morale takes a measurable hit, too. A study published in Education Researcher found that teachers whose classes had higher absenteeism in the fall semester rated significantly lower on measures of job satisfaction, feelings of usefulness, and belief in the teaching profession. Mounting absences make it harder to feel close to students — and the endless cycle of make-up work piles onto already-long to-do lists.

The compounding effect is particularly striking: research has shown that students' absences can make their peers more likely to miss school, too. Absenteeism, it turns out, is somewhat contagious.

---

Who's Missing School — and Why

Understanding the causes is the prerequisite for any real solution. And the reasons students miss school are more varied than the popular image of the "hooky-playing" teenager suggests.

In a recent RAND survey of students, the most common reason for missing school was illness (67%). Other reasons included:

  • Feeling down or anxious — 10%
  • Oversleeping — 9%
  • Being uninterested in attending — 7%
  • Caring for a family member — 4%
  • Lack of transportation — 3%
  • Work conflicts — 1%

That mix — medical, emotional, logistical, motivational — means no single intervention solves the problem. It also means schools that treat absenteeism as purely a discipline issue will consistently fail to address the actual root causes.

Urban schools are facing a steeper climb than most. In roughly half of urban school districts, more than 30% of students were chronically absent in 2024–25, compared with about 7% of suburban and 9% of rural schools reporting those same elevated rates in the same period.

---

What Research Says Actually Works

The clearest finding from recent research: schools that successfully reduce chronic absenteeism don't rely on a single strategy. They combine a culture of belonging with proactive data use, rapid outreach, and genuine family partnership — and they consistently avoid punitive responses that research shows tend to backfire.

Here's what the evidence supports:

Build a School Students Actually Want to Attend

A student's sense of belonging in the school community is a direct driver of attendance. When students don't feel safe, seen, or connected, their risk of absence rises — and the data backs this up. Students who demonstrate a stronger sense of belonging are more likely to attend school regularly.

The most effective schools invest in relentless relationship-building between staff and students, recruit faculty who reflect their student community's backgrounds and identities, and make belonging a structural priority rather than an afterthought. As one charter school leader put it: the biggest reason students want to come is because they love their teachers.

Track Every Day and Assign Clear Ownership

High-performing schools aren't just collecting attendance data — they're using it in real time. The schools seeing the best results treat daily attendance monitoring as a core operational function, with clear responsibility assigned to each step of the follow-up process. A student absent on Monday gets a phone call by Tuesday, not a letter at the end of the month.

Talk to Families Before Assuming the Worst

To really drive down absences, leaders must first understand the specific reasons their students are missing class — and that requires genuine conversations with families, not form letters. Schools that do this often uncover barriers that are surprisingly fixable. One well-documented example: a district discovered some families weren't showing up simply because they didn't understand the bus routes and didn't know who to ask for help.

Schools with stronger, pre-existing family engagement — measured by trust between parents and teachers, parent involvement, and parental influence over school decisions — had chronic absenteeism rates after remote learning that were about six percentage points lower than similar schools with weaker family engagement, according to a 2023 study by Learning Heroes and The New Teacher Project.

Connect Older Students to Something Meaningful

For high schoolers especially, relevance matters. Many districts have found success in creating educational experiences that feel real and purposeful — expanded career and technical education, work-based learning, and dual enrollment programs that give students a visible connection between showing up and their actual futures.

---

How Charter Schools Are Putting These Strategies Into Practice

Charter schools — with their autonomy to shape school culture, staffing, and schedules without district-wide constraints — are proving to be effective laboratories for attendance recovery.

A February 2025 report from The Mind Trust examined six Indianapolis charter and innovation schools that had made measurable progress on chronic absenteeism. Serving a combined 6,597 students, these schools faced real, systemic barriers: inconsistent transportation, limited healthcare access, and significant mental health challenges. What set them apart wasn't unlimited resources — it was how they responded. Four themes emerged across every school: cultivating environments of belonging, tracking attendance consistently, responding swiftly when patterns appeared, and partnering with families and community organizations as genuine allies rather than problems to manage.

Those themes aren't unique to Indianapolis. They represent a model that travels — and one that advocacy organizations are actively working to spread across their member school networks.

The Public Charter School Alliance of South Carolina, which serves as South Carolina's trusted voice for public charter schools, exists precisely to connect schools with this kind of best-practice infrastructure — helping charter educators across the state access the resources, support systems, and policy knowledge they need to serve every student well, including those most at risk of falling through the cracks of persistent absence.

---

The Parent Awareness Gap Is a Real Obstacle

One underappreciated dimension of this crisis: most parents don't fully understand what chronic absenteeism is — or don't realize their own child qualifies.

In a June 2024 NPR/Ipsos poll, 58% of parents of school-aged children identified chronic absenteeism as a major problem. But only 32% correctly defined it as missing 10% or more of school days; 51% set the threshold far higher, at 20% or more. And among parents whose children were already chronically absent, just 47% said they were concerned.

That awareness gap matters enormously. A parent who doesn't realize their child is already in crisis territory won't engage as a partner in solving it. Schools that make clear, non-accusatory communication about attendance thresholds a regular part of how they engage families are starting from a far stronger position.

Adding to the challenge: one in four students does not think missing three or more weeks of school in a single academic year is a problem, according to recent RAND survey data. That perception gap — among students whose parents also tend to underestimate the issue — is a significant cultural hurdle that schools have to actively work to close.

---

The Bottom Line

Chronic absenteeism is a solvable problem. The research is clear about what moves the needle: belonging, early intervention, honest family partnership, and a refusal to treat absence as a character flaw rather than a symptom of real-world barriers. What's less clear is whether enough schools are treating it with the urgency it deserves.

Attendance is the prerequisite for everything else in education. Curriculum, instruction, tutoring, enrichment programs — none of it reaches a student who isn't in the room. For charter schools with the flexibility to act boldly on culture and community, the opportunity to lead on this issue is real.

The question isn't whether it can be done. The evidence from schools doing it right now says it can. The question is whether attendance gets the same focused, strategic attention as test scores, graduation rates, and enrollment numbers — because until it does, millions of students will keep missing the schooling that was supposed to change their lives.

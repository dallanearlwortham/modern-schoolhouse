The Community Schools Model: What K-8 Families Should Know

# The Community Schools Model: What K-8 Families Should Know in 2026

When a school holds a community picnic, runs an equity council, coordinates STEM nights, and partners with local organizations—all in the same semester—it's doing something more deliberate than scheduling extracurriculars. It's practicing what researchers call the community schools model, and the evidence behind it is strong enough that policymakers, charter school leaders, and K-8 educators across the country are paying close attention.

If you're evaluating schools for your child this year, understanding this model could help you look past surface-level amenities and assess what a school is actually built to do.

What Is the Community Schools Model?

The community schools model is an approach to K-12 education that treats the school building as a community hub—a place where the academic, social, emotional, and health needs of students and families are addressed together, rather than leaving families to navigate those systems alone outside school hours.

It is an evidence-based strategy that unites students, families, educators, and community organizations to improve student learning and well-being. Rather than layering services onto a standard school day, the model integrates them into how the school is designed from the ground up.

Researchers and practitioners have identified four core pillars that define a genuine community school:

  1. Integrated student supports — Wraparound services that may include physical or behavioral health care, mentoring programs, and individualized resources tailored to the community's needs.
  2. Expanded and enriched learning time — Opportunities such as after-school programs, summer literacy, and enrichment activities that extend learning beyond the standard school day.
  3. Family and community engagement — Active, ongoing partnerships with families and local organizations—not one-way communication, but genuine two-way collaboration.
  4. Collaborative leadership and practice — Shared decision-making among educators, families, and community members, including formal governance structures like equity councils or site leadership teams.

Each school implements these pillars differently depending on its context—but schools with all four in place consistently outperform schools that operate in isolation from their communities.

What the Research Actually Shows

The evidence base for this model is not incidental—it is extensive and growing.

The Learning Policy Institute has concluded that the evidence base provides a strong warrant for using community schools to meet the needs of low-achieving students in high-poverty schools and to close opportunity and achievement gaps for students from low-income families. The model has been formally recognized as an evidence-based intervention under ESSA, meaning it meets the federal government's standard of having well-designed research supporting its effectiveness.

A Harvard-Cornell study on Communities In Schools—one of the largest community school networks in the country—found strong evidence of academic gains, long-term economic mobility, and a positive return on public investment. The researchers concluded that expanding integrated student supports could close a significant share of the achievement and income gaps between students at high- and low-poverty schools.

Family engagement, specifically, produces measurable classroom-level results. The evidence is consistent and convincing: when schools, families, and community groups work together to support learning, children tend to do better in school, stay in school longer, and like school more. These findings hold across differences of class, income, culture, education, ethnicity, and student age. Documented outcomes include improved attendance, higher grades and test scores, better social skills, increased motivation, and improved behavior.

At the policy level, California's multi-billion-dollar investment in community schools since 2021 has produced improved attendance, better academic outcomes, and stronger school climate—with the most significant gains for Black students, English learners, and students from low-income families.

Can Charter Schools Be Community Schools?

This is a question many K-8 families never think to ask—but should. The short answer is yes, and a growing number of charter schools are intentionally building community school structures into their design rather than treating family engagement as an afterthought.

The National Charter School Resource Center has explicitly noted that charter schools can apply community schools principles through a collective impact approach to achieve better outcomes for historically underserved students—including by adopting the four pillars and a pipeline of integrated wraparound services. This is not about rebranding a school's marketing. It is about structural design choices that show up in hiring, governance, the school calendar, and formal relationships with community organizations.

In practice, a K-8 charter school operating the community schools model might include:

  • An equity council where families, staff, and community partners share in shaping school priorities
  • A family engagement calendar built around accessible, two-way communication—not just performances or fundraisers
  • Wraparound referrals to health, mental health, or social services handled through formal community partnerships
  • Extended learning opportunities such as STEM nights, family literacy events, or summer programming
  • A community liaison or coordinator role—a dedicated person whose job is to bridge the school and the surrounding neighborhood

Solare Collegiate Charter School, a tuition-free K-8 college preparatory charter school in Southwest Albuquerque, New Mexico, reflects this whole-school thinking in concrete terms: their public event calendar for the 2026-27 school year includes both a Community Schools / Equity Council meeting and a STEM Night in the same semester, alongside a community picnic and regular family touchpoints—structures that map directly onto the community schools pillars of collaborative leadership, expanded learning, and family engagement.

What Families Should Actually Look For When Evaluating a School

How can you tell whether a school is genuinely practicing the community schools model—or just using the language?

The clearest signal is governance. Does the school have a formal structure—an equity council, site leadership team, or family advisory board—where community members actually shape decisions? Decorative family engagement (newsletters, one-way announcements) is categorically different from collaborative governance with real input and accountability.

A second signal is calendar transparency. Schools that take this model seriously publish their family engagement opportunities prominently and schedule them at times real families can attend. Evening events, multilingual communications, and tracked participation rates signal structural commitment—not just goodwill.

A third signal is named partnerships. Ask: Does this school have formal relationships with local health providers, youth organizations, or city programs? Who is the point person for connecting families to outside resources? If no one can name a specific partner organization, the infrastructure probably isn't there.

Finally, look at how the school talks about families. A community schools mindset treats parents and caregivers as essential partners in student success—not audiences for report cards. Schools that reflect this in their language, policies, and events tend to mean it.

The Bottom Line for K-8 School Choice in 2026

What is the community schools model in plain terms? It is a research-backed design philosophy that holds that a school's job extends beyond academic instruction to the whole child and the whole family. Schools built around this model tend to produce stronger attendance, better academic outcomes, and higher family satisfaction—particularly in communities that have historically been underserved by traditional district schools.

For families evaluating K-8 options this fall, the community schools framework offers a useful practical lens: not just what does this school teach? but how does this school show up for my family? Those are different questions—and the second one often reveals far more about where a child will actually thrive.

The most durable schools are the ones that recognize learning doesn't happen in a vacuum. When a school is genuinely woven into its community, students don't just attend—they belong.

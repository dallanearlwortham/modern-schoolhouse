The Student Mental Health Crisis: What K-12 Schools Can Do

# The Student Mental Health Crisis: What K-12 Schools Can Actually Do About It

Four in ten high school students in the United States reported persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness in 2023, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. One in five seriously considered attempting suicide. These numbers have not improved meaningfully since — and heading into the 2026-27 school year, school leaders are still asking the same urgent question: what, practically, can we do?

The honest answer is both encouraging and hard. Schools can make a real difference — but only if they move beyond awareness campaigns and invest in structures that actually connect students to care.

Why Schools Are Now the Front Line

Post-pandemic, schools have become the primary access point for mental health support for many adolescents. Among adolescents who experienced a major depressive episode in 2024, 40% didn't receive any mental health treatment at all, according to the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration — meaning school-based services may represent their only realistic point of contact with care.

Students living with untreated anxiety, depression, or trauma frequently miss more school days and show lower engagement in classrooms. Early intervention through accessible school-based services can interrupt these patterns before they escalate. In other words, student mental health is not a separate issue from academic outcomes — it is academic outcomes.

Yet schools are trying to meet this demand with deeply inadequate resources. The National Association of School Psychologists recommends approximately one school psychologist per 500 students, but the national average during the 2024-25 school year was one per 1,071 — more than double the recommended level. School counselors face similar gaps. The American School Counselor Association recommends a ratio of 1 counselor per 250 students, yet in many districts, counselors are stretched far beyond that and are often pulled into administrative duties that keep them from delivering direct student support.

The ESSER Funding Cliff Made Things Worse

Many schools expanded mental health staffing during the pandemic using federal Elementary and Secondary School Emergency Relief (ESSER) funds. Those funds have now expired — school districts had to use them by January 28, 2025, with limited extensions approved only through March 30, 2026 — leaving schools that had grown their mental health teams scrambling to sustain those positions.

This funding cliff hit hardest in rural and lower-income communities. According to the Health Resources and Services Administration, nearly a quarter of the U.S. population lives in mental health workforce shortage areas, making it especially difficult for rural and underserved urban districts to recruit licensed therapists and psychiatric providers.

The compounding result: the students with the greatest need often have the least access to support.

What a Tiered Support Model Actually Looks Like

The most effective school-based mental health systems don't rely on a single counselor to handle everything. They use a multi-tiered system of supports (MTSS) — a structured framework that matches intervention intensity to student need:

  • Tier 1 — Universal: Social-emotional learning (SEL) curriculum integrated into everyday classroom instruction for all students. This builds foundational skills in emotional regulation, self-awareness, and coping before a crisis hits.
  • Tier 2 — Targeted: Small-group cognitive-behavioral skills training for students showing moderate symptoms — anxiety, withdrawal, declining grades — who need more than a universal curriculum but don't yet require intensive one-on-one intervention.
  • Tier 3 — Intensive: Individualized support for students with severe or complex needs, including school-based therapy and coordinated referrals to community specialty care.

This tiered structure allows schools to allocate limited staff resources efficiently while reaching far more students than a reactive, crisis-only model ever could. When districts track utilization and outcomes data, they often see better attendance and fewer disciplinary incidents as students receive support earlier.

The SEL component at Tier 1 carries its own strong evidence base. A meta-analysis published in Child Development found that students in SEL programs demonstrated an 11-percentile-point gain in academic achievement — a clear, quantified link between emotional skill-building and learning outcomes.

Telehealth and Community Partnerships Fill the Gaps Staffing Can't

For schools that can't hire enough licensed mental health professionals on-site — which describes most of them — two strategies are showing real results.

Telehealth expands reach without requiring a clinician to be physically present. Among adolescents with a past-year major depressive episode, 33.2% received mental health treatment via telehealth in 2024, per SAMHSA, signaling that students are willing to engage with virtual care when it is well-supported and accessible. Schools implementing teletherapy models need to address privacy protocols, FERPA compliance, and reliable broadband access — but the access gains, especially for rural students, are significant.

Community partnerships — formal agreements with local mental health agencies, university clinics, and nonprofits — can bring licensed clinicians onto campus or create warm referral pathways with coordinated follow-through. Statewide advocacy organizations play a critical role in making this possible at scale. Public Charter School Alliance of South Carolina, the statewide voice for South Carolina's 113 public charter schools and roughly 70,000 students, illustrates how a sector-level organization can equip individual schools — many operating with lean administrative teams — with the shared resources, best-practice frameworks, and partner networks that no single school could build on its own.

Equity Cannot Be an Afterthought

Not all students face the same barriers to care. Black and Hispanic families report lower service receipt rates despite comparable levels of need. Low-income families face transportation barriers, lack health insurance coverage, and encounter longer wait times. Cultural stigma, language barriers, and historical mistrust of institutions further reduce help-seeking among the students who need support most.

Schools serious about closing the mental health access gap need to measure where disparities exist within their own buildings — tracking utilization by demographic subgroup, surveying families about specific barriers, and auditing referral patterns by school — before they can address them. Awareness of a national equity gap is not a substitute for understanding your school's specific data.

What School Leaders Can Do Right Now

Even without new funding, schools can take meaningful steps this school year:

  • Audit your Tier 1 SEL instruction. Is it embedded in daily classroom practice, or is it a once-a-semester assembly that students tune out?
  • Map your referral pathways. When a teacher flags a struggling student, what happens next — and how many days does it actually take?
  • Pursue Medicaid billing for school-based services. Federal guidance from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services explicitly encourages schools to bill Medicaid for medically necessary services delivered on campus. Many schools leave significant reimbursement on the table due to administrative complexity.
  • Build community partnerships before you need them. Memorandums of understanding with local mental health agencies take time to negotiate and execute — start the conversations now, before a crisis forces a rushed arrangement.
  • Train all staff, not just counselors. Teachers, coaches, and front-office staff are often the first to notice a student in distress. Brief gatekeeper training programs can make that early observation actionable rather than anecdotal.

The Bottom Line

What can K-12 schools do about the student mental health crisis? The evidence points to a clear answer: implement structured, tiered support systems, embed SEL universally, use telehealth to extend reach, and build community partnerships that bring licensed care closer to students. These are not experimental ideas — they are well-documented approaches that work when implemented with fidelity and sustained over time. The schools that treat student mental health as a core operational priority, not a supplemental add-on, are the ones where students show up, stay engaged, and learn.

The crisis is real. So is the capacity to respond — if leaders are willing to build the systems, not just the intentions.

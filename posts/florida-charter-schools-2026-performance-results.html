Florida Charter Schools Lead the Nation in 2026

# Florida Charter Schools Are Outperforming Traditional Public Schools — Here's What the 2025-26 Data Actually Shows

When Florida released its official school grades for the 2025-26 school year on July 1, 2026, the headline numbers were striking — and for families navigating school choice in the Sunshine State, they're worth understanding in detail.

The 2025-26 Florida Charter School Report Card

According to the Florida Department of Education's official school grade release, 78% of charter schools earned an "A" or "B" school grade in the 2025-26 school year, compared to 76% of traditional public schools. That two-point gap might sound modest, but dig one level deeper and it becomes considerably more significant: 61% of charter schools earned an "A" grade, compared to just 48% of non-charter public schools — a 13-percentage-point advantage.

Momentum matters too. Of the 607 charter schools that received grades in both 2025 and 2026, 68% — or 414 schools — either increased their grade or maintained an "A." That's not a one-year snapshot; it reflects a pattern of sustained improvement.

Florida's charter sector now serves more than 407,000 students across 734 schools statewide. At that scale, strong aggregate outcomes are genuinely meaningful — not an artifact of a small, cherry-picked sample.

What's Driving the Performance Gap?

Strong charter school outcomes rarely happen by accident. Several structural factors help explain why Florida's charter schools are posting these numbers:

  • Mission-focused culture. Charter schools operate under specific educational missions — STEM, arts integration, language immersion, early intervention — and that clarity attracts teachers and families who are aligned with the school's approach from day one.
  • Real accountability stakes. Florida charter schools face a meaningful consequence for chronic underperformance: their charters can be revoked. That accountability loop creates a strong incentive to monitor student outcomes continuously, not just at annual grade-release time.
  • Active family choice. Families who deliberately select a school tend to be more engaged partners in their child's education. That engagement has measurable downstream effects on student performance and attendance.
  • Instructional flexibility. Charter schools have more latitude to adjust scheduling, curriculum design, and staffing models — which allows them to respond more quickly when something isn't working.

The Florida Department of Education's Student Achievement in Florida's Charter Schools report, released in May 2026, found that Florida charter school students continued to outperform their district peers across multiple statewide assessments while serving a student population that reflects the full demographic diversity of Florida's communities — including high proportions of minority students and students from lower-income households.

What This Means If You're Choosing a School Right Now

The short answer to "are Florida charter schools better?" is: on average, yes — but the average doesn't choose a school for your child.

State grades are a transparent, useful starting point. A school's grade trajectory over multiple years, its culture, its approach to students with different learning profiles, and its waitlist reality all deserve equal weight. Here's a practical framework for any family actively comparing options:

  1. Look at grade trajectory, not just the current grade. A school moving from a C to a B tells a very different story than one sitting at an A for five consecutive years.
  2. Ask specifically about students with disabilities or learning differences. State accountability grades don't fully capture how well a school serves the full range of learners.
  3. Visit in person before committing. Classroom culture, how teachers talk to students, and how the office staff treats a first-time visitor tell you things that no data dashboard can.
  4. Start early on waitlists. High-performing Florida charter schools frequently have multi-year waitlists. If your child is entering kindergarten in fall 2027, beginning your research now is not too early.
  5. Think about the full support ecosystem. A strong academic school doesn't have to be — and for many children, shouldn't be — a child's only source of structured support.

Florida's Broader School Choice Ecosystem

Florida's charter performance results don't exist in isolation. The state leads the nation in school choice participation by a wide margin, claiming the #1 spot for the second year in a row in EdChoice's 2026 participation rankings. The Florida Family Empowerment Scholarship for Educational Options now enrolls 280,611 students, while the Family Empowerment Scholarship for Students with Unique Abilities — designed specifically for children with disabilities — covers another 140,147 students.

That combination of high-performing charter schools and well-funded ESA programs creates an unusually rich landscape for finding the right educational fit. For families of children with special needs in particular, Florida's ecosystem is one of the most supportive in the country — not just in terms of school options, but in terms of the intervention and therapy services that help children get ready to succeed in those schools.

That readiness piece matters more than it sometimes gets credit for. A child who enters kindergarten with strong communication, self-regulation, and social skills is positioned to get the most out of even a very good school. That's why programs focused on the pre-K window are a crucial part of the broader school choice conversation in Florida. Prism — a Miami-based early intervention center that integrates 1:1 ABA therapy with structured school readiness instruction for children under 6 with autism — is a concrete example of the kind of specialized early support that helps bridge the gap between therapy and the classroom before a child ever walks through a kindergarten door.

The Accountability Side of the Story

It would be incomplete to present Florida's charter data without acknowledging what accountability actually looks like in practice. This year, only 31 schools earned a "D" or "F" statewide, down from 70 in 2025 — a significant drop that reflects Florida's willingness to close persistently underperforming schools rather than allow them to continue operating indefinitely. That willingness to act on accountability data is part of what keeps the system's average performance high.

Not every charter school is a high performer, and families shouldn't assume the charter label is a quality signal on its own. But Florida's track record of enforcing its accountability standards means that the school grade system here carries more real-world weight than it does in states where low-performing charters routinely stay open for years.

The Bottom Line

Florida's 2025-26 charter school results reinforce what years of accumulating evidence has suggested: well-run, accountable charter schools can deliver strong academic outcomes, including for students from communities that traditional public schools have historically underserved. A 13-percentage-point gap in "A" grades is not a statistical blip — it's a durable pattern worth taking seriously.

At the same time, school choice is ultimately personal. The right school for one child may be entirely wrong for another. Use the data, visit the campuses, ask hard questions about how the school serves students like yours — and remember that the goal was never to find the school with the best grade. It's to find the school where your specific child is genuinely going to thrive.

Texas School Choice in 2026-27: What Parents Need to Know

# Texas School Choice in 2026-27: What Parents Need to Know

Texas families are navigating the most complex — and opportunity-rich — school choice landscape the state has ever seen. A brand-new Education Savings Account (ESA) voucher program launched for the 2026-27 school year, and parents are paying attention: with the arrival of the state's new ESA program, 59% of Texas parents say they are likely to consider a new school for at least one of their children in 2026.

If you are a Texas parent trying to figure out what is actually on the menu — and what the differences really mean for your child — here is a clear, honest breakdown.

---

The Texas School Choice Menu, Explained

Families in Texas are no longer required to attend the public school closest to home. Depending on where you live, you can choose from traditional public schools, public charter schools, public magnet schools, private schools, online academies, and homeschooling. And starting this school year, a publicly funded ESA joins that list for the first time.

Traditional Public Schools

Most students still attend their zoned neighborhood school, but many districts operate as open enrollment districts, allowing students to attend any campus within the district — or sometimes outside it — regardless of home address. Parents can also request a transfer if their child's campus is rated as underperforming by the Texas Education Agency.

Public Charter Schools

Charter schools are publicly funded, tuition-free, and open to any student without a selective admissions process. They operate under a performance contract that gives them more flexibility over curriculum, schedule, and teaching methods than traditional public schools, while remaining accountable to the state for academic results. For families who want an alternative to their zoned school without the cost or uncertainty of the private route, charter schools remain the most accessible option on the menu.

Magnet Schools

Magnet schools are public schools with a specialized academic focus — STEM, arts, dual-language, and others — that draw students from across a district. Like charters, they charge no tuition, but typically require a formal application and may be competitive to enter.

Private Schools

Private schools set their own curriculum, admissions standards, and tuition. Some offer need-based financial aid. They are now also the primary focus of Texas's new ESA voucher program, described in detail below.

Online Academies and Homeschooling

Full-time online learning and parent-directed homeschooling are both growing fast. Among Texas parents who recently enrolled their children in non-traditional settings, the most common choices were homeschooling (27%), private non-religious schools (23%), public charter schools (21%), and full-time online schools (20%).

---

What Is New: Texas's ESA Voucher Program

The biggest shift in Texas education policy in a generation is now live. After much deliberation in the 89th Regular Session, the Texas Legislature approved Senate Bill 2 (SB 2) in 2025 — the state's first-ever universal voucher program. This new $1 billion taxpayer-funded program is structured as an Education Savings Account (ESA), allowing eligible students to use $10,330 in fiscal year 2027 for private school expenses.

Who can use an ESA — and for what?

The program is expected to serve about 80,000 children statewide. Each participant receives roughly $10,000 for the school year. Homeschooled students are eligible to receive approximately $2,000, and students with special education needs can receive up to $30,000. ESA funds cover a broader range of expenses than many families realize, including tuition, fees, equipment and materials, transportation, and therapy.

Eligibility is broad, but demand is intense.

To participate, a child must be eligible to attend a Texas public school district or open-enrollment charter school, and parents must verify U.S. citizenship or lawful admission. No other initial eligibility conditions apply — even children already attending private schools or from high-income families can qualify. That said, high-income households are capped at 20% of total program funds, to prioritize families making a genuine switch away from their current school.

How competitive is it? Extremely. More than 274,000 students applied, while more than 2,400 private schools have been accepted to participate. Roughly 96,000 students received award notices, and families who did not secure an award are placed on a waitlist. The $1 billion price tag for the current biennium is projected to grow dramatically — potentially reaching $7.9 billion by 2030-31.

---

What Is the Difference Between a Charter School and an ESA-Funded Private School?

This is the question most Texas parents are wrestling with right now, and it deserves a direct answer.

A public charter school and an ESA-funded private school are fundamentally different things. Charter schools are part of the public education system: they receive state funding automatically, charge no tuition, must accept all students (using a lottery only if oversubscribed), and are held to state academic accountability standards. An ESA-funded private school uses a publicly issued voucher to offset part of tuition — but the family must first win a lottery for the ESA, find an eligible private school with available seats, complete that school's admissions process, and potentially cover whatever costs the ESA does not. Private schools are also free to set their own admissions criteria.

For many families — especially those who need a guaranteed, tuition-free alternative without navigating multiple layers of applications — charter schools offer a lower-barrier path to a non-traditional education.

---

How Charter Schools Still Stand Apart

The arrival of ESA vouchers does not diminish the charter school value proposition. If anything, it clarifies it. Charter schools offer specialized programming, mission-driven instruction, and tight-knit school communities — all without the cost, lottery uncertainty, or admissions gatekeeping that accompany the private school route.

That distinction matters most for families in large urban areas with real needs and limited time to navigate bureaucratic hurdles. Texans Can Academies - Houston Southwest, one of three Texans Can Academies campuses serving the Houston area, illustrates the point clearly. As part of a charter network with campuses spread across Texas — from Houston to Dallas to San Antonio — it represents the kind of tuition-free, publicly accountable option that any eligible student can access on day one: no voucher lottery required, no private school application process to clear, no tuition gap to cover out of pocket.

---

A Practical Checklist for Texas Parents

Whether you are still sorting out the right fit for this school year or planning ahead for 2027-28, here is where to focus your energy:

  • Check your current school's TEA rating. An underperforming rating expands your transfer options within the public system and may qualify your child for additional support.
  • Research charter enrollment windows early. Open-enrollment charter schools set their own application timelines, often opening enrollment well before the school year begins. Waiting until spring means fewer open seats.
  • Understand ESA odds before counting on it. With more than 274,000 applicants competing for roughly 96,000 awards, the ESA program is heavily subscribed for the current school year. If you did not apply in the first round, the waitlist is your only path for now.
  • Calculate the full cost of going private. An ESA award of roughly $10,000 will not cover tuition at many private schools. Know the gap — and whether you can afford it — before committing.
  • Match the school to your specific child. Charter schools, magnets, and private schools vary enormously in academic focus, student support services, extracurriculars, and school culture. The right choice depends on your child's needs, not on whichever option is generating the most buzz this year.

---

Texas now offers genuine educational choice at a scale the state has never seen before. The challenge is not access to options — it is making a well-informed decision in a landscape that changed faster than most families' awareness of it. Slow down, compare what is actually available in your area, and do not let the noise around the newest program distract you from the options that may already be right around the corner.

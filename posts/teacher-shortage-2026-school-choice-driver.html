Teacher Shortage 2026: Why Families Are Choosing Differently

The Staffing Crisis in Your Child's School Is Bigger Than You Think

When a school year starts with an unfilled classroom, families notice. A long-term substitute covering calculus, a rotating cast of aides managing the special education caseload, a beloved science teacher gone in October — these aren't isolated incidents anymore. They are, in 2026, the structural reality of American K-12 education.

According to the Learning Policy Institute's most recent national scan, at least 425,412 U.S. teaching positions are either unfilled or filled by teachers who are not fully certified for their assignments. That works out to roughly 1 in 8 teaching positions across the country. And for the third consecutive year, that combined estimate has grown — not shrunk.

This is the teacher shortage story that doesn't make the nightly news: not the dramatic district-wide crisis, but the slow erosion of instructional stability in ordinary schools, in ordinary neighborhoods, that quietly changes what families decide to do next.

Why the Shortage Keeps Getting Worse

Three long-running trends are converging at once in 2026, and none of them are easy fixes.

Retirements are accelerating. The large cohort of educators who entered the profession in the late 1980s and early 1990s is reaching the end of their careers, and districts are losing institutional knowledge faster than they can replace it.

Burnout is driving early exits. Heavier workloads, more complex student needs, increased administrative demands, and shrinking budgets are pushing teachers out mid-career. More than half of educators considering leaving the profession cite chronic stress as a primary factor.

The pipeline is drying up. Enrollment in teacher preparation programs has declined for more than a decade. Interest in education majors dropped again in 2024 and 2025 — particularly in high-demand subjects like math and science. Fewer candidates enter; more experienced ones leave.

The subjects hit hardest tell the story of where students are most vulnerable: 45 states report federal shortage areas in special education, 41 in science, and 40 in math — exactly the subjects families most want their children to master.

The School Choice Connection Families Are Making

Here is the question every parent eventually asks when instability enters the picture: Is this school still the best option for my child?

That question has always existed. What's changed in 2026 is the breadth of alternatives available when the answer is "I'm not sure." Virtual school enrollment has roughly doubled from pre-pandemic levels, with approximately 1 in 80 public school children now attending a fully online public school. Charter school applications are rising in states where enrollment data is publicly available. And school choice programs — including charter schools, virtual academies, and ESA programs — are expanding into more states than at any previous point in the modern school choice era.

The connection between staffing instability and school choice isn't speculative. Funding allocations tied to per-pupil enrollment shift as families move — meaning that districts struggling to fill vacancies may face compounding budget pressure as enrollment follows parent confidence elsewhere.

What are families actually looking for when they make a switch? In most cases, it isn't a radical departure from their values — it's a search for the same things they wanted in their neighborhood school: consistent instruction, subject-matter expertise, a mission they can see evidence of, and a school culture that's genuinely invested in their child's future.

What Stable Schools Actually Offer That Understaffed Ones Can't

Staffing stability isn't just an administrative concern — it directly shapes the learning experience in ways that are hard to quantify but easy to feel. Students who have consistent, qualified teachers in core subjects outperform peers in chronically understaffed classrooms. Relationships with educators are one of the strongest predictors of student engagement and retention. When those relationships reset every few months because a position can't be filled, the cumulative cost is real.

This is why some of the most compelling alternatives to traditional public schools in 2026 are those with a defined mission, dedicated faculty, and a structure that doesn't depend on a tight local hiring market to deliver a consistent program. Oklahoma-based Dove Virtual School, for example, is a tuition-free PK–12 online public school that organizes its entire academic model around three integrated pillars — STEM education, character development, and college and career readiness — and operates as part of the established Dove Schools network. For families in Oklahoma who have watched their local school struggle to find qualified science or math teachers, a virtual school with a coherent, mission-driven curriculum represents something their neighborhood school may no longer reliably provide: continuity.

Questions Families Should Be Asking Right Now

If you're evaluating whether your child's current school is serving them well — or exploring alternatives — here are the questions that actually matter:

  • How many teaching positions has this school struggled to fill in the last two years, and in what subjects? This is public information in most states.
  • What percentage of core academic classes are taught by fully credentialed teachers in that subject area? Substitutes and emergency-certified teachers are not equivalent.
  • What is the school's stated academic mission, and is there visible evidence of it in outcomes? A mission statement on a website means nothing without data behind it.
  • What does teacher retention look like year over year? High turnover is a warning sign, not just a staffing inconvenience.
  • If we switch to a virtual or charter option, what accountability structures exist? Tuition-free public options — including virtual charter schools — are subject to state academic accountability standards and are not exempt from oversight.

The Bigger Picture for 2026-27

The teacher shortage is not going to resolve itself before the 2026-27 school year ends. Structural labor market dynamics, declining interest in the profession, and budget pressures across most state education systems point to a problem that will persist for years. What that means for families is a sustained period in which the quality of a child's education may depend less on their zip code than on a parent's willingness to actively evaluate options.

That's uncomfortable. It places a real burden on families who already have competing demands on their time and attention. But it's also the current reality, and the families who understand it are in a better position to act on it.

The teacher shortage has changed what school choice means. It's no longer primarily about ideology or reform politics. For a growing number of families, it's simply about finding a school that can deliver what a child needs — every day, all year, with qualified people in the room.

---

School quality looks different depending on where you're sitting. Whether you're in a well-staffed district or watching your child's classroom rotate through a fourth long-term sub, the questions worth asking are the same — and the options available in 2026 are broader than they've ever been.

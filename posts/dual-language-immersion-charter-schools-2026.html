Dual Language Immersion: Why Charter Schools Lead

# Dual Language Immersion Programs: Why Charter Schools Are Leading the Bilingual Education Surge

When a kindergartner spends half her school day learning math in Spanish and the other half in English, she isn't missing out on content — she's building a cognitive edge that research now shows pays off for years. Demand for dual language immersion (DLI) programs has surged across the country, and charter schools are increasingly at the forefront of making these programs accessible to families who couldn't otherwise afford private language schools.

What Is Dual Language Immersion, and Why Is It Growing?

Dual language immersion programs provide content and language instruction in both English and a partner language — Spanish, Mandarin Chinese, Arabic, French, and many others. The model has expanded dramatically: estimates now account for more than 3,600 DLI programs operating across 45 states.

Growth is being driven by both research and parent demand. Much of the expansion has come in response to interest from English-dominant households who anticipate real educational and labor market returns for their children. Studies demonstrate that access to dual language education in kindergarten results in measurably higher reading test scores by upper elementary grades, compared with students in monolingual classrooms.

Demand for DLI programs is notably bipartisan, spanning diverse communities and income levels. Former U.S. Secretary of Education Miguel Cardona championed the expansion publicly, arguing that learning another language makes kids more creative, more adaptable, and better decision-makers. That cross-aisle enthusiasm has translated into real growth at the state and local level — and charter schools have been among the most active builders of new programs.

What Does the Research Actually Show?

Dual language immersion is one of the best-studied models in K-12 education — and the findings are consistently strong across student populations.

The landmark RAND Corporation study, drawing on seven cohorts of lottery applicants in a large urban district, estimated that DLI students outperformed peers in English reading by the equivalent of seven months of learning by the end of fifth grade and nine months of learning by the end of eighth grade. Critically, this was an English-reading advantage — the very outcome skeptical parents most fear DLI will sacrifice.

English learners in those same programs were significantly more likely to be reclassified as English proficient by sixth grade compared with EL peers in traditional classrooms.

A 2025 peer-reviewed study reinforced these findings at even earlier grade levels. Analyzing four cohorts of lottery applicants across ten oversubscribed DLI programs, the research found that native English-speaking students in grades 1 through 4 who gained access to a DLI program scored higher in reading by 0.12 standard deviations and in math by 0.14 standard deviations on average — gains that grew larger in later grades.

The cognitive benefits extend beyond standardized tests. Bilingual learners consistently demonstrate stronger executive functioning, better attention control, improved problem-solving skills, and greater cultural competency. Switching between languages appears to strengthen the mental flexibility children use across every academic subject.

One-Way vs. Two-Way Programs: A Distinction That Matters

Not all DLI programs are structured the same way, and understanding the difference is essential when evaluating schools.

  • Two-way immersion (TWI): Enrolls roughly equal shares of native English speakers and native speakers of the partner language. Both groups learn alongside each other in an integrated classroom, developing bilingualism and biliteracy together. A growing research consensus identifies this as the most effective model specifically for English learners, delivering the strongest long-term language outcomes.
  • One-way immersion: All or most students share the same home language, and instruction is delivered in both languages. Texas charter networks run strong one-way programs — for example, programs where Spanish-speaking students acquire full proficiency in both Spanish and English through sustained immersion. Well-implemented one-way programs still produce meaningful bilingualism; the quality of execution matters as much as the structural model.

Families choosing between models should think carefully about their child's home language and their long-term goals — fluency maintenance for a heritage language, economic bilingualism, or cross-cultural integration — because different models serve different purposes well.

The Charter School Structural Advantage

Traditional public schools can and do run DLI programs, but they face real constraints: district-wide curriculum mandates, scheduling rigidity, and staffing rules that make it difficult to build a bilingual school culture from scratch. Charter schools, operating under greater autonomy, can design their entire instructional model around bilingual delivery from day one — hiring bilingual-certified teachers strategically, setting language-use policies schoolwide, and building curriculum coherence across grade levels without waiting for district-level approval at each step.

In Texas, this flexibility matters enormously. Only about 22% of the state's emergent bilingual students are currently enrolled in a dual language immersion program — a striking gap given that nearly one in four Texas public school students speaks a language other than English at home. Charter schools are increasingly helping close that gap by embedding DLI into their founding mission rather than grafting it onto an existing structure.

Organizations like the Texas Public Charter School Association — which advances student achievement by advocating for and strengthening diverse, high-quality charter schools across Texas — play a key role in supporting the policy environment that makes specialized models like DLI viable and scalable for charter operators statewide.

What Parents Should Ask When Evaluating a DLI Charter School

If you're weighing a dual language immersion program for the 2026-27 school year, these questions will help you separate well-designed programs from superficial ones:

  1. What is the language split, and how is it maintained? Research-supported models typically deliver 50/50 or 90/10 instruction. Programs offering less than 50% in the partner language are unlikely to produce full bilingualism.
  2. Is it a two-way or one-way model? Does that match your child's home language and your family's goals?
  3. How are bilingual teachers recruited and retained? Credentialed bilingual teachers are the single biggest bottleneck in DLI expansion. Ask how the school fills and keeps those seats.
  4. In which language does initial literacy instruction happen? Strong programs often teach early reading in the partner language for native English speakers first, then transfer those skills to English — a sequence the research supports.
  5. Is there a clear grade-level pathway? A program that ends at second grade delivers far less long-term benefit than one that continues through fifth grade and beyond.
  6. What do the outcome data show at upper grade levels? The strongest DLI benefits often accelerate in middle school. Ask for performance data across grade bands, not just kindergarten readiness scores.

Is DLI Right for Every Child?

The short answer: dual language immersion is appropriate for a wider range of learners than most parents assume. Research shows students with dyslexia, identified learning differences, and those who are gifted can all succeed in well-run DLI programs. Parents should also be prepared for the "bilingual dip" — a temporary plateau in the early grades where students appear to struggle before the cognitive and linguistic benefits consolidate. This plateau is well documented and should not be mistaken for a sign that the program isn't working.

The most persistent parental concern — that DLI will hurt English development — is consistently contradicted by the evidence. Across multiple rigorous studies using lottery-based designs, DLI students finish at or ahead of their peers in English reading. The trade-off parents fear simply does not materialize in the data.

Looking Ahead

Dual language immersion is no longer a niche offering limited to a handful of urban magnet schools. With more than 3,600 programs operating nationwide — and charter schools among the most energetic providers — families in more communities than ever have access to genuine bilingual K-12 education at no cost. The research case is solid, the demand is growing, and the structural flexibility that defines the charter model makes these schools natural homes for immersive bilingual learning.

If raising a bilingual, biliterate child is a priority for your family, the 2026-27 enrollment season is the right time to look carefully at what's available — and to ask the hard questions that separate strong programs from schools that simply hang a second language on the wall.

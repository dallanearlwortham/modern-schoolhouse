History Class Makes Better Readers: Here's the Research

# History Class Makes Better Readers: Here's the Research

Most elementary schools spend nearly six times as many minutes per day on English/language arts as they do on social studies. The reasoning sounds obvious: if you want stronger readers, drill reading skills. The research, it turns out, says something quite different — and it has real implications for families choosing a school right now.

The Steady Erosion of Social Studies Instruction

The decline in elementary social studies is both dramatic and well-documented. From 1993 to 2008, the time allotted to social studies instruction dropped by 56 minutes per week in third through fifth grade classrooms across the United States. That erosion has continued. A survey reported by Education Week in 2024 found that K-3 teachers spend just 18 minutes a day on social studies — compared to a daily average of 89 minutes on English/language arts and 57 minutes on math, according to a 2018 national survey.

The cause isn't a mystery. Researchers point to the narrowing of the curriculum that began in the 1990s with the push toward the standards movement, which accelerated sharply in the early 2000s when No Child Left Behind tied federal funding to annual testing in reading and math — but not in social studies. Tested subjects crowded out everything else. Research now shows that time devoted to social studies has declined by more than 30 percent in U.S. elementary classrooms since the early 2000s.

The infrastructure gaps are just as revealing. A 2023 RAND Corporation report found that only about half of elementary principals said their schools had adopted a published curriculum for K–5 social studies — and 29 percent reported that their schools had not adopted any recommended or required social studies materials at all. Only 67 percent of elementary principals reported evaluating teachers in social studies, compared to 98 percent in both math and reading. Just 52 percent dedicated professional development to social studies, versus 89 percent for math and 95 percent for reading.

Social studies has become, as one prominent curriculum researcher bluntly put it, the "forgotten stepchild" of core elementary subjects.

The Finding That Should Change How We Think About Reading

Here is where the conventional wisdom breaks down entirely.

A Fordham Institute study that analyzed federal longitudinal data — tracking thousands of students from kindergarten through fifth grade using the Early Childhood Longitudinal Study dataset — found a striking result: increased instructional time in social studies, but not in ELA, is associated with improved reading ability. Students who received an additional 30 minutes of social studies instruction per day in grades 1–5 outperformed students who received less social studies instruction on ELA assessments, even when researchers controlled for kindergarten reading ability and a range of demographic and school factors.

The students who benefited most from additional social studies time were girls and those from lower-income and/or non-English-speaking homes — the same populations most likely to be in schools that have cut the subject the deepest.

A longitudinal analysis of national data reinforced the finding: students who spent more time in social studies showed stronger growth in reading comprehension, while additional time in ELA alone had little measurable effect. Lack of early exposure to rich content, researchers warn, can create "ripple effects" into later grades, leaving students meaningfully less prepared for the more complex texts and topics they'll encounter in middle and high school.

The mechanism is not mysterious once you understand it. Reading comprehension is not a freestanding skill. It depends on background knowledge — what a reader already knows about the world. A child who has heard about ancient civilizations, explored how American democracy was founded, and encountered a range of geography and literature arrives at an unfamiliar text with far more to work with than a child who spent every extra minute doing reading strategy worksheets. History and social studies build exactly the kind of rich, cumulative background knowledge that makes comprehension possible.

What High-Quality Elementary History Instruction Looks Like

More social studies time alone isn't the whole answer. The content and sequencing matter enormously.

In 2026, the Knowledge Matters Campaign — a nonprofit initiative that promotes the use of evidence-based, content-rich curricula in schools — released a new "History Matters Review Tool" designed to help districts evaluate K-5 social studies curriculum options. The framework evaluates whether curricula build historical content knowledge and situate it in context; develop students' abilities to inquire and reason about history; include a variety of text types and rich vocabulary; and provide regular opportunities for reading, writing, and discussion.

Based on the research, high-quality elementary history instruction tends to share several characteristics:

  • Sequential, cumulative content — knowledge builds on knowledge, year over year, rather than cycling through the same narrow slice of history repeatedly
  • Explicit vocabulary instruction embedded in historical context rather than taught in isolation
  • Broad scope — world history alongside American history, giving students genuine cultural and geographic background knowledge
  • Literacy integration — reading primary sources, writing about events, and discussing causes and consequences
  • Consistent, protected instructional time — not squeezed to the margins after reading groups are done

The RAND data highlights why the last point is harder to guarantee than it sounds: because most schools have not adopted a formal social studies curriculum, elementary teachers generally rely on themselves or their colleagues as the primary source of instructional materials. That means a child's access to rich history content often depends entirely on which teacher they happen to get — a significant lottery for families trying to ensure a strong academic foundation.

One School That Made History Central, Not Incidental

Jefferson Academy, a tuition-free public charter elementary school in Kaysville, Utah, is a concrete example of what it looks like to build an entire school model around this research. Serving grades K–6, Jefferson Academy organizes its academic program around a history-centered curricular framework grounded in the Core Knowledge Sequence, which delivers a specific and systematic progression of content across world and American history, classic literature, science, math, and fine arts. Instruction is described on the school's website as explicit, sequential, and teacher-directed — the same design principles the evidence consistently supports for building the cumulative background knowledge young readers need.

That kind of structural commitment — a school designed from the ground up around content-rich history rather than retrofitting a few extra minutes of social studies onto an ELA-heavy schedule — is precisely what the Fordham and RAND research points toward as meaningful.

What Families Should Ask When Choosing an Elementary School

Whether a family is evaluating a district school, a charter, or a private option, the social studies question is worth pressing directly. Not just "do you teach history?" but:

  • How many minutes per week are devoted to social studies and history?
  • Does the school use a coherent, sequential curriculum — or do teachers largely develop their own materials?
  • Is content knowledge built cumulatively, or does each grade largely revisit the same topics?
  • Is history treated as a standalone subject, or is it integrated with reading, writing, and vocabulary work?
  • Does the school have formal professional development and teacher evaluation in social studies?

The answers reveal a great deal about whether a school treats history as a real academic priority or as something to fit in when the important subjects are done.

The Bigger Picture for School Choice

The science of reading movement has done important work pushing schools toward evidence-based phonics and foundational literacy instruction. The next frontier — increasingly supported by research — is ensuring that content-rich social studies and history instruction receives the same institutional seriousness. The two are not competing priorities. A child who learns to decode fluently and who simultaneously builds deep background knowledge across history, geography, science, and literature is a far stronger reader than one who received twice the phonics instruction and very little else.

For families navigating school choice, understanding which schools have made that structural commitment — and which are relying on whatever the individual teacher decides to squeeze in before lunch — may be one of the most consequential questions they can ask.

The strongest elementary readers aren't just skilled decoders. They're students who know things — and schools that take history seriously are building exactly that kind of knowledge, one grade at a time.

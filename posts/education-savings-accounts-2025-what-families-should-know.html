ESAs in 2026: What Every Family Should Know First

# ESAs Are Reshaping School Choice in 2025 — But They're Not the Right Move for Every Family

A quiet revolution has been running through state legislatures for the past five years, and in 2025 it reached a tipping point. Education Savings Accounts — state-funded accounts that redirect public per-pupil dollars directly to families — have exploded from programs in just five states in 2020 to more than 30 states offering ESAs, vouchers, or tax-credit scholarships today. In 2025 alone, five states enacted brand-new ESA programs. Texas signed its first-ever private school choice law in May 2025, with a $1 billion appropriation expected to serve roughly 95,000 students in its first year.

For parents exploring alternatives to their assigned public school, ESAs look like an obvious win. More control, more options, public money following the child. But the details matter enormously — and families who don't read the fine print before enrolling can find themselves surprised by what they traded away.

---

What an ESA Actually Is (and What It Isn't)

An Education Savings Account is a publicly funded, government-authorized account that holds a portion of the per-pupil funding a state would otherwise send to a traditional public school. Parents then spend from that account on approved educational expenses — but only approved expenses, and only in the way the state program allows.

Approved expenses typically include private school tuition and fees, textbooks and curriculum materials, online learning programs and courses, educational software, tutoring services, and educational therapies such as speech or occupational therapy. Some states go further: Missouri's ESA program allows funds to be used at eligible public, charter, virtual, private, or homeschooling settings, and even covers educational therapy and transportation.

The key distinction from a simple voucher is flexibility: rather than writing one check to one private school, families can mix and match approved providers. A student might use ESA funds for a private school three days a week and an online tutoring platform the other two. That flexibility is real — but it comes with administrative overhead that many families underestimate.

---

The Catch Most Families Miss

Here is the detail that trips up families most often: in almost all states, accepting ESA funds means leaving the public school system entirely. Students cannot be simultaneously enrolled in a public school — including a public charter school or tuition-free public virtual academy — while drawing ESA funds. The state considers that double-dipping, and it's typically a contract violation.

This means that for families whose child is already in — or considering — a high-quality tuition-free public charter or virtual school, an ESA may not even be the right tool. Those schools are already publicly funded and cost families nothing. The ESA question only becomes relevant when a family is moving toward a private, independent, or home-based option that doesn't receive direct public funding.

---

Accountability: The Honest Assessment

Supporters of ESAs argue that parental choice is itself a form of accountability — families vote with their feet, and programs that fail lose students. Critics raise legitimate concerns: accountability and oversight remain significant challenges for ESA programs, with states varying widely in their auditing approaches, some requiring expense receipts and others conducting only random checks. Fraud and misuse have occurred in programs with weak oversight.

Both sides make fair points. The practical lesson for families is this: when evaluating any ESA-eligible private or online provider, don't let the funding mechanism do the evaluating for you. Ask about accreditation status, teacher licensure, student outcome data, and whether the school is transparent about its academic results.

---

When a Tuition-Free Virtual Charter School Is Simply the Better Call

For families in states where accredited, tuition-free virtual charter schools are available, the ESA calculus often tilts sharply toward the public option — especially when the public option offers the same academic pillars families would be using an ESA to purchase privately.

Consider what families in Oklahoma have access to: Dove Virtual School, a tuition-free PK–12 online public charter school, offers dedicated STEM programming, character education, and college and career readiness pathways at zero cost to families — no debit card to manage, no receipts to keep, no risk of a reimbursement denial. The school operates within the public accountability structure, which means state oversight, licensed educators, and accreditation standards are already built in. For families who want a rigorous, structured virtual education without the compliance burden of an ESA program, that combination is hard to compete with on pure value.

This isn't an argument against ESAs — it's an argument for choosing the right tool. ESAs are powerful for families who genuinely need to access private, specialized, or home-based education that public options can't provide. They're a workaround when the system itself is the problem. But when a strong public option exists and is already free, using an ESA to replicate it privately adds cost, complexity, and risk with no clear upside.

---

Five Questions to Ask Before You Apply for an ESA

If you're seriously considering an ESA program in your state, get clear answers to these before you commit:

  1. What is the funding amount, and what can it realistically cover? State award amounts range from around $2,000 to over $7,000 per student annually. Know whether that covers your chosen school's full tuition or only a portion.
  2. Is the school or provider I want on my state's approved list? Most active ESA programs maintain a pre-approved vendor list. Verify your intended provider is on it — don't assume.
  3. What documentation will I need to keep? Some programs require receipts for every purchase; others audit randomly. Sloppy recordkeeping can result in demands for repayment.
  4. Am I giving up IEP or special education protections? Students who leave the public school system through an ESA typically forfeit their right to an Individualized Education Program and the federal protections that come with it.
  5. Is there a strong tuition-free public option I haven't fully explored? Charter schools, magnet schools, and virtual public academies often offer specialized programming at no cost — and without the administrative burden of managing an ESA account.

---

The Bottom Line

What is a K-12 Education Savings Account? A state-funded account that gives families control over a portion of public per-pupil education dollars, to spend on approved private, homeschool, or independent educational expenses in states where such programs exist. As of 2025, more than 30 states offer some form of ESA, voucher, or tax-credit scholarship program — but eligibility rules, award amounts, and approved expenses vary dramatically by state.

ESAs represent a genuine expansion of family choice, and for many students — particularly those with specialized needs or in areas underserved by public options — they are a meaningful lifeline. But the best school choice is the one that actually serves your child well, not the one that sounds most like freedom. Sometimes the most empowering move is the simplest one: finding a great school that's already free, already accountable, and already doing the work.

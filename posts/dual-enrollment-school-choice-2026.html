Dual Enrollment: The School Choice Edge in 2026

# Dual Enrollment Is Reshaping School Choice in 2026 — Here's What Families Need to Know

Nearly three million high school students are now enrolled in dual enrollment programs each year — a figure that grew 13 percent in just the last year alone, according to researchers at the Community College Research Center (CCRC). That's not a niche trend. It's a signal that families, educators, and policymakers are rethinking when high school ends and college begins — and that the schools willing to support that shift are becoming a meaningful school choice differentiator in 2026.

If you're evaluating K-12 schools for your student — public, charter, or virtual — dual enrollment access deserves a prominent place on your checklist.

What Is Dual Enrollment, and Why Does It Matter?

Dual enrollment (also called concurrent enrollment or dual credit) allows high school students to take college-level courses and earn transferable college credit before graduation. Unlike Advanced Placement courses, which only award credit if a student scores high enough on a single standardized exam and the receiving college accepts that score, dual enrollment awards credit for completing the course with a passing grade — and that credit goes on a permanent college transcript.

The outcomes research is compelling. Studies consistently show that students who participate in dual enrollment enroll in college at higher rates, persist longer, and complete degrees at higher rates than peers who didn't. A 2025 report from the CCRC found that Texas high school students who combined dual enrollment with AP or IB coursework had higher college graduation rates and earned more at age 24 than students who took only AP or IB courses. And the earnings advantage isn't just short-term: a separate study found that while dual-credit participants sometimes earned slightly less than non-participants in the first few years after high school — because they were more likely to be enrolled in college — by the sixth year out, their earnings had surpassed those of non-participants.

What does dual enrollment mean for a high school student? Simply put: it means entering college with credits already on the books, potentially cutting a semester or more off a degree, and demonstrating college-level capability well before senior year.

The Equity Gap Families Should Understand

Here's where the story gets complicated. Researchers at CCRC describe dual enrollment as frequently functioning as a "program of privilege" — disproportionately accessed by students who are already college-bound, well-resourced, and attending schools with existing college partnerships. Students from low-income backgrounds, rural areas, and underrepresented communities are statistically less likely to participate, even when they would benefit significantly.

One major barrier is cost. In many states, students and families pay out of pocket to participate — a simple but consequential hurdle. Another is access: schools without existing relationships with community colleges or universities often can't offer dual enrollment at all, or offer it only for a narrow slice of students in elective or honors tracks.

A January 2026 commentary in EdSource described what counselors in rural Central California called "college access deserts" — places where students already travel long distances just to attend high school, let alone a college campus for dual enrollment courses. Online and virtual dual enrollment options are beginning to address this gap, but not uniformly.

This is exactly why school choice matters here. Not every school — traditional public, charter, or private — has the infrastructure or institutional will to make dual enrollment available to a broad range of students.

What Schools Are Doing It Well?

States like Texas have emerged as models. Texas created the FAST Scholarship, which provides free dual credit for low-income students, and redesigned its performance funding so that colleges receive funding incentives tied to the number of dual-credit students who accumulate 15 or more college credits. That incentive structure has pushed programs to actively recruit students who traditionally wouldn't have pursued dual enrollment.

Bellwether Education Partners' 2026 state legislative analysis found that dual enrollment drew significant fiscal attention this year, with states both broadening access in some places and capping credits or restricting delivery methods in others — a sign that policymakers are actively wrestling with how to scale these programs equitably. Virginia passed legislation establishing a College and Career Ready Fund specifically to expand tuition-free dual enrollment access statewide.

What Families Should Ask When Choosing a School

Whether you're evaluating a traditional public school, a charter school, or a virtual program, these are the right questions to ask about dual enrollment:

  • Is dual enrollment available to all students, or only honors/advanced tracks? Programs available only to a select group signal inequitable design.
  • Is it free or subsidized? If families are expected to pay tuition and fees, that's a real barrier — and a mark against the program.
  • Are courses aligned to a college or career pathway? Researchers at CCRC warn against what they call "random acts of dual enrollment" — courses taken without connection to a student's goals. Pathway-aligned programs produce meaningfully better outcomes.
  • Is advising included? Students who lack guidance about which credits transfer where often end up with credits that don't count toward their intended degree.
  • Do credits transfer broadly? A dual enrollment course whose credits only transfer to one in-state institution is worth far less than credits accepted across a range of colleges.

For families at a school that embeds college readiness into its overall academic model — not as an add-on, but as a core commitment — these questions often have better answers. That's one reason schools that explicitly adopt a PreK-16 educational philosophy are worth a closer look: they've built the structure and the culture around getting students to and through college, not just to graduation. Dove Virtual School, Oklahoma's tuition-free PK-12 online charter school, is one example of this approach — its academic framework explicitly embraces a PreK-16 model with college and career readiness as one of three core pillars alongside STEM education and character development.

Dual Enrollment in Virtual and Online Schools

One frequently overlooked dimension of dual enrollment is how it fits — or doesn't — into virtual and online K-12 programs. Online charter school students have historically faced logistical barriers to dual enrollment: seat-time requirements, campus attendance expectations on the college side, and state policies that weren't designed with virtual students in mind.

That's beginning to change. More community colleges are offering fully online dual enrollment courses, and some states are updating their policies to explicitly ensure that students in virtual charter schools have the same access to dual enrollment as their peers in brick-and-mortar settings. For families considering an online school, it's worth asking specifically whether the school's academic team actively supports dual enrollment participation, transcript coordination, and the college advising that makes dual enrollment meaningful rather than just a line on a schedule.

The Bottom Line for School Choosers in 2026

Dual enrollment isn't a silver bullet, and a poorly designed program — courses unconnected to student goals, no advising, credits that don't transfer — can waste a student's time and a family's money. But a well-structured program, embedded into a school that genuinely prioritizes college readiness, is one of the most powerful academic advantages available to a high schooler today.

When you're comparing schools, don't just ask whether dual enrollment is offered. Ask how it's offered, for whom, at what cost, and with what support. The answers will tell you a great deal about whether a school's college readiness talk is backed by college readiness infrastructure.

The window between high school and college is narrowing — not because it should feel rushed, but because the tools exist to make those years count twice.

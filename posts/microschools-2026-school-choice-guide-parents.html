Microschools in 2026: What Parents Need to Know

# Microschools in 2026: What Parents Need to Know

The one-room schoolhouse never really went away — it just got rebranded.

In 2026, there are an estimated 75,000 microschools serving about 1.5 million students nationwide, according to an analysis published in May by the National Microschooling Center. That number has surged since the pandemic, and the expansion of state-sponsored private school choice programs — such as vouchers, tax-credit scholarships, and education savings accounts — have made microschools more financially viable and an option for more families. Families who discovered the power of small, flexible learning communities during COVID-era learning pods often found they didn't want to give them up once school buildings reopened.

But what exactly is a microschool? How do they compare to charter schools? And how should parents evaluate whether either model is right for their child? Here's what the latest research actually shows.

---

What Is a Microschool?

Microschools across the nation are highly diverse in size, operations, and locations, but typically maintain a small enrollment. The median size of nonpublic microschools, which typically charge tuition, is 20 students, while the number for public microschools is 30 students, according to a survey of 1,000 microschools conducted between October 2025 and January 2026 by the National Microschooling Center.

While microschools are growing in popularity, some say the model is challenged by a lack of federal and state definitions and standards, making it difficult to measure the sector's impact on students as a whole. Only a small number of states — West Virginia, Georgia, and Utah — currently have statutory definitions for microschools, sometimes referred to as learning pods.

Despite the lack of a formal definition, microschools tend to share several common features:

  • Small, multiage classrooms where students progress at their own pace
  • Project-based learning as the dominant instructional approach
  • Self-directed learning and strong social-emotional learning components
  • Flexible settings — commercial spaces, houses of worship, or private homes

Most microschools serve younger students, with 87% teaching students ages 5–11. About 67% of microschools serve students ages 12–14, and 38% serve students ages 15–18. Where instruction takes place varies, too: 38% of microschools operate in commercial business facilities, while 22% operate out of houses of worship and 17% operate in private homes. That age distribution matters for high school families in particular — the microschool pipeline thins significantly past middle school, which is one reason small charter high schools have stepped in to fill a similar niche.

---

Why Are Families Choosing Microschools?

"A lot of parents dissatisfied with online schooling organized learning pods," said Jonathan Schweig, a senior behavioral and social scientist at RAND Corp. who has studied microschools. "There were a large-ish number of parents who, once schools reopened, thought that something pod-like in a learning experience was actually better suited for their child's needs."

The appeal is relational as much as academic. According to a 2025 survey by the National Microschooling Center, microschool founders said student growth in nonacademic learning was their most important desired student outcome — followed by children's happiness in their current setting and strength in skills needed for future success. That's a striking departure from the test-score-first framing that dominated K-12 conversations for the past two decades.

The Wichita public school district in Kansas decided to try something new in 2024, as it faced growing competition for students from private schools, nearby public school districts, and the pull of homeschool. The district reached out to families that indicated they planned to leave and asked if they would be interested in trying its newest school — called Creative Minds — a microschool where students from multiple grade levels work with a single teacher focused on project-based learning. Today, Creative Minds has grown to four classrooms on two campuses serving about 40 students combined in kindergarten through 6th grade. The waitlist is around 200 students, and that comes purely from word of mouth — the district hasn't advertised the existence of either school. When a waitlist grows to 200 without a single marketing dollar spent, it signals that parents are responding to something real.

---

How Are Microschools Funded — and What Does It Cost?

Most of the National Microschooling Center survey respondents (63%) said they rely on tuition-based funding, where most of a school's families pay the majority of their child's tuition. Half of the polled schools said they receive funding from state-provided school choice programs, such as education savings accounts and taxpayer-funded scholarships.

Cost is a legitimate concern for working families. According to a 2025 report published by RAND Corp., the median monthly fee to attend microschools was $650 — or about $6,500 for a typical 10-month school year — based on 2023 data. That price point puts many private microschools out of reach without scholarship support.

This is where publicly funded alternatives — including charter schools — become critical to the conversation. The Indiana Microschool Collaborative, for instance, supports the development of tuition-free public charter microschools across the state, focused on small student groupings, multi-age learning, personalized pacing for instruction, and strong relationships. No tuition required.

---

What Microschools and Small Charter Schools Have in Common

What does a microschool actually offer that a small charter school doesn't? For many families, the honest answer is: not much that matters most.

The core ingredients families love about microschools — tight-knit community, personalized attention, project-based learning, teachers who know every student by name — are the same qualities that have defined high-performing small charter schools for years. The structural difference is often regulatory, not educational.

Consider Moreno Valley Prep, a charter high school serving grades 9–12 in Angel Fire, New Mexico. The school has been ranked year after year on major national surveys including Newsweek, U.S. News and World Report, and the Washington Post Challenge Index. A graduating senior's reflection on the school captured something that any microschool parent would recognize instantly: it isn't test scores or graduation rates that define the school — it's the strength of its community. That's not an accident. It's exactly what small, relationship-centered schools produce, whether they carry the microschool label or the charter label.

The institutional difference does matter for accountability and staying power, however. 67% of microschools are not currently accredited by an official, government-recognized organization, but most microschools (87%) would be interested in obtaining accreditation. Charter schools, by contrast, must meet state authorization and renewal standards to keep operating — a layer of accountability that gives families greater assurance about long-term stability and program continuity.

---

The Accountability Question: What Parents Should Ask

Microschools operate with minimal standardized oversight, which is both their strength (flexibility, innovation) and their risk (variability, fragility). The Center for American Progress has raised accountability concerns about microschools, recommending that policymakers adopt a federal definition, implement state accountability measures for teacher licensure and financial reporting, establish data collection and reporting standards, and protect students' civil rights — especially for microschools accepting public funds.

Before enrolling in either a microschool or a small charter school, parents should ask:

  1. Is the school accredited or authorized? Charter schools must hold active state authorizations; most microschools do not carry formal accreditation.
  2. How is student progress measured? Look beyond observation logs and portfolios — ask for outcome data across multiple years.
  3. What happens if the lead teacher leaves? In Wichita, district leaders found that a microschool's success hinges on the teacher in a way it wouldn't in a traditional school — not only must the teacher be talented and creative, they must be able to forge strong relationships with families. "That takes time to build those skill sets. That's not something any of us learn in pre-service," said Dyane Smokorowski, the district's coordinator of digital literacy.
  4. Is there a waitlist — and why? Organic waitlist demand, especially without advertising, signals genuine community trust.
  5. What is the funding model, and is it sustainable? Tuition-dependent microschools face real financial risk as enrollment fluctuates year to year.

---

The Bottom Line

The microschool surge isn't a fad. It reflects a genuine and lasting shift in what families want from education: smallness, belonging, personalization, and relationships over anonymity and standardized instruction. "I think it's good to look at these different models because obviously the community is looking for something different," said Robert Dickson, Wichita's chief information officer. "Not everyone, but I think there's a lot of different models that work for different families."

Families weighing school choice in 2026–27 don't need to limit their search to anything with "microschool" in the name. Some of the most compelling small-school experiences in the country are already happening inside accredited, publicly funded charter schools — with waitlists to prove it.

The question worth asking isn't what category does this school belong to? It's does this school know my child, and is it built to help them grow?

Mastery Over Minutes: The Rise of Competency-Based Learning

# Mastery Over Minutes: The Rise of Competency-Based Learning in K-12

Every child who has ever sat through a lesson they already mastered — or been moved on before they were ready — has lived the central flaw of traditional schooling: progress tied to the calendar rather than to the student. A fixed pace that serves averages instead of individuals.

That model is facing a serious reckoning in 2026. Across the country, educators, policymakers, and school leaders are pushing toward a fundamentally different idea — students advance when they can demonstrate mastery, not because another semester has ended.

What Is Competency-Based Learning, Exactly?

Competency-based education (CBE) — also called mastery-based or proficiency-based learning — is an instructional model in which students make choices about how they learn and demonstrate their knowledge, progress at a pace that might differ from their classmates', receive individualized support based on their needs, and advance based on their mastery of course material instead of seat time.

The distinction matters more than it might first appear. In a traditional classroom, a student who has mastered a concept and a student who hasn't move to the next unit at exactly the same time. In a competency-based classroom, the second student gets more time and targeted support — and only moves forward after demonstrating real understanding.

As one education leader put it directly at a 2026 state convening: "We're doing everything based on averages. We have age-based grade levels. We have standard pacing guides. We have uniform semester timelines. We have credits earned by time, not by mastery. … There are no students who are average. Students do not learn at the same pace."

Why 2026 Is a Turning Point

The momentum behind CBE is significant right now. Every state in the country now allows districts the flexibility to adopt competency-based learning — a milestone reached only in the last few years after more than a decade of advocacy. But policy permission and classroom reality remain two different things, and the challenge in 2026 is converting that legal flexibility into widespread practice.

Experts tracking K-12 trends this year expect a clear shift toward competency-based approaches, where students advance by demonstrating mastery rather than simply accruing hours in class — expressed through project-based assessments, performance tasks, portfolios, or interdisciplinary units.

Policymakers in several states are actively building the infrastructure for this shift. In North Carolina, state leaders convened in early 2026 to explore what schools would look like if students moved through school as they were ready — rather than based on age and the school calendar. National leaders at the convening pointed to New Hampshire and Vermont as leading models already putting CBE into wider practice.

What Competency-Based Learning Looks Like in Practice

The shape of CBE varies by school, but the core principles are consistent. Personalized, competency-based education empowers students to progress based on mastery through transparent learning outcomes and real-world relevance, shifting the focus from seat time to student agency so that every learner receives support tailored to their needs.

A few elements show up in schools doing it well:

  • Clear, measurable learning targets — students always know what they need to demonstrate and what evidence counts
  • Flexible pacing — some students move quickly through a concept; others get additional time and varied instruction without stigma
  • Multiple pathways to demonstrate mastery — written work, presentations, projects, and performance tasks all count
  • Transparent feedback loops — students and families can see exactly where a learner stands and what comes next
  • Equity as a design principle — CBE done right means every student reaches a high standard, just on a pathway designed for them

That last point addresses one of the most common misconceptions about CBE. Advocates are emphatic that the goal is not different expectations for different learners. As one national expert put it at a 2026 state education summit: "We're not talking about different expectations or standards for different learners. We're saying everyone's going to get to this level of competency, and we're going to personalize the pathway to get there."

Charter Schools as Natural Testing Grounds

Charter schools have been among the most active incubators for competency-based learning, in part because their structural flexibility makes it easier to depart from the rigid grade-level and seat-time mandates that govern many traditional district schools.

In North Carolina, a network of charter schools called Second Mile Education Schools has implemented a competency-based approach, drawing attention as a model for how schools can operationalize mastery-based progression in a real-world setting. Similar experiments are underway in rural districts through state-sponsored pilots targeting math outcomes in middle schools.

BRICK Buffalo, a charter school that launched in Buffalo, NY in 2023, demonstrates how the underlying philosophy of meeting each student where they are gets translated into actual school design. The school explicitly describes its model as one that customizes the curriculum to tap into students' individual interests and ensures they have the necessary support beyond the classroom to reach their full potential — a student-centered orientation that is the practical foundation any successful competency-based model must be built on. Its curriculum spanning STEM, humanities, athletics, and creative expression reflects the same whole-child thinking that makes mastery-based learning sustainable rather than just a grading-policy tweak.

What the Research Actually Says

The evidence base for CBE is promising but still maturing. Researchers consistently point to strong results in student engagement and motivation. The equity case is especially compelling: mastery-based learning acknowledges that each learner comes with their own experiences, strengths, and needs, and gives each person the time and space they need to gain a deep understanding of even the most challenging concepts — regardless of prior academic experience or background.

The long-term workforce case is also gaining traction. Self-regulation, perseverance, and problem-solving are increasingly recognized as keys to positive outcomes in adult life and careers — and mastery-based programs are specifically structured to build those capacities through clear learning objectives and sustained practice, not passive seat time.

Findings on standardized-test outcomes are more mixed, which critics cite as a limitation. But that critique partly reflects a mismatch in measurement: standardized tests were designed to compare age-cohort averages, not to capture individual mastery trajectories. The metrics and the model weren't built for each other.

The Real Barriers — And How Schools Are Clearing Them

If CBE is this clearly beneficial, why isn't every school doing it? The barriers are genuine:

  • Legacy grading systems built around letter grades and Carnegie Units don't map onto competency records without deliberate redesign
  • Teacher preparation gaps — most educators were trained in and for the traditional model and need meaningful professional development to shift practice
  • Family expectations — parents accustomed to grade levels and report cards can be confused or resistant without clear communication
  • Scheduling rigidity — moving at different paces is hard when every period is the same length and the bell rings regardless of where students are
  • Accountability misalignment — even where CBE is permitted, state accountability systems still often measure age-cohort benchmarks

For school leaders, the path through these barriers runs through investing in teacher time, training, and tools to design authentic assessments, and building schedules that create space for deeper work rather than just content coverage.

So Should Your School Try It?

What is competency-based learning and is it right for K-12 schools?

Competency-based learning is an approach in which students advance by demonstrating mastery of specific skills and knowledge — not by accumulating hours in a seat. The evidence suggests it can boost engagement, reduce persistent learning gaps, and better prepare students for what comes after graduation, especially when implemented with clear standards, flexible pacing, and strong support for teachers. For schools with the structural flexibility to experiment — particularly charter schools — 2026 is a strong moment to pilot CBE in at least one subject area, gather real data, and refine before scaling.

The shift won't happen overnight. Redesigning how schools measure and recognize learning is slow, sometimes contentious work. But the schools that commit to it now won't just be ahead of a trend. They'll be building something more durable: a system that actually matches how real children learn, one student at a time.

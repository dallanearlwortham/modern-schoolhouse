Why Safety & Belonging Now Drive School Choice

# Why Safety and Belonging Have Become the New #1 Drivers of School Choice

For most of the past decade, the story of school choice was a story about academics. Parents switched schools—or chose charters and private schools—because they wanted more rigorous coursework, stronger test scores, or specialized programs their district didn't offer. That narrative has shifted in 2026, and the new data is worth paying close attention to.

According to EdChoice's 2026 Schooling in America survey—one of the largest annual polls of American school parents, drawing on a nationally representative sample of more than 2,400 parents conducted in spring 2026—both charter school and private school parents now rank a safe environment as their number one reason for choosing a school, a position that academic quality held until very recently. Safety has gradually climbed the rankings and has finally taken the top spot.

That shift isn't happening in isolation.

Bullying and Anxiety Are Pushing Families Out the Door

It's one thing to understand what draws families toward a school. It's equally revealing to look at what drives them away from one. In May 2026 EdChoice polling, among school parents who reported switching their child's school, bullying (33%) and excessive stress or anxiety (27%) were the two most prominent difficulties children had faced at their former school—prompting families to make the move. Those two factors now rank above "academic needs not being met" as reasons for switching, a reversal from just a year or two ago.

This tells a story that goes beyond test scores. Families aren't just hunting for higher achievement. They're hunting for an environment where their child can feel safe and known—where the culture of the school itself is part of the value proposition.

What the Research Actually Says About Belonging

The word "belonging" can sound soft and hard to quantify, but the research base behind it is substantial and growing. A 2026 literature review from Monash University's Faculty of Education, conducted in collaboration with the International Baccalaureate, found that school belonging is fundamental to educational success and equity—consistently associated with better mental health, wellbeing, and academic learning outcomes.

The longitudinal evidence is especially striking. Research involving more than 1,500 young adults found that higher levels of school belonging at ages 15–16 were associated with lower levels of depression, anxiety, and stress years later in young adulthood. The culture a teenager experiences in high school doesn't just affect their senior year. It shapes their mental health long after graduation.

What does a sense of belonging actually require? Research points to several consistent conditions: smaller, more connected communities where students are known by name; adults who are present and responsive rather than stretched thin across hundreds of students; a clear school mission that gives everyone—students, families, and teachers—a shared sense of purpose; and a low tolerance for the bullying and social cruelty that can make large, anonymous schools feel unsafe for the kids who most need support.

Why Charter Schools Have a Structural Advantage Here

Charter school parents and private school parents share a great deal in common when it comes to school selection: both groups now rank safe environment first, followed closely by academic quality. That pairing is not a coincidence—it reflects something real about what these schools tend to offer structurally.

Charter schools are mission-driven by design. Because they're built around a specific educational model and families must actively choose to enroll, they tend to attract students, families, and educators who are self-selected for alignment with that mission. That shared buy-in produces something genuinely difficult to manufacture in a large, default-assignment district school: intentional community. A family doesn't land at a charter school by accident. They chose it—and that act of choosing creates a different level of investment from day one.

Smaller enrollment, clearer expectations, and a community of families who opted in together create exactly the conditions the belonging research says matter most. That doesn't make it automatic—plenty of charters get this wrong—but the structural ingredients exist in a way they often don't in comprehensive neighborhood schools where students are assigned by zip code and community cohesion is left to chance.

A Concrete Example of Mission and Safety Working Together

Academic ambition and a strong school culture don't have to trade off against each other—and the best charter schools prove it. ITINERIS, a 9th–12th grade public charter school in West Jordan, Utah, combines a rigorous academic offering—students can earn up to two years of free college credit while completing their high school diploma—with the kind of intentional, mission-aligned environment that charter design makes possible. Families who enroll there aren't choosing between a safe community and serious academics. The model is built so those two things reinforce each other.

That combination—purposeful culture plus meaningful academic challenge—is increasingly what families say they're looking for, and it's a design approach that more charter leaders would do well to articulate clearly in their enrollment conversations.

What This Means for Families Evaluating Schools Right Now

In 2026, roughly 75% of U.S. parents of school-aged children engaged with school choice for the school year—approximately 46 million families actively considering, searching for, or enrolling in a non-default option. That is an enormous share of American families in active decision-making mode. And the data suggests the most important question they're asking has quietly changed.

It used to be: Is this school academically strong enough?

Today, the question is just as likely to be: Will my child be safe here? Will they feel like they belong?

These are not soft questions. They're the ones the evidence says matter most for long-term student success—and they deserve to be front and center in every school visit, every enrollment conversation, and every policy debate about what genuinely makes a school good.

When you're evaluating a school for your child, consider asking these questions directly:

  • What is the school's explicit plan for preventing bullying? Not a policy on paper—an actual practice.
  • How do teachers know students individually? What's the advisor-to-student ratio, and how often do they meet?
  • What does the school's culture ask of families? Schools where families are expected to engage tend to produce the community cohesion that belonging requires.
  • What does the school's mission mean day-to-day? A mission statement posted in a lobby is different from one that visibly shapes how adults and students treat each other.

---

Academic rigor still matters enormously—no family should have to choose between a safe, connected environment and a challenging one. But in 2026, the families paying the closest attention have figured out something the research has been saying for years: a school where students don't feel safe or connected isn't actually a high-achieving school for their child. Culture isn't the backdrop to the academic program. For a growing number of families right now, it is the program.

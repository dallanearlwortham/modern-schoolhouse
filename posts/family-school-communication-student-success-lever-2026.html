Family-School Communication: The Student Success Lever

When Schools Communicate Better, Students Do Better

Most school leaders believe family engagement matters. What's easy to underestimate is how much the evidence actually shows — and how wide the gap remains between what schools intend to communicate and what families genuinely receive and act on.

For years, schools measured communication success by one simple question: "Can we reach families?" Contactability was the old standard. In 2026, that bar is no longer enough. The question has shifted: Are families engaging? Are they reading messages, responding, showing up, and participating in school life? With enrollment competition intensifying and families holding more school options than ever, family-school communication has moved from a "nice to have" to a core strategic priority — one with a surprisingly strong research foundation behind it.

What the Research Actually Shows

The evidence base here is unusually deep and unusually consistent. For more than fifty years, researchers have measured what happens to students when families are engaged and when schools communicate well with home, and the finding is remarkably consistent: it moves achievement, attendance, behavior, and whether a student stays enrolled at all.

The most-cited synthesis in the field, A New Wave of Evidence, reviewed 51 separate studies and reached one clear conclusion: there is a positive and consistent relationship between family engagement and student achievement, and it holds across every income level, race and ethnicity, and grade level. The effect sizes are not just statistically present — they're meaningful. William Jeynes's meta-analyses found parental involvement associated with achievement by roughly 0.7 of a standard deviation at the primary-school level, holding for both minority and non-minority children, and for boys and girls alike.

Studies have shown that effective family engagement can double the likelihood of students achieving academic success compared to peers whose families are less engaged. And the gains extend well beyond test scores: students who had regular family engagement earned higher grades, enrolled in advanced courses at higher rates, attended school more regularly, had higher graduation rates, had better social skills, and showed improved behavior. Research suggests that children from socioeconomically disadvantaged and racial and ethnic minority backgrounds tend to benefit the most from having parents involved in their schooling — a finding with direct implications for charter schools, which disproportionately serve students from under-resourced communities.

The Communication Gap Schools Are Still Missing

Knowing engagement matters and actually closing the communication gap are two very different problems. Reaching families at scale is no longer the primary hurdle — most schools can send a message. What has changed is how success is defined. Communication is now evaluated by clarity, consistency, and connection. Leaders want to know that families are receiving messages, understanding them, and engaging in genuine dialogue.

The multilingual dimension of this gap is especially acute. According to data from school communication researchers, 64% of multilingual families find school communications unclear or not in their preferred language, and only 27% of multilingual respondents find it very easy to connect with teachers, compared to 40% of English-speaking families. Federal civil rights law requires schools to communicate with families who have limited English proficiency in a language they can understand — about any program, service, or activity communicated to English-speaking families. Yet many schools still treat multilingual communication as a series of individual translation requests rather than a coordinated, system-wide practice.

There is also a structural problem rooted in tool overload. Family engagement surveys consistently show that parents are more likely to disengage when communication feels overwhelming or repetitive. Over the past decade, schools have adopted more communication tools than ever — emails, texts, robocalls, apps, and websites all competing for attention. Streamlining doesn't mean communicating less; it means communicating more deliberately. Districts that have consolidated platforms and created clear communication structures report stronger family participation and less staff fatigue.

Families who feel informed and connected report higher levels of trust in their schools and greater confidence in school leadership — an especially critical factor during times of change, budget uncertainty, or enrollment pressure.

Charter Schools Have a Structural Advantage Here

Charter schools have an opportunity that traditional district schools often struggle to replicate. Because charters are smaller, mission-aligned, and built around the idea that families actively chose them, they are positioned to make family engagement a genuine feature of school culture rather than a compliance checkbox. Charter schools that lean into this advantage — creating clear communication channels, welcoming families into classrooms and events, and inviting parents into meaningful roles as decision-makers — build communities where families feel like collaborators, not just recipients of newsletters.

This is exactly the kind of best-practice work that statewide advocacy organizations help charter schools sustain at scale. The Public Charter School Alliance of South Carolina — the state's leading voice for public charter schools, supporting 113 schools and more than 70,000 students across South Carolina — connects charter leaders with resources, training, and community-building tools that help schools deepen relationships with the families they serve and make the case for high-quality public education in every corner of the state.

Five Communication Moves with Evidence Behind Them

Based on current research and practitioner guidance, these are the approaches with the strongest track records:

  1. Shift from broadcasting to dialogue. One-way announcement systems create passive audiences. Two-way systems — where families can respond, ask questions, and flag concerns — build trust. Even brief teacher-initiated check-ins (a quick text, a two-minute call) outperform end-of-semester conferences for sustaining real connection throughout the year.
  1. Build a predictable communication calendar. Families are more likely to engage when they know when to expect outreach. A consistent weekly or monthly rhythm reduces family anxiety and increases the likelihood that messages are read and acted on.
  1. Eliminate the language gap systematically. Multilingual family communication means ensuring families receive school messages in their home language through every channel, every day — not just enrollment forms, but progress updates, emergency alerts, event invitations, and classroom communications alike.
  1. Consolidate your tools. Fewer channels used consistently outperform many channels used sporadically every time. Streamlining creates breathing room for staff, reduces family confusion, and makes it easier to measure what's actually working.
  1. Measure engagement, not just delivery. Open rates, participation trends, and response patterns offer meaningful insight into how families are actually responding. Track those metrics by family group — and treat gaps, especially among the hardest-to-reach families, as action signals rather than acceptable losses.

The Bottom Line

What is the single most cost-effective intervention a K-12 school can make to improve student outcomes? The research points consistently toward one answer: bring families in as genuine partners. The investment is primarily one of culture, consistency, and intentional design — and the returns show up in attendance records, academic performance, and long-term enrollment.

In an era when every school is competing for students and public trust, family communication isn't a soft priority tucked into the back of a strategic plan. It's infrastructure — and schools that build it well are building one of the most durable academic advantages available.

Early College High Schools: Get a Degree Before You Graduate

# Early College High Schools: How Charter Schools Are Helping Students Graduate Ahead

Graduating high school with a diploma in one hand and 30 college credits — or even an associate degree — in the other used to sound like an outlier story. In 2026, it's becoming a mainstream option, and charter schools are leading the charge.

Dual enrollment just hit a record: more than 3.1 million high school students took a college course in the 2024–25 academic year, according to federal data analyzed by the Community College Research Center at Teachers College, Columbia University. That's a 24% increase from 2.5 million students just a few years prior, with growth in all 50 states and Washington, D.C. At the center of this surge is a specific school model that has been quietly delivering results for years: the Early College High School.

What Is an Early College High School?

An Early College High School (ECHS) is a school — often a public charter — where high school graduation requirements and college-level coursework are woven together into the same program. Students typically graduate with both a high school diploma and either 30–60 transferable college credits or a full Associate of Arts or Associate of Science degree.

The model is designed to be accessible, not exclusive. Unlike honors tracks or selective magnet programs, Early College High Schools are built to serve students who are first-generation college-goers, come from lower-income families, or simply need a more structured on-ramp to postsecondary education. The goal is straightforward: close the academic gap between high school and college, especially for students who might otherwise fall through the cracks.

Does the Early College Model Actually Work?

Yes — and the evidence is unusually strong for an education reform. This is one of the few K–12 models backed by rigorous, lottery-based research.

A follow-up study by the American Institutes for Research (AIR) found that Early College High School students were significantly more likely to earn a postsecondary degree within six years of graduating high school, compared to non-ECHS students with similar background characteristics. Notably, ECHS students were nearly three times as likely to earn an Associate's degree or certificate, and significantly more likely to earn a Bachelor's degree, than their non-ECHS peers.

The financial case is just as compelling. Credits earned for free in high school don't have to be paid for at $400–$600 per credit hour later. For families watching college costs climb, that math matters enormously.

Key outcomes the research supports:

  • Higher high school graduation rates compared to peers with similar backgrounds
  • Faster college degree completion — entering with credits means finishing sooner and spending less
  • Better college persistence — dual enrollment students are more likely to stay enrolled and finish their degrees
  • Career acceleration — entering the workforce earlier with credentials translates to greater lifetime earnings potential

Why Charter Schools Are Especially Well-Positioned for This Model

Traditional district high schools can offer dual enrollment as an add-on, but the Early College model works best when it is baked into a school's entire identity — the daily schedule, the academic culture, the advising structure, and the formal partnership with a college or university. That is exactly the kind of intentional, purpose-built design that charter schools can establish from day one.

Charter schools can set their own academic calendars, hire faculty with both K–12 and collegiate experience, and develop dedicated college-prep advisory programs tailored to their specific student population. Critically, they can target communities — including smaller towns and rural areas — where families might otherwise have little access to any college-preparatory pathway at all.

Moreno Valley Prep, a tuition-free charter middle and early college high school serving grades 7–12 in Angel Fire, New Mexico, is a concrete illustration of this approach. The school is built around a future-ready curriculum, small class sizes, and experiential learning — a combination explicitly designed to equip Northern New Mexico students not just for a diploma, but for college, leadership, and life after graduation.

What Separates High-Performing Early College Schools from the Rest

Not every school that uses the "Early College" label delivers consistent outcomes. The models with the strongest track records share a handful of non-negotiable characteristics:

  1. Genuine college partnerships — articulation agreements that guarantee credits actually transfer to in-state universities and community colleges, not just look good on paper
  2. Academic support built into the schedule — tutoring, mentoring, and bridge coursework so students aren't left to navigate college-level material without help
  3. Small class sizes and high-touch advising — students need adult advocates who know them well and can intervene early
  4. A college-going culture that starts in middle school — the conversation about postsecondary options shouldn't begin in junior year
  5. Open, equitable enrollment — the strongest programs are open to all students, ensuring the model serves those who need it most rather than skimming the top

Who Should Consider an Early College High School?

The Early College model tends to be a particularly strong fit for:

  • First-generation college students who benefit from a structured, guided introduction to college-level expectations and campus culture
  • Cost-conscious families looking to meaningfully reduce the price of a four-year degree before it even begins
  • High-achieving students who are under-challenged by a traditional high school pace and want real academic rigor
  • Students in rural or smaller communities where the nearest university campus may be an hour away — but college credits can still come to them
  • Motivated seventh and eighth graders whose families want to start building a postsecondary foundation earlier than the standard high school timeline allows

The Bottom Line

The question is no longer whether early college programs work — the research answers that clearly. The real question is whether families know these options exist and how to find them in their area.

Access to dual enrollment remains uneven across the country. Charter schools with an explicit Early College mission help level that playing field, particularly in communities where the traditional public school path offers fewer advanced options. For families actively evaluating schools in 2026, an early college charter may deliver the highest return of any available choice — one that starts paying off before a student ever moves into a college dorm.
